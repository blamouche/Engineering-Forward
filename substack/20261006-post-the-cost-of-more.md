# The cost of more

*Adding rules, context, and capital makes AI worse, not better — and the week's evidence is unusually clear.*

Katie Parrott spent months improving her AI writing setup. She expanded her style guide from 759 words to 3,855. She linked 385 files across folders using a Zettelkasten system. She logged every experiment so that repeated mistakes became checklist items. Each improvement felt rational in isolation. Together, they produced 127 drafts and over a million words for a single essay — all flat, all committee-like, all dead on the page. The German word for this is Verschlimmbesserung: making something better only to make it worse.

She tore the whole system down and rebuilt from scratch. The new guides run 513 words instead of 6,109. The rules are principles, not templates. The model asks before adding anything to the guides. Every folder stands alone. The result is better work from less instruction. Parrott's account, published in Every, is the most detailed first-person case study of context engineering gone wrong published to date. It is also the clearest illustration of a principle that runs through the week's other stories: in AI, more is often the problem.

## The architecture of less

ByteByteGo's explanation of the "lost in the middle" effect describes the same problem from the inside of the model. Language models show higher accuracy for information at the beginning and end of prompts, and lower accuracy for the middle. The cause is structural: the transformer attention mechanism, combined with causal masking, gives early tokens more routes through which to influence later computation, and gives end-of-prompt tokens the advantage of proximity to the answer. The middle gets neither. The result is a U-shaped accuracy curve that larger context windows cannot fix.

The distinction ByteByteGo draws between maximum context size and effective context size is the technical version of Parrott's discovery. Providers market context windows as capacity — 128K, 1M, 2M tokens — but capacity is not reliability. The RULER benchmark evaluated 17 models and found widespread degradation as input length increased. An agent that loads 50 files into context before acting may be less reliable than one that loads 5 well-chosen files, even if the 50-file approach fits within the window. More information does not produce proportionally better results. Parrott's 6,109 words of context produced worse drafts than her 513. The model's attention mechanism is the reason.

The mechanism of failure in Parrott's case is instructive on its own. The model could not distinguish a record from a rule. When she told the model that an outline was "UNRECOGNIZABLE," that correction went into the log. When she said something similar twice, it became a checklist item that every subsequent draft had to pass. The system she built to help her write turned her reactions to one draft into immutable law for all future drafts. GPT-5.6 Sol, reviewing the workflow, diagnosed it precisely: "concrete rules are easier to verify, so they can overwhelm the subtler ones while every checklist still passes." The measurable crowded out the meaningful. [Own the judge](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/own-the-judge) described the measurement crisis in AI — benchmarks gamed, scaffolding dead weight. Parrott's story is the personal version: the rules she built to measure quality became the rules that destroyed it.

## The economics of less

The principle extends from context to capital. CO/AI's Signal/Noise newsletter argues that AI demand is real but AI financing is a bubble. The evidence is a single number: the cost of a given AI task fell 79x in one year. Every curve points up and to the right — adoption, spending, capability. But the same number that proves the technology is real also suggests that the $518 billion committed to frontier compute is pointed at demand that is actively walking away. When agents are given a budget, they route work off the premium model to whatever is good enough at a tenth of the price. SaaStr's agent, handed a $240,000 bill for API access, proposed a $5 Postgres instance instead.

The SaaStr anecdote is not a hypothetical. It is a real agent making a real routing decision that, multiplied across millions of agents, represents the systemic risk to frontier compute economics. The agent is doing what it is designed to do: find the cheapest path to the job. The cheapest path leads away from the frontier where the capital was committed. Broadcom handed Anthropic a $42 billion convertible note to buy chips — the supplier is now the bank, taking the hardware if a payment slips. This is the same pattern that destroyed telecom cap tables in 2000: the technology was real, adoption was real, but the money was committed faster than cash flows could service it.

John Lindquist's Jev demonstrations, published in Lenny's Newsletter, put a number on the cheap path. Jev is a decision engine — it outputs scores, classifications, probabilities, and function calls instead of text. Lindquist spent 73 cents across 23 development runs. Claire processed 5 gigabytes of JSON for 40 cents. The chess benchmark: Jev analyzed a full game in under a second, 10x faster and 4x cheaper than a low-reasoning LLM, with no accuracy loss. The right mental model for Jev is an if/else statement, not a chatbot. When decisions cost nearly nothing and return nearly instantly, brute-force approaches become rational. Lindquist calls it "efficient inefficiency" — running every possible path because each one is cheap enough to afford. The constraint is the feature: by restricting output to decisions, Jev unlocks a class of applications that were impractical with general-purpose models.

## The market consolidates

Big Technology's analysis of Anthropic's pre-IPO numbers, using data from Similarweb and Apptopia, reveals a market that is consolidating rather than expanding. Claude's daily active user market share rose from 1.4 percent in January to 14.9 percent in September, while ChatGPT's fell from 41.8 percent to 30.7 percent. Claude's website hit 950 million visits in August, up 540 percent year-over-year. Enterprise SSO logins jumped 4x since March. Claude users spend the most time per session — 27 minutes, ahead of Grok's 23.4 and ChatGPT's 23.1.

The most strategically important number is the one that explains the dynamic: over 80 percent of users actively use just one chatbot. Another 10 percent use two. The market exhibits winner-take-most dynamics. Most users commit to one bot and rarely switch. ChatGPT users who downloaded Claude subsequently spent 13.5 percent less time in ChatGPT. Claude is not adding new users to the market — it is cannibalizing ChatGPT's existing base. The market is not growing; it is concentrating. [The seven percent](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-seven-percent) described the scaling gap between piloting and production. The market data adds a corollary: the consumer market is also consolidating, not expanding. The companies that capture users now will keep them, because the switching cost is not the model — it is the accumulated context, the Dot that knows your schedule, the agent that has been running overnight.

Nubank's withdrawal from its £10 billion Monzo acquisition is the financial version of the same principle. Nubank's shares fell 10 percent on the day the M&A talks leaked. Two days later, the company walked away. The market rendered its verdict on the price before Nubank's management did. The pivot to Nu Global — a stablecoin account operating in 35 countries without a banking licence — is a bet that the future of cross-border banking bypasses traditional licensing rather than negotiating through it. The acquisition was more — more capital, more regulatory overhead, more complexity. The alternative is less: a stablecoin account that operates without a banking licence. The market preferred less.

## The tension at the center

Tibo Sottiaux, who leads ChatGPT and Codex at OpenAI, told Lenny Rachitsky at DevDay that loops, graphs, and fine-tuning agent workflows are a passing phase. The technology is evolving beyond these intermediate patterns. His own Dot warned him about a production outage five minutes before a launch — not because someone built a monitoring graph, but because the agent was watching and noticed. The prediction challenges the current engineering orthodoxy, and Parrott's Verschlimmbesserung story is the counterargument from the other direction. Removing scaffolding does not automatically improve things. Parrott did not just delete her rules — she rebuilt from scratch with different principles. The agent asks first. Every folder stands alone. Guides stay short. The rebuild took deliberate work, and it produced a system that is structurally different from the one it replaced, not just a smaller version of it.

OpenAI's Wallet, revealed in Linas's Newsletter, and Anthropic's Claude Commerce Agents represent divergent strategies for agentic commerce that mirror the consolidation pattern. OpenAI is building the infrastructure for agents to spend money — holding the consumer's balance. Anthropic is building the infrastructure for merchants to accept agent payments — capturing the merchant processing fee. These are two sides of the same transaction, but they represent different bets on where value accrues. OpenAI bets on the consumer side, where the 80-percent-single-bot dynamic gives the wallet provider a captive audience. Anthropic bets on the merchant side, where the Frontier Academy strategy — embedding 10,000 trained engineers inside McKinsey and Morgan Stanley — buys the switching cost that survives a capital reckoning. [The while-loop at work](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-while-loop-at-work) described the loop as the basic unit of software. The wallet and the commerce agent are the financial infrastructure for loops that transact.

## Looking ahead

Parrott's personal rule — no major context decisions under pressure, including the "thrilling new-model-tilt-a-whirl" kind that triggered her original mess — is the meta-principle the market needs. New frontier models create the illusion that entire workflows should be rebuilt. Anthropic's IPO, penciled for November, will be the first public test of whether the frontier's capital structure can service its own commitments. Sequoia's private briefing sizes the opportunity against a $10-20 trillion services market. The bets are enormous. The evidence from context engineering, from attention mechanisms, from agent routing, and from market consolidation all points the same direction: the value is in what you subtract, not what you add. Fewer rules. Less context. Cheaper models. Constrained output. The Verschlimmbesserung is not coming. It is here, and the companies that recognize it will be the ones that scale.
