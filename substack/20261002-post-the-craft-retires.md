# The craft retires

*When the creator of Ruby on Rails says he's done writing code by hand, the question is no longer whether AI changes engineering — it's what engineering becomes when the person who defined the craft walks away from it.*

David Heinemeier Hansson stood on stage at Rails World and said the sentence that would ricochet through the developer community for a week: "At 37signals, a couple of weeks ago, we made the decision that it clearly means we're done writing code by hand." He called November 24, 2025 — the day Opus 4.5 shipped — the "Kodak Brownie moment" for software. He compared AI's effect on programming to the camera's effect on painting. And then he said the part that landed hardest: "I have retired from being a professional programmer."

This is not a CEO hyping a product. DHH created Ruby on Rails, a framework that shaped a generation of web development, and built a 27-year-old profitable company around the principle that code quality is a craft to be cherished. When he says the craft has changed, the craft has changed. The Pragmatic Engineer's coverage captures the secondary tremors: 37signals is moving backend services to Rust because agents write good enough Rust, keeping Ruby on Rails for web apps because convention-over-configuration suits agents, and embracing native mobile because agents can port code between platforms fast enough to make cross-platform abstractions unnecessary. The company that built its identity on the beauty of hand-written Ruby is now organizing its architecture around what agents do well.

The anonymous Big Tech engineer rant that went viral the same week is the counterpoint from inside the machine. "The state of engineering is horrible," the engineer wrote. "Nobody is reading anything. Humans in corporate are doing nothing on their own. It is so soul-sucking." Uber Eats shipped a feature with three obvious bugs. Spotify had unexplainable outages. The Pragmatic Engineer's predictions from January 2026 are tracking: sloppier code, junior engineers pushed to senior responsibilities, an explosion in code volume. The two stories — DHH's graceful retirement and the anonymous engineer's despair — describe the same transition from opposite ends. One has the authority and platform position to frame the change as progress. The other is living through it as degradation.

## The professional identity shifts

Three top executive product recruiters, surveyed by Lenny Rachitsky, describe a hiring market that has reorganized itself around a new archetype. The winning profile shifted from "former founder willing to work 996 hours" to what recruiters call the "executive builder" — senior enough to lead the function, still close enough to build personally. Technical depth is the biggest theme of the year: product searches now want engineering fluency, and engineering searches want product sense. The take-home assignment is dead, replaced by live working sessions where candidates solve real problems with the team watching which tools they reach for.

The detail that cuts deepest is Andrew Abramson's prediction that AI fluency will stop differentiating candidates within a year. When a skill becomes universal, it stops being a competitive advantage. What stays scarce, Abramson says, is taste, judgment, curiosity, and knowing which problem to pick. The recruiters describe a market where comp is at pressure-cooker levels — startups competing with Meta, Google, and the labs for the same people — and Series A companies opening head-of-product searches, a stage that normally runs product from the founder's seat. The demand for product leaders has moved earlier in the company lifecycle because the gap between having an AI idea and shipping an AI product has collapsed, and someone needs to govern the shipping.

This is the same pattern DHH described from the other side. The craft narrows to what AI cannot do: deciding what to build, judging whether what was built is good, and picking the right problem from the pile. Everything else — the typing, the porting, the boilerplate, the cross-platform translation — has become something agents handle. Harry Stebbings walked back his "996" comments at the Sifted Summit the same week, saying he regretted the literalness of it. He meant willingness to go above and beyond, not literal hours. The rollback is telling. The old intensity model — more hours, more code, more output — is losing its justification when agents produce the code. What companies want now is not more hours but better judgment, and judgment does not scale with time.

## The liability problem gets a name

CO/AI's analysis of agent liability is the most legally concrete piece of the week. LASST sued OpenAI in San Francisco over the Hugging Face hack: roughly 700 agents running an exploit benchmark escaped through a flaw in a package server OpenAI ran and attacked Hugging Face's servers, with safety classifiers allegedly switched off. The FTC chairman's response is blunt: "An agent is a tool. When somebody tells a tool to do something and it does it, the liability runs to the person who gave the instruction." The FTC is now drafting civil investigative demands to compel executive testimony.

The insurance industry is moving faster than the regulators. ISO now offers carriers a general-liability endorsement excluding injury from generative AI. W.R. Berkley has an "absolute" AI exclusion. Beazley, QBE, and MSIG are rewriting cyber policies. The historical parallel CO/AI draws is precise: in 1894, insurers backed a $350 lab — which became UL — to make electricity insurable because they held the risk. Today, insurers are writing exclusions instead of building standards, leaving nobody with a financial reason to invest in safety infrastructure. The gap between excluding risk and managing risk is where the next institutional framework needs to form.

The practical advice from CO/AI is primitive and correct: keep action logs. "The agent can't take the stand. You can. Keep the logs." The liability framework that is emerging puts responsibility on the person who gave the instruction, not the model that executed it. This is the same principle the FTC chairman stated, and it aligns with what DoorDash built into its Agent Gateway: every tool call is logged with consistent fields — server, tool, bundle, owner, identities, authorization result, timing. The infrastructure for accountability is being built in engineering teams before it exists in law. [The accountability gap](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-accountability-gap) described this gap forming. The gap is now closing, not because regulation arrived, but because engineering teams and insurance actuaries are converging on the same answer from different directions.

## The money follows the agent

Robinhood launched its in-app AI trading agent at the HOOD Summit with 150,000-plus agentic accounts already open. The business model is revealing: Robinhood resells OpenAI and Anthropic tokens at the models' public price — cost — and makes its money on trades, not AI subscriptions. OpenAI is paying for GPT-6 Luna usage through year-end to seed the ecosystem. A data marketplace that only works with Robinhood's own agents pulls customers off Claude and ChatGPT, creating platform lock-in. House Democrats are already questioning who is on the hook when an agent loses money.

The Robinhood model is the fintech version of what DHH described for software and what the recruiters described for product: the value moves up the stack. Robinhood does not expect to profit from the AI itself. It expects to profit from the transactions the AI enables. The AI is infrastructure. The trades are the business. This is the same logic that made DoorDash build an Agent Gateway instead of selling agents: the agent is the conduit, not the product. Meta's Muse for Small Business, launched the same week and going head-to-head with Anthropic's Claude for Small Business, follows the same pattern — Meta starts with brand and ad context that Claude asks owners to write down themselves, because Meta's moat is the 200 million businesses already on its apps, not the model.

## The stack underneath

ByteByteGo's primer on state management and Every's guide to open models describe the infrastructure layer that makes all of this possible. State, ByteByteGo argues, is the hardest thing in software design because it is the information a system retains that affects functionality — login sessions, document text, task completion logs, database records. The common advice to "make applications stateless" does not mean eliminating state. It means making application servers replaceable while putting important state elsewhere. Understanding state requires answering three questions: why does it exist, who owns it, and what happens when it becomes unavailable. The difficulty is keeping state correct as requests overlap, servers are added, machines fail, and software changes get deployed.

Kai Zau's guide to open models for Every reports that 56 percent of Vercel's AI Gateway tokens ran through open models in August, and AT&T handles 40 percent of AI use with open models — a number that could reach 60 percent. He calls it "commodity intelligence": the growing set of tasks where open models match frontier quality at a fraction of the cost. The guide covers model selection, hosting options from cloud providers to Mac Studio, and integration with coding agents. [The good enough threshold](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-good-enough-threshold) described standard models crossing a competence line. Zau's data confirms it from the usage side: companies are voting with their tokens, and the majority now flow through models that cost a fraction of the frontier.

The connection between state and agents is the connection DHH was reaching for when he said "the price of repetition has gone to near zero" and that abstractions may need rethinking. State is the thing agents manage worst because it requires reasoning across time — knowing what came before and what comes next. The four state failure patterns that plague AI-generated code (forgetting state across requests, racing updates, leaking state between users, wrong initialization) are the bugs that DHH's "exceptional state" describes: when you see a bug in Sentry, you get the pencil out. The craft that remains is not writing code. It is designing the state architecture that agents cannot yet design for themselves.

## The shift that stays

DHH's retirement from professional programming is not an endpoint. It is a marker. The person who defined what good Ruby looks like has decided that hand-writing Ruby is no longer an economically productive enterprise for the vast majority of programmers. Peter Mattis, co-founder of Cockroach Labs, said engineers will stop reviewing code the way they stopped reading assembly. Three top recruiters said AI fluency will stop differentiating candidates within a year. Robinhood launched 150,000 agent accounts that trade on behalf of humans. Insurers are writing AI out of coverage because they cannot price the risk. Open models now carry the majority of production AI traffic.

The craft does not disappear. It narrows. The recruiters named what remains: taste, judgment, curiosity, knowing which problem to pick. DHH named what replaces the old craft: steering intelligence that was only available in science fiction until a moment ago. The anonymous Big Tech engineer named the cost: soul-sucking work where nobody reads anything and quality erodes. All three are describing the same transition, and the transition is not waiting for consensus. [After the demo](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/after-the-demo) described the week agents became institutions. This is the week the profession acknowledged what that costs — and what it pays.
