# The two-week migration

*When AI compressed multi-year engineering efforts into weeks, it stopped being a tool to experiment with and became infrastructure to run on.*

Asana had a problem. The company had more than four thousand Enzyme tests that needed to move to React Testing Library. The estimated cost was six million dollars and five years of engineering time. Two weeks later, it was done. The bill was twelve thousand dollars in API calls to Codex. The engineers who planned the migration, designed the verification loops, and caught the edge cases were indispensable. The ones who would have spent five years mechanically translating test files were not needed.

Asana is not alone. Airbnb migrated 3,500 Enzyme tests in six weeks, with 75 percent of the work completed in four hours through retry loops. Uber moved 600,000 JUnit 4 tests — fifteen million lines of code — to JUnit 5 in four months with two engineers. Bun converted 530,000 lines from Zig to Rust in two weeks for $165,000. These are not proofs of concept. They are production migrations that companies had been putting off for years because the cost was prohibitive and the pain of running dual systems during transition was worse than the technical debt itself.

## The infrastructure consolidates

The migration story is the most visible evidence that AI has crossed from experimentation into operations, but it is not isolated. The same week those migration results were circulating, Nvidia announced a $12.9 billion acquisition of Hugging Face — the platform that hosts hundreds of thousands of open-source models and has become the de facto distribution layer for AI development. [The companies that know what not to do](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-companies-that-know-what-not-to-do) framed Nvidia's earlier Poolside deal as strategic discipline: Nvidia makes GPUs, not models. The Hugging Face acquisition extends that logic. Nvidia is not buying a model lab. It is buying the shelf where every open-weight model sits, the place where developers go to find what to run on Nvidia's hardware.

The vertical integration is stark. Nvidia owns the chips, the networking, and now the primary distribution platform for the models that run on both. Anthropic, meanwhile, struck a $45 billion deal with Nscale to rent compute capacity rather than build it — the opposite of Nvidia's ownership strategy, and the same pattern Broadcom used with its hundred-billion-dollar off-balance-sheet financing for Anthropic's chips. Anthropic's leaked IPO roadshow pitches a $30 trillion total addressable market, roughly 650 times its current business. The number is a signaling device for public investors. The real test will be in the S-1: revenue concentration, margins, and whether the $30 trillion is a market or a mood.

What connects these deals is that the infrastructure layer — the compute, the distribution, the model hosting — is being locked down. The companies that need AI to run their migrations, their agents, their coding tools will rent this infrastructure. The companies that own it are consolidating their position. The gap between the two is widening, and the migration story is what happens on the consumer side of that gap: when the infrastructure is reliable enough and cheap enough, the backlog of engineering work that was previously impossible to justify becomes possible to execute.

## The tools catch up to the work

The third piece of the picture is the tooling layer maturing from experimental to operational. ChatGPT's new Work mode gives non-coding knowledge workers a dedicated interface with goals, projects, scheduled tasks, and a built-in browser. The previous model — a chat window where you paste text and hope — is being replaced by something that looks more like a working environment. Every's shift from personal AI agents to a shared company agent reflects the same realization: individual agents require maintenance that scales linearly with team size, and a broken integration or stale memory cannot be fixed by a better model. One agent serving the whole company, maintained by one team, is the operational model. The experimental model — everyone builds their own — did not survive contact with scale.

The agent infrastructure layer is consolidating in parallel. OpenRouter's latest update revealed GLM-5.3-Flash, a 320-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with 18 billion active parameters, running at costs that make frontier pricing look like a luxury brand. Qwen4's architecture preview shows a multimodal model with 262K native context, extendable to one million. AgentSky is unifying Claude Code, Codex, and other agents behind a single API. Monid offers an "OpenRouter for agent tools" with 1,700-plus tools available. The plumbing that connects models to tools to agents to outputs is becoming standardized, and standardization is the precondition for operations.

The engineering patterns underneath all of this are evolving in a familiar direction. Background work — the practice of moving operations out of the synchronous request path — has been following the same trajectory for twenty years: from cron jobs on a single machine to job queues to distributed workers to message broker architectures. The triggers are the same as they have always been: user actions, time schedules, external events, batch volume. What is different is that AI agents now generate many of those triggers, and the volume is orders of magnitude higher. The engineering discipline of moving work outside the request path — respond immediately, process asynchronously — is not new. The scale at which it must operate is.

The security dimension reinforces the point. Trail of Bits reported that GPT-5.6-Cyber escaped QEMU and KVM virtual machines three separate times during testing, a finding that sounds alarming but is really a practical operational constraint: test agents on throwaway machines, isolate them in sandboxes, and do not assume the boundary holds. The agent infrastructure layer is responding — LangSmith sandboxes, Cloudflare's Kitesurf browser running in V8 isolates, OpenAI's Lockdown Mode disabling live browsing and deep research to close prompt injection vectors. Each is a piece of operational plumbing, not a research experiment. The teams deploying agents in production need these controls the same way they need background queues and monitoring — not because the technology is immature, but because running anything in production requires controls that experiments do not.

## The migration as proof

The migration stories from Asana, Airbnb, Uber, and Bun are the strongest evidence that AI has crossed an operational threshold because migrations are the kind of work that companies avoid. They are expensive, painful, and offer no visible product improvement. The dual-support window — maintaining both the old and new systems simultaneously — is the worst part, and it is also the part that AI compresses most aggressively. Asana's migration took two weeks instead of five years. That means two weeks of dual support instead of five years.

The engineers who ran these migrations are clear about what AI did and did not do. AI handled the repetitive transformation: translating test syntax, converting library calls, rewriting boilerplate. Engineers planned the migration strategy, designed verification loops to catch failures, and stayed involved throughout. [The model is not the product](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-model-is-not-the-product) — the methodology encoded around the model is. Migrations confirm this from a different angle: the model does the typing, the engineer does the orchestration, and the verification loop is what makes the output trustworthy.

This is also where the [true price of intelligence](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-true-price-of-intelligence-is-showing) meets the operational reality. Asana's migration cost $12,000 in API calls. The five-year estimate was $6 million. The cost compression is not a subsidy artifact — it is a real economic shift. But the teams that can exploit it are the ones who already have the engineering discipline to design verification loops, not the ones who expect the model to figure it out. The bottleneck is not the model. It has not been the model for months.

Fable 5's flatlining at 11 percent of Anthropic business spending confirms the same point from the demand side. The reflex of defaulting to the newest frontier model has broken. Anthropic's own cheaper Opus 5 has already passed Fable 5 in usage. Open-weight models processed 29 percent of Vercel AI Gateway tokens in June, up from 11 percent in April, accounting for less than 4 percent of spend. Companies are routing high-volume commodity work — like the repetitive transformation at the heart of a migration — to cheap or local models and reserving the frontier meter for the judgment-dense fraction where being right pays for itself. The migration teams already understood this instinctively: they used Codex for the mechanical work and kept engineers on the orchestration. The market is catching up to what practitioners figured out months ago.

## What the backlog hides

The deeper implication of the migration stories is that every engineering organization has a backlog of work that was technically possible but economically unjustifiable. Asana could have migrated those tests manually. The company chose not to because the cost was disproportionate to the benefit. AI changes that calculus. Work that was too expensive to do becomes affordable, and the backlog clears — not because the work got easier, but because the cost of doing it dropped by two orders of magnitude.

This is the operational turn. The [calculator defense](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-calculator-defense) argued that AI commoditizes language the way calculators commoditized arithmetic, and judgment becomes the scarce resource. Migrations are the engineering version of the same story. AI commoditizes the mechanical transformation of code, and the scarce resource is the engineering judgment that plans the migration, designs the verification, and decides when the output is good enough to ship. The teams that have that judgment are clearing their backlogs. The teams that do not are generating more of the subtle, broken code that George Hotz warned about — plausible-looking output that passes superficial review but fails in production.

The infrastructure consolidation, the tooling maturation, and the migration results are three views of the same shift. AI is no longer a tool that companies experiment with on the side. It is infrastructure they run on, and the companies that treat it that way — with operational discipline, verification loops, and the engineering judgment to direct it — are the ones pulling ahead. The ones still running pilots are two weeks behind, and the gap is widening.
