# Cheap minds, expensive hands

*When the frontier model premium collapses and a personal benchmark proves the cheap model wins, the bottleneck moves from intelligence to everything intelligence needs to touch.*

Uber cut its cost per AI request by 34 percent and its cost per session by 52 percent — not by building a better model, but by dropping the one it was using. Pinterest runs its AI at less than 8 percent of what closed-model equivalents would cost. AT&T cut inference spending by 56 percent with a 2 percent quality loss it deemed acceptable. These numbers, compiled by The Pragmatic Engineer from engineering reports at Uber, Pinterest, AT&T, Stripe, Coinbase, and Ramp, describe something more than optimization. They describe a price ceiling settling over the frontier model market.

The pattern is consistent across companies that share nothing else. Most queries an application handles are simple. Sending them all to the most expensive model is like hiring a senior partner to answer every support ticket. The fix — model routing — classifies each request by complexity, dispatches the cheap ones to cheap models, and escalates only when needed. ByteByteGo estimates the savings at 10× for applications that implement it properly. The mechanics are not exotic: semantic routing classifies by meaning, cascade routing tries the cheap model first and escalates on quality failure, and complexity-based routing uses a classifier model to predict difficulty before dispatch. None of this requires a research breakthrough. It requires the operational discipline to admit that most of your traffic does not need a frontier model.

What makes this moment different from ordinary cost optimization is the evidence that the cheap models are not just adequate — they are sometimes better. Every, the publishing company, built personal benchmarks for its employees: five to ten recurring tasks per person, scored against pass/fail criteria the employee defined. GPT-5.6 Luna, the smallest and cheapest model in the lineup, outperformed both Fable 5.1 and GPT-5.6 Sol on these personal benchmarks. Public benchmarks had ranked Luna well below its larger siblings. The gap between public ranking and personal performance is the gap between what models are good at in aggregate and what they are good at for your specific work. For Dan Shipper's team, the cheaper model won on the tasks that actually mattered.

## The harness and the hand

OpenAI's Codex, the coding agent now deployed across millions of cloud instances, was written in Rust. Tibo Sottiaux, who built it, told The Pragmatic Engineer that the harness — the scaffolding around the model — is always slightly ahead of the model it serves. As models improve, crutches are discarded. What took weeks to architect can now be restructured in hours. The harness-model co-evolution means the infrastructure layer absorbs the model's weaknesses, then sheds that absorption as the weaknesses disappear. Codex supports multiple models because OpenAI learned that winning meant letting users choose, not forcing them onto the frontier.

The Codex story connects to the routing story in a way that matters for any company building on AI. The harness is where the cost discipline lives. A well-designed harness routes, falls back, and escalates. A poorly designed one sends everything to the most expensive endpoint and calls it innovation. Ramp, the corporate spend platform, reported the first month-over-month decline in enterprise AI spending in August — not because companies are using less AI, but because they are using it more efficiently. The volume is going up. The bill is going down. This is what commoditization looks like from the inside: the product gets better and cheaper simultaneously, and the companies that profit are the ones who built the plumbing, not the ones who built the model. The [stack settling](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-stack-settles) into its permanent shape was always going to produce this — a protocol layer, a context layer, and a pricing table that treats intelligence as an input, not a destination.

## The hands stay expensive

The reindustrialization of American manufacturing has a specific bottleneck, and it is not software. Every joint in every humanoid robot needs a neodymium magnet doped with dysprosium or terbium — heavy rare-earth elements that give the magnet heat resistance. China refines 94 percent of the world's rare-earth magnets, up from roughly 50 percent in 2005. The United States and Australia have the ore. They do not have the processing capacity. XPENG's IRON robot walked off the first automotive-grade humanoid assembly line this month, and every unit produced tightens a supply chain that runs through a country the US is actively trying to decouple from.

Intelligence is cheap and getting cheaper. Open-source distillation means a capable model can be downloaded and run locally. Kimi K3, a 2.78-trillion-parameter model, runs on a single CPU with 8 GB of RAM. The physical supply chain for the robots that intelligence is supposed to control cannot be downloaded. The bottleneck has moved from the mind to the hands — from the software that decides to the hardware that executes. Rebuilding mineral processing capacity is not a matter of a training run or a funding round. It is a matter of years, environmental permits, and the kind of industrial policy the US has spent four decades dismantling.

The financial infrastructure layer tells a parallel story. Anthropic grew from $9 billion to $65 billion in annualized revenue over seven months — a growth rate that broke the billing systems designed for e-commerce, not AI. AI fraud is not e-commerce fraud: it is metering-based, high-velocity, and built on micro-transactions that traditional payment processors were never designed to handle. Stripe's acquisition of OpenRouter for over $7 billion is a bet that whoever owns the metering layer for AI consumption owns a piece of every AI company's revenue. The battle is not over who builds the best model. It is over who counts the tokens.

The banking system is drawing its own lines. The OCC rejected Wise's application for a US charter, then approved Revolut's six weeks later. The difference is proprietary infrastructure: Revolut built PRAGMA, an AI trained on 40 billion banking events, giving it a credit-risk capability Wise never developed. Chime acquired Stride Bank for $590 million and promised to stay under $10 billion in assets to avoid the Durbin Amendment's interchange-fee cap. A regulatory threshold, not a charter, shaped the deal. The infrastructure you own, not the model you run, determines whether regulators treat you as a bank or a distribution layer.

## What trust costs

Meta launched Muse, its consumer AI agent, with a free tier of 100 million tokens per week — the first mass-market agent give-away at that scale. The timing was uncomfortable: an $18 billion child-safety settlement landed 13 days before launch. That trust deficit forced the most complete safety architecture any consumer agent has shipped. A separate Sentinel agent inspects every outbound action. Passwords are hidden from Muse. Approval gates gate every transaction. Meta's trust problem produced the most trustworthy consumer agent on the market — not by choice, but by necessity. The safety design [is the product](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-approval-economy), and the company with the worst record built the most rigorous version of it.

All three major agents — Muse, Grok Bot, and ChatGPT — run on the same Stripe-powered wallet. If the payment infrastructure is identical, the competitive ground shifts to something harder to commoditize: the approval screen, the moment a human says yes. Lenny's Newsletter documented the same dynamic in enterprise: Stripe built Kai, its internal AI assistant, with 1.5 engineers in two weeks, achieved 86 percent adoption across 10,000 employees, and discovered that the hard part was not the model but governance — project scopes, tool policies, skill routing, and the roughly 2,000 skills that needed taming. Enterprise AI is a governance problem wearing a model's clothing. The agent moat, as Claire observed after migrating from OpenClaw to Grok Bot, is reliability and user experience, not capability. You treat each bot as a new hire with a name and a scope. The capability table is level. The reliability curve is not.

The writers who use AI most effectively have already internalized this split between cheap intelligence and expensive judgment. Kevin Roose wrote 448 pages in a year using AI as an "outboard brain" — a pace that would have taken three to five years without it. Maggie Appleton uses dictation and Claude Code to surface connections she would have missed. Dan Shipper's Compound Writing plugin integrates AI at every stage of the process, but the plugin works because Shipper knows his own process well enough to identify where AI helps and where it hurts. The boundary between help and crutch is personal, shifting, and requires the one thing no model can provide: enough self-knowledge to recognize the difference. Alexandra Samuel wrote 52,000 words in 10 days using AI as a conversational partner. The tool was the same across all of them. The craft was not.

Europe's capital markets are learning the same lesson from the investment side. Sequoia's venture scout program is expanding across the continent, giving the firm grassroots deal-flow access previously reserved for partners. The EU's €5 billion Scaleup Europe Fund, managed by EQT, has become the dominant force in late-stage European tech funding, attacking the chronic growth-capital shortage that was the continent's primary bottleneck. Bending Spoons acquired Miro for $1.36 billion — its second major acquisition since its July IPO — proving that European companies can execute large-scale M&A. Samsung, Nvidia, and ASML backed Mistral's €3 billion Series D, bringing strategic industrial capital rather than the reflexive AI-on-AI spending that dominates US frontier model economics. The capital is arriving. The question is whether Europe can build the infrastructure — processing plants, payment rails, regulatory frameworks — that turns capital into capacity.

The cost of thinking is dropping toward zero. The cost of doing — building robots from scarce magnets, moving money through regulated rails, earning the trust to ask a human for approval, knowing your own process well enough to spot where the machine is wrong — is not. The companies that understand this split will invest in the hands, not the mind. The mind is a commodity now. The hands are where the work is.
