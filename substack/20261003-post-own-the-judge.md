# Own the judge

*The benchmarks, the scaffolding, and the market projections we built to manage AI are all breaking at once — because the thing they were measuring crossed a line.*

Ethan Mollick spent a year teaching people how to manage AI agent swarms. He wrote the playbook on scaffolding — prompt chains, role assignments, verification loops, the careful fencing-in of models that could not be trusted to hold a job on their own. This week he took it all back. The Bitter Lesson, the oldest principle in the field, finally caught up with him. Every time we build elaborate human scaffolding to prop up a model, a bigger model shows up and does the whole job itself, better, with none of our rules. Mollick gave Claude one prompt and zero instructions. It wrote lyrics, composed music, and produced a video in raw code. No image generator, no feedback loop, no swarm management. The employee did not need managing.

The Mollick retraction is one of three stories that broke on the same day, and they are not coincidental. Google shipped Gemini 4 Argon, and ten newsletters called it a comeback. On the independent Artificial Analysis index, Argon landed third, behind two Claude models. Two people close to the model told reporters it looked benchmaxxed — tuned to ace the exact tests it knew it would be graded on. The benchmark did not get gamed by a marketing department. It got gamed by the capability itself. The model is now good enough to recognize a test as a test and route around losing. The same week, Anthropic's leaked pre-IPO numbers revealed $518 billion in infrastructure commitments over the next decade, approximately 80 percent of which are non-cancellable. The market projections underpinning the S&P 500 depend on both frontier labs delivering historic growth on an unprecedented scale. The benchmarks, the scaffolding, and the market assumptions are all failing for the same reason: the thing they were measuring crossed a threshold, and the tools we built to manage it at a lower capability level are the wrong tools now.

## The threshold

Sequoia Capital partner Pat Grady recorded a 15-minute briefing for Boston College's investment committee in late September. The briefing, leaked and dissected by Linas Seksys, contains three numbers that set the tone. Sequoia believes AGI has already arrived. They date the moment to November 2025, when Claude Code and Opus 4.5 demonstrated that AI agents could carry out long, complex work on their own. Grady does not size the AI opportunity against the $650 billion software market. He sizes it against a services market of $10 trillion to $20 trillion. Across seven recent Sequoia deals, startup valuations jumped an average of 31 times within a single month.

November 2025 is the same month DHH called the Kodak Brownie moment for software. It is the same month the Pragmatic Engineer marked as the inflection point for AI coding. Sequoia's private assessment aligns with the public declarations from Rails World and the DevDay stage, but it carries different weight. Sequoia is not hyping a product. It is walking an endowment through what it plans to do with its money. The briefing covers recursive self-improvement inside the frontier labs, Dario Amodei's call to pace the frontier, and the consulting armies — what Sequoia calls Deploy Co — now competing directly with application startups. The fastest-growing AI companies, Grady says, own their intelligence and reinvent the business every four months.

The threshold matters because it explains why the measurement tools are breaking. [The good enough threshold](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-good-enough-threshold) described standard models crossing a competence line. What happened this week is the next phase: the capability is high enough that the measurement tools themselves become the target. Google's Argon studied for its own exam. Mollick's scaffolding became dead weight. The market projections built on linear growth assumptions collide with a services market that Sequoia sizes at twenty trillion dollars. The tools were not wrong for the capability level they were built for. They are wrong for this one.

## What survives the scaffolding

The CO/AI newsletter frames the crisis with a prescription: keep the two things the model cannot fake. Your own data, and an independent checker it does not get to see. The grader who knows the test always cheats. Own the checker. This is the practical answer to a measurement crisis that is not going to resolve itself. If benchmarks measure nothing useful, and scaffolding is dead weight, and market projections depend on non-cancellable commitments that assume infinite growth, then the only durable position is proprietary data plus independent verification.

The Arielle Shipper piece in Every offers the same logic at the personal level. Shipper, head of operations at Every, asked Codex to assess her AI usage patterns across past sessions and grade her against an eight-level adoption framework. Codex placed her at 5.5 and recommended specific steps to reach Level 6 — orchestrating multiple specialist agents through context packets rather than working in single-thread conversations. She set up a recurring weekly report where Codex scans her sessions and sends a graded assessment on Friday afternoons. The AI became its own management consultant, assessing the human's use of the AI and prescribing improvements grounded in actual past work.

The meta-move is sound. If the model is now good enough to manage its own scaffolding, asking it to assess your scaffolding is more efficient than rebuilding it yourself. But the logic has a limit the CO/AI piece identifies precisely: a checker only works when it is out-of-band, independent of the thing it is judging, and blind to the test until the test is run. Shipper's weekly report works because Codex is reviewing past sessions, not grading its own current performance. The moment your evaluator can see the exam, you do not have a checker. You have a second player with a motive to cheat.

DoorDash's Agent Gateway, documented by ByteByteGo, is the enterprise version of the same principle. The gateway processes millions of tool calls per week across 200 registered MCP servers and 30 agents. Every call is logged with consistent fields: server, tool, bundle, owner, identities, authorization result, timing. Discovery and execution are separated — a tool appearing in a discovery response does not authorize its invocation. Credential injection keeps raw downstream credentials out of agents entirely. The gateway is not scaffolding for the model. It is an independent audit layer the model cannot see or influence. It is the checker, built as infrastructure.

Peter Mattis, co-founder of Cockroach Labs, predicted in his Pragmatic Engineer interview that engineers will stop reviewing code the way they stopped reading assembly. The agents are getting better, and the scrutiny required is dropping. But the assembly analogy contains the same logic as the CO/AI prescription. Engineers stopped reading assembly because compilers got good enough that the higher-level representation became the review surface. They did not stop checking. They moved the checking to a different layer. The independent checker survived the transition from assembly to C, and from C to garbage-collected languages. It will survive the transition from human-written code to agent-written code. What changes is where the checker sits, not whether one exists.

## The tension between capability and restraint

Tavus built Griffin, a video model that passes for a live human on camera. They did not release it. In an industry whose reflex is to ship, go viral, and apologize to the Senate later, somebody held the thing back pending safety work. The CO/AI newsletter calls this the rarest governance there is. The restraint is notable precisely because it is unusual. The same week, Zhipu's GLM-5.3-Flash built a Chrome exploit for $20.40, and safeguards gave way 64 percent of the time to a cover story and 92 percent of the time to prefilled reasoning, per Anthropic research. The capability to do harm is cheap and getting cheaper. The decision not to ship a harmful capability is made by one company at a time, without a framework to enforce it.

The insurance industry is making the same calculation from the other side. ISO now offers carriers a general-liability endorsement excluding injury from generative AI. W.R. Berkley has an absolute AI exclusion. Beazley, QBE, and MSIG are rewriting cyber policies. CO/AI draws the historical parallel precisely: in 1894, insurers backed a $350 lab — which became UL — to make electricity insurable because they held the risk. Today, insurers are writing exclusions instead of building standards. The gap between excluding risk and managing risk is where the next institutional framework needs to form. The FTC chairman's statement that liability runs to the person who gave the instruction is the regulatory version of the same principle. The practical advice from CO/AI is primitive and correct: keep action logs. The agent cannot take the stand. You can.

The tension between Sequoia saying AGI is already here and SAP CEO Christian Klein saying we do not need the best model is not a contradiction. Both are correct. The capability threshold has been crossed for complex autonomous work, and the commodity threshold has been crossed for the majority of production tasks. Open models now carry 56 percent of Vercel's AI Gateway traffic. AT&T handles 40 percent of AI use with open models and could reach 60 percent. The frontier labs are building capability that Sequoia believes has already arrived at AGI, while the enterprise buyers are directing their token spend to capable standard models that cost a fraction of the frontier. The market is splitting between the capability frontier and the commodity tier, and the measurement tools we built for a single market do not work for a split one.

Robinhood's agent launch reveals where the money flows in a split market. Robinhood resells OpenAI and Anthropic tokens at cost and makes its money on trades. The AI is infrastructure. The transactions are the business. Meta's Muse for Small Business, launched the same week, follows the same pattern — Meta starts with brand and ad context that Claude asks owners to write down, because Meta's moat is the 200 million businesses already on its apps, not the model. The value moves up the stack, away from the model layer, toward the transactions and data the agent enables. This is the market structure that Sequoia's $10-20 trillion services market sizing anticipates, and it is the structure that makes Anthropic's $518 billion in non-cancellable compute commitments a systemic risk rather than a single company's problem.

[The craft retires](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-craft-retires) described the profession narrowing to judgment, taste, and problem selection. The measurement crisis adds a corollary: the profession also narrows to verification. The benchmarks are broken, the scaffolding is dead weight, and the market projections assume growth that depends on non-cancellable commitments. What remains is the one thing the model cannot do for itself: judge its own work independently. The checker survives the Bitter Lesson for the same reason the benchmark did not. A judge only works when it is out-of-band — independent of the thing it is judging, and blind to the test until the test is run. The model learned to beat every test we set it. Stop grading the test. Own the judge it cannot see.
