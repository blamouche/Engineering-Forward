# The companies building what they can't buy

*When Ramp, Stripe, and Shopify reject off-the-shelf AI tools to build their own, the "buy, don't build" convention cracks — and a new divide opens between companies that encode their own workflows and those that rent everyone else's.*

Ramp needed an AI coding agent that could run dozens of parallel sessions, manipulate frontends, and spin up remote development environments in under five seconds. No vendor offered that. So the fintech company built Inspect, an in-house coding agent running on Cloudflare Durable Objects and Modal sandboxes, with internal data access and snapshot restoration for fast cold starts. Every session is public. Over 150 engineers have contributed. The decision was not about saving money on vendor licenses. It was about control over a workflow that no general-purpose tool could replicate.

Ramp is not alone. Block built Goose. Stripe built Minions. Shopify built River. The conventional engineering wisdom — "buy, don't build" for developer tooling — has quietly inverted for AI, and the companies breaking that convention are the ones with the most to lose from generic tools.

## The infrastructure layer consolidates; the application layer fragments

While companies build their own agents, the infrastructure beneath them is consolidating through acquisitions large enough to restructure the market. Nvidia agreed to buy Hugging Face for $12.9 billion, giving it control from GPU silicon through to open-weight model distribution — a vertical integration play that means the same company selling you the chips now owns the platform where you discover and download models. Stripe acquired OpenRouter for $7 billion and Clerky, a legal workflows startup, to embed itself into company formation and contracting. Anthropic struck a $45 billion deal to rent compute capacity from UK-based Nscale rather than build data centers — a bet that compute-as-a-service will scale faster than owned infrastructure.

[Own the judgment, rent the rest](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/own-the-judgment-rent-the-rest) covered this consolidation: the stack from silicon to distribution is compressing, and the layers worth owning are narrowing. But the flip side of that compression is what happens at the application layer, where AI meets actual work. There, the pattern runs in the opposite direction. Instead of consolidation, you get fragmentation — each company building tools tuned to its own workflows, expertise, and constraints.

The reason is structural. Off-the-shelf AI coding agents are built to serve the median customer. Ramp is not the median customer. Its needs — many parallel agents, frontend manipulation, remote sandboxes with internal data access — exceed what frontier AI labs ship as defaults. The same is true for Stripe, where payment-systems code has compliance requirements no general-purpose agent handles, and for Shopify, where merchant-facing tooling demands a level of customization that off-the-shelf harnesses cannot provide. The gap between what vendors ship and what high-end engineering teams need has grown wide enough that building becomes the rational choice, not the eccentric one.

The security dimension sharpens the calculation. Researchers demonstrated that encrypted reasoning blocks from frontier AI models can be replayed into cheaper models in the same family to extract hidden chain-of-thought in plaintext — proprietary reasoning that providers deliberately withhold. Trail of Bits warned that cyber-agents escaped QEMU and KVM virtual machines. OpenAI added a lockdown mode to counter prompt injection. When you run your engineering workflows through a third-party agent, you are routing your proprietary code and reasoning through infrastructure you do not control. Ramp's Inspect runs on internal sandboxes with internal data access. That is not just a performance decision. It is a security posture.

## Encoding what benchmarks can't measure

The build-your-own impulse extends beyond coding agents. Every, a media company, is systematically turning its employees' expertise into agent skills. KateBench, trained on 30,000 of editor Kate Lee's historical edits, runs a first copyediting pass before human review. Tracked changes feed back into Codex for self-improvement. DanLens encodes marketing instincts. A self-improving workflow feeds errors back as targeted instruction edits, so the skills compound over time. The plan is to clone expertise across the company — UX feedback, logistical planning, social positioning — turning individual judgment into an institutional resource.

This is not generic AI usage. It is the encoding of institutional knowledge — the kind that lives in an editor's accumulated corrections, a marketer's pattern recognition, a consultant's framework discipline — into systems that can apply it at scale. Twelve Claude Skills built on published consulting frameworks (Minto's Pyramid Principle, Playing to Win, the CIA's Analysis of Competing Hypotheses) lifted Claude Fable 5 from 38% to 97% on a partner-level checklist. The frameworks were never secret. The constraint was always the two years of partner-supervised practice required to internalize them. Encoding them as executable steps with checkpoints and worked examples collapses that constraint. Companies paid McKinsey, BCG, and Bain roughly $37 billion last year for advice mostly delivered by associates with under two years of experience. Clients were buying the method, not the person. The method can now be encoded.

[The model is not the product](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-model-is-not-the-product) made this argument: the methodology encoded around the model is what creates value. The companies building custom agents are taking that argument to its operational conclusion. They are not just wrapping an API. They are building systems that capture their specific judgment, their specific workflows, and their specific quality standards — things no vendor can ship because no vendor has them.

The measurement gap reinforces the build decision. Companies spending $100 million a year on AI often have no idea whether those models save employees time or produce trustworthy work. Public benchmarks measure general capability, not performance on the specific tasks a company needs. Every's KateBench illustrates both sides: an 85-90% acceptance rate that looks impressive, but hides a 40-suggestion cap, non-determinism, and residual editor work that the metric does not capture. The practical prescription is concrete: pick one recurring job, document five failure modes with gold-standard examples, and test models against those cases. That tells you more than any leaderboard comparison. And once you are building internal evals, the distance to building the agent itself shrinks.

The migration stories tell the same story from a different angle. Asana migrated 4,000 Enzyme tests to React Testing Library in two weeks for roughly $12,000 — work previously estimated at $6 million over five years. Airbnb migrated 3,500 tests in six weeks. Uber moved 600,000 JUnit 4 tests across 15 million lines of code in four months with two engineers. But in every case, engineers owned the planning, the verification loop design, and the orchestration. AI did the mechanical work. The strategic work — deciding what to migrate, designing the retry loops, verifying correctness — stayed in-house. That division of labor is the same one driving the build-versus-buy decision for agents. The commodity work can be automated. The judgment work cannot be bought.

## What building costs

The case against building is straightforward. Engineering talent is expensive and scarce. Maintenance burden grows with scale — Every's candid update on OpenClaw noted that expired credentials, broken integrations, and stale memory compound with team size, and no stronger model solves that. The maintenance problem is real enough that Every is pivoting from personal agents to a shared Slack-based company agent, trading individual customization for institutional manageability. Not every company has Ramp's 150-contributor engineering bench or Stripe's platform team. The "buy, don't build" convention exists because building is hard and the opportunity cost is real.

But the companies choosing to build are making a different calculation. Ramp's Inspect runs on remote sandboxes with pre-warming and snapshot restoration, achieving cold-start times under five seconds — a performance characteristic no third-party harness matches. The sessions are public with no opt-outs, creating an internal knowledge base of agent interactions that itself becomes a training asset. Block's Goose, Stripe's Minions, and Shopify's River each address similar gaps between what vendors offer and what these companies' engineering workflows demand. The question each company answered is not whether they can afford to build, but whether they can afford to depend on tools that do not understand their work.

Fable 5's flatlining at 11% of Anthropic business spend — two months after launch, according to Ramp data covering 70,000 companies — tells the demand-side story. The reflex of defaulting to the newest frontier model has broken. An Accel partner put it plainly: most people do not need the frontier. Companies are routing high-volume commodity work to cheaper models, reserving the frontier for judgment-dense tasks. GLM-5.3-Flash, a 320B-parameter mixture-of-experts model with 18B active parameters, approaches Claude Opus 4.8 on coding at far lower cost. Open-weight models processed 29% of Vercel AI Gateway tokens in June for less than 4% of spend. When the model layer commoditizes, differentiation moves to the layer above — the orchestration, the evaluation, the workflow encoding. That is the layer companies are building.

## The dividing line

[The parts that don't write themselves](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-parts-that-dont-write-themselves) noted that the engineering work remaining in human hands — performance, security, evaluation, architecture — is where the value has moved. The companies building custom AI tooling are making a related bet: that the work of encoding those workflows is also worth keeping in-house. Every's self-improving Codex workflow — where mistakes become instruction edits that prevent recurrence — is a flywheel that off-the-shelf tools cannot replicate. Ramp's public session model creates an institutional memory of agent interactions. The consulting frameworks encoded as Claude Skills turn a $37 billion industry's methodology into a downloadable artifact. Each of these systems captures something specific to the company that built it, and that specificity is the moat.

The companies that only buy will face tools built for the median customer — tools that serve everyone and no one. They will hit the measurement wall sooner: spending money on models whose impact they cannot verify, because the evals that would verify it require understanding their own workflows well enough to build them. The dividing line is not technical sophistication. It is whether a company treats AI as a product to consume or a capability to build. The answer to that question will determine which companies compound their advantage and which ones plateau on someone else's roadmap.
