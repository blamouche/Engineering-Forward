# The land grab

*When the model becomes a commodity, the companies buying banks, agent platforms, and entire legaltech markets are betting that ownership beats intelligence.*

Meta acquired Stilla, a Stockholm-based company-wide agent platform, and folded it into Meta Business Agent. Chime paid $590 million for Stride Bank, its Oklahoma sponsor bank of seven years. Lexroom, a Milan-based legal AI platform, bought two companies in a week to expand across five European markets. Sequoia deployed venture scouts across Europe to hunt for deals at the grassroots level. These happened in the same week that Uber, Pinterest, and AT&T reported cutting their AI bills by 50 to 90 percent by switching from frontier models to open alternatives. None of these stories are about AI capability. They are about owning the layer underneath.

The pattern is not subtle. When intelligence is cheap and getting cheaper, the companies that build durable positions do it by acquiring the infrastructure that intelligence runs on — the banking charter, the agent platform, the legal market, the deal flow. Meta's three-product loop — Muse for the buyer, Business Agent for the seller, Stilla for the post-sale work — closes a transaction circuit that advertising never could. Chime's bank acquisition was shaped not by the charter itself but by the Durbin Amendment's $10 billion asset threshold, a regulatory line that determines whether interchange revenue stays or gets capped. Lexroom's CEO Paolo Fois called it a "land grab moment." He was talking about legaltech. He could have been talking about the whole industry.

## The price collapse that triggered the grab

The Pragmatic Engineer compiled evidence from Uber, Pinterest, AT&T, Stripe, Coinbase, and Ramp that the economics of frontier models have broken down. Uber cut cost per AI request by 34 percent and cost per session by 52 percent through open weight models, optimized model selection, cheaper subagents, and context compaction. Pinterest achieves cost per transaction at less than 8 percent of what comparable closed models cost by post-training open source models on its own data. AT&T cut its AI bill by 56 percent with a 2 percent quality loss. Anthropic's Opus 5 is now 100x more expensive than models like GPT-5.6 Luna and DeepSeek for workloads where the capability gap has narrowed to single digits. Ramp's data shows AI spend at the top 1 percent of businesses declined 10 percent in August — the first hard evidence that the enterprise AI spending boom has peaked, not from less usage, but from smarter spending.

This price collapse is the precondition for the land grab. When the model is a commodity, the model is not the business. The business is the infrastructure around it — the payment rails, the data, the trust architecture, the regulatory license, the customer relationship. The companies spending billions on acquisitions this week are not buying models. They are buying the rails.

Every CEO Dan Shipper and head of evals Mike Taylor built personal benchmarks for every employee and found that GPT-5.6 Luna — the smallest, cheapest model in the lineup — outperformed Fable 5.1 and GPT-5.6 Sol on tasks tailored to their actual work. Public leaderboards had ranked Luna well below its larger siblings. The insight has a structural implication: if a cheaper model wins on the tasks that matter to a specific company, the premium model market has a ceiling. And if the premium model market has a ceiling, the companies whose valuations depend on model superiority — Anthropic at $65 billion in annualized revenue, OpenAI at its IPO pricing — face a different kind of risk than the one their safety teams are warning about. They face the risk that their product becomes electricity: essential, ubiquitous, and priced as a utility.

OpenAI's decision to productize the Codex harness as a public API with no platform fee is a direct response to this pressure. The harness — managed compute environments, tool routing, subagent coordination, context compaction — is what makes an agent reliable enough to delegate to. By giving it away and charging only for model and tool usage, OpenAI is positioning itself as the default substrate for agentic applications. The strategy mirrors AWS: own the infrastructure layer, let the applications compete on top. Sakana AI's Fugu approach, routing work across swappable model pools instead of defaulting to the largest model, validates the same thesis from the other side — the model is swappable, the routing layer is the asset.

## What the rails look like

Meta's acquisition of Stilla is the most complete illustration. Stilla gives Meta the internal layer its Business Agent lacked: one agent across Slack, Teams, GitHub, and roughly 3,000 tools, with company memory, per-user permissions, and SOC 2 compliance. The founders previously sold Tictail to Shopify and worked on Shop Pay. Meta bought the people who built the checkout it plans to integrate into Muse. The three-product loop — Muse for the buyer, Business Agent for the seller, Stilla for the post-sale work — means Meta's agents sit at both ends of a transaction that runs over payment rails Meta does not own. The acquisition is also a trust admission: Muse shipped with reported safeguard failures, and enterprises will not accept that when GitHub and payroll are attached. Meta bought Stilla's permission model rather than building one. [The approval economy](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-approval-economy) traced how trust became the product; Meta's Stilla acquisition is the purchase order for that product.

The financial infrastructure stories follow the same logic. Chime's $590 million acquisition of Stride Bank was shaped by the Durbin Amendment's $10 billion asset threshold — above that line, debit interchange gets capped, and interchange represents 39 percent of Chime's 2026 revenue. The charter eliminates the sponsor bank fee but constrains the growth ceiling. The deal reveals how regulatory thresholds shape fintech strategy more than charters do. Revolut's preliminary conditional OCC approval for a national bank charter, granted six weeks after Wise was rejected, was decided by proprietary infrastructure: PRAGMA, a foundation model trained on 40 billion behavioral events from 25 million users across 111 countries. No third-party model can replicate it because no third party has Revolut's data. The bank charter is the rail. The data is the moat. [Cheap minds, expensive hands](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/cheap-minds-expensive-hands) described how the bottleneck moved from intelligence to infrastructure; the banking stories prove the point from inside the financial system.

The legaltech consolidation tells the same story in a different register. Legora, valued at $5.6 billion after raising $600 million this year, has signed five acquisitions. Harvey, valued at $15.5 billion after raising $550 million, has signed four. Lexroom's two acquisitions in a week mark the moment the race reached the mid-tier. Fois differentiates Lexroom's approach as geographic — going deeper in existing markets rather than competing for the same product-driven targets as the gorillas. The consolidation is happening because a legal AI platform's value increases with its jurisdictional footprint and data corpus, and acquisitions build both faster than organic growth. The model is the same model every legaltech company uses. The rail is the customer relationship and the data it generates.

## The risk conversation's policy shadow

The AI risk conversation that dominated the week — Anthropic alignment lead Evan Hubinger putting better-than-10 percent odds on AI extinction this decade, senior researcher Jacob Coxon resigning in protest, CEO Dario Amodei publishing an essay arguing companies should deliberately slow model improvement — has a direct connection to the land grab. Alex Kantrowitz, writing in Big Technology, identified the structural dynamic that matters: both OpenAI and Anthropic are pursuing recursive self-improvement, and the prisoner's dilemma means no one who could slow down can afford to. Speaking out about risk may lead to data center pauses and policy hearings, which would dent IPO momentum for both companies. But going public about fears is not marketing for the labs — the downsides of pauses do the opposite of what good marketing requires.

CO/AI's analysis went further, arguing that Amodei's silence during the week his own safety chief went on record with an extinction number is the informative signal. The expensive silence is the one that costs something. The two men with all the information and everything to lose went quiet or went vague, while the junior researcher who screamed has little stock to protect. The piece framed it through the Kubrick Doomsday Machine: the terror was never that a villain would choose to fire it, but that the device took the human hand off the switch by design. Build, or be beaten.

The policy implication connects to the land grab directly. If AI risk triggers regulation — data center pauses, export controls, licensing requirements — the companies that already own the rails are the ones that survive. Meta owns the agentic commerce loop. Chime owns a bank charter. Revolut owns a data moat and a charter. The companies that depend only on model superiority are the ones exposed. The risk conversation is not separate from the consolidation story. It is the policy shadow of the same dynamic: ownership is the hedge against a future where the rules change.

CO/AI's parallel observation — that the $400K which funded the 9/11 attacks now represents the cost ceiling for AI-enabled infrastructure attacks, and that sophistication has stopped being a reliable signal of who is behind an operation — adds a darker dimension. Anthropic's abuse report found that a lone hacktivist ran a complex influence operation previously attributed to state actors. The barrier to sophisticated operations has collapsed. The regulatory framework for autonomous, digital threats barely exists. The land grab is not only commercial. It is also a race to build the defensive infrastructure — the trust architecture, the approval gates, the audit trails — before the offensive infrastructure outpaces it.

## What the scouts see

Sequoia's venture scout program expanding across Europe is the investment-side version of the same pattern. Scouts — typically operators and former founders — give the firm grassroots deal-flow access that traditional partner-led outreach cannot match. European founders are launching their own funds, bringing deep operational experience to early-stage investing. Bending Spoons acquired Miro for $1.36 billion in cash, following its $2.25 billion acquisition of Airtable in August, building what the Italian tech group calls a "legendary institution" portfolio. Samsung, Nvidia, and ASML backed Mistral's €3 billion Series D at a €21 billion valuation, bringing strategic industrial capital rather than the reflexive AI-on-AI spending that dominates US frontier model economics.

The European capital markets are building the growth infrastructure they lacked for a decade. The EU's €5 billion Scaleup Europe Fund has become the dominant force in late-stage European tech funding. The Sifted 100 DACH & CEE leaderboard highlights capital-efficient startups across the region. Nordic Tech Week's "Pitching politicians" session ahead of Sweden's general election signals growing startup community lobbying around share options, housing, and policy fragmentation. The scouts are not looking for the next frontier model. They are looking for the next rail — the payment infrastructure, the data moat, the regulatory license, the customer relationship — that the model runs on.

The land grab has a simple logic. When the model is cheap, the model is not the business. The business is everything the model touches: the bank charter, the agent platform, the legal market, the deal flow, the trust architecture. The companies spending billions this week know this. The ones still betting on model superiority are the ones the price collapse will catch. The [half-life of what you know](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-half-life-of-what-you-know) described how tool-specific expertise depreciates while fundamentals compound. The same principle applies to companies: model-specific advantages depreciate. Infrastructure-specific advantages compound. The land grab is the acquisition strategy that follows from that recognition.
