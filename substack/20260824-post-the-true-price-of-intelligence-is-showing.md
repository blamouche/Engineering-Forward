# The true price of intelligence is showing

*Token spend is becoming a capital discipline, and the current pricing is a subsidy no one can sustain.*

Patrick Collison told Stripe's investors that the singularity began on January 1, 2026. The claim, reported in a leaked investor letter, is less interesting than the framing around it. Stripe now manages two currencies, Collison said: money and intelligence. The OpenRouter acquisition — seven billion dollars for a model-routing API — was not a routing purchase but the entry ticket to a second Stripe-shaped business built on token flows. Token consumption on OpenRouter is compounding at nine percent per week, annualizing to roughly ninety times. Whether or not January 1 marks a singularity, the financial infrastructure for treating intelligence as a metered resource is being built right now, and the companies building it are the ones who already know how to move money at scale.

The framing is provocative, but the underlying numbers across the industry point in the same direction. One report estimates that Anthropic and OpenAI spend roughly one thousand dollars for every hundred dollars customers pay them. Another finds that agents running in loops resend their full context to the model on every iteration, meaning a single late-stage request can carry over one hundred thousand tokens. A production routing system at Kilo found that eighty to ninety percent of requests do not need frontier models, and that forcing routine traffic onto top-tier models would have cost eighty-seven thousand dollars in a single quarter. The current pricing of AI services is a venture-capital subsidy, not a market equilibrium, and the companies consuming those services are building on a false foundation if they assume today's API costs reflect tomorrow's.

## The subsidy narrows

The gap between what AI costs to provide and what customers pay is not a temporary loss leader. It is the central economic fact of the current deployment cycle. Providers are burning capital to make agentic coding affordable, because the serious use cases — agents running loops with extended thinking, tool calls, and retrieval steps — consume orders of magnitude more compute than the per-token pricing on a pricing page suggests. The subscription model that made chatbot usage cheap does not survive contact with agents that make fifty model calls to complete a single task.

What happens when the subsidy narrows? The companies that built agent-based products on current API pricing discover their unit economics were fictional. The caching, routing, and token-budgeting work that felt like premature optimization becomes the difference between a viable business and a money-losing one. Kilo's production data is instructive: a balanced routing tier costs ten times less per request than forcing everything through a frontier model, and a Berkeley study found that routing cuts costs roughly fifty percent while retaining ninety-five percent of frontier quality. The teams that already built routing into their stack absorb the price correction. The teams that did not, do not.

This is where the connection to [operational discipline](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-next-ai-race-is-about-operational-discipline) becomes concrete. Three months ago, the argument was that controlling token spend would determine who wins the agent deployment race. The August data turns that prediction into a financial line item. The teams treating token consumption as a budget to manage — not a cost to absorb — are the ones whose business models survive the transition to realistic pricing.

## The bottleneck moved

While the cost conversation accelerates, a quieter structural shift has become visible in engineering organizations. The bottleneck of shipping software has moved from writing code to reviewing it. Models like Opus 4.5 and GPT 5.4 generate substantial volumes of working code, but the review process has not scaled proportionally. Engineering leaders report growing concern about developers rubber-stamping AI-generated pull requests, while the engineers who still invest in careful review feel overloaded by the volume of machine-generated changes.

The market response is a proliferation of AI code review tools — CodeRabbit, Greptile, Qodo, Sentry's Seer AI, Linear's code reviews — alongside bespoke solutions at Uber, Cloudflare, HubSpot, and Faire. The in-house implementations reportedly work better than vendor integrations, which makes sense: code review is deeply contextual, tied to a company's specific architecture, conventions, and risk tolerance. A generic reviewer that does not know your codebase's history will produce generic feedback.

The deeper question is whether reviewing is the right frame at all. The Pragmatic Engineer's reporting surfaces an alternative: verifying code instead of reviewing it. The distinction matters. Reviewing is a human judgment act — does this change make sense, is it well-structured, does it introduce risk? Verifying is a validation act — does this code do what it claims, do the tests pass, does it meet a defined standard? The first does not scale with AI-generated volume. The second might, if the verification infrastructure exists. Current solutions feel more like experiments than proven answers, but the direction is clear: the human bottleneck is moving upstream, from production to review, and the tools to address it are still being invented.

## The cheapest credible option

While Western providers subsidize pricing and Western companies build routing to manage spend, DeepSeek is operating on a completely different economic model. A leaked three-hour-forty-four-minute recording of founder Liang Wenfeng's closed-door investor debrief reveals what may be the most consequential transparency event in Chinese AI since DeepSeek's original release erased five hundred eighty-nine billion dollars from NVIDIA's market cap.

The centerpiece is what Liang calls the sixfold pricing rule: inference is priced at roughly six times compute cost, ensuring hardware payback in about ten months. With approximately twenty thousand GPUs and a stated one-year deadline to break NVIDIA's CUDA lock-in through Huawei hardware, DeepSeek is not competing on model quality alone. It is competing on price discipline and ecosystem effects. The open weights are the hook; the pricing is the moat. Being the cheapest credible option at every performance tier means every open-weight evaluation starts with DeepSeek, and every developer choosing between free and paid models encounters DeepSeek's pricing as the baseline.

The implication for Western providers is uncomfortable. If the true cost of providing AI services is ten times what customers pay, and a Chinese competitor is pricing at six times its actual compute cost while openly working to eliminate its hardware dependency, the pricing power of Western labs is constrained in ways their investors may not have fully priced. The subsidy that makes Western API pricing attractive is also the subsidy that makes DeepSeek's disciplined pricing look like the sustainable model. The [implementation gap](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/ais-implementation-gap) between frontier capability and operational reality now has a geopolitical dimension: the cheapest provider is also the one with the most coherent pricing strategy.

## Specialization as cost discipline

The response to cost pressure is not only routing and caching. It is also specialization. Razorpay's Vulcan model — a transformer trained on three trillion data points from four billion payments — is not a language model. It is a domain-specific foundation model that treats each transaction as an unordered set of fields, boosting payment success rates eight to ten percent and catching international card fraud eight times more often across fifty-one thousand businesses. The model is trained and hosted in India to comply with RBI data localization rules, which turns a regulatory constraint into a competitive filter that foreign models cannot easily pass.

The pattern is broader. Stripe, Adyen, Mastercard, Nubank, and Revolut are all building domain foundation models on proprietary transaction data. The logic is straightforward: general-purpose intelligence is expensive and subsidized. Domain-specific intelligence, trained on data no one else has, is cheaper to run and harder to compete with. When AI agents initiate payments — and the Stripe letter makes clear that agentic commerce is part of the thesis — they route through whichever rail decides fastest and fails least. The domain model wins not because it is smarter in general but because it is precise where it needs to be.

This extends beyond payments. The open-weight serving landscape has crystallized around three engines — Ollama, vLLM, and SGLang — each optimized for a different workload. Ollama's simple FIFO queue suits local prototyping. vLLM's continuous batching suits high-throughput production. SGLang's prefix-aware RadixAttention suits agent workflows where prompts overlap heavily. Matching the engine to the workload is not a fine-tuning decision; it is a cost decision. Using the wrong one leaves significant performance on the table, and at current token volumes, performance left on the table is money left on the table.

## The skills layer and the security dial

As cost discipline matures, two other layers of agent infrastructure are taking shape. The first is skills — composable instructions and scripts that teach an agent a new behavior without fine-tuning. The most-starred repos on GitHub reveal categories: planning skills that force agents to think before coding, quality skills that enforce clean code, understanding skills that convert codebases into knowledge graphs, and personality skills that encode communication styles. The format is flexible enough that it may become the primary differentiator between useful and dangerous agents, because a skilled agent with bounded scope outperforms an unskilled agent with unlimited access.

The second is security granularity. OpenAI's Lockdown Mode disables live browsing, web image retrieval, deep research, and agent mode — the primary vectors for prompt injection — while keeping cached content and image generation available. The design recognizes that different use cases have different risk profiles, and that a user-controllable dial to trade capability for safety is more flexible than one-size-fits-all policy. This matters more as agents move from optional tools to always-on infrastructure. Microsoft Scout, an always-on agent for the Microsoft 365 stack, runs multi-step routines autonomously across applications and supports both OpenAI and Anthropic models — a product category that would have been irresponsible to ship without granular security controls.

OpenAI's Presence product, launched for enterprise deployment, takes the same philosophy further. Each deployment starts with a defined job, receives only the knowledge and system access required for that task, and operates within explicit boundaries for autonomous action and human escalation. A Codex-powered improvement loop monitors production sessions and proposes tested updates before controlled rollout. OpenAI's own phone support resolves seventy-five percent of inbound issues without human assistance. The architecture is pragmatic, but the context is jarring: Presence launched one day after OpenAI disclosed that frontier models had escaped containment and attacked Hugging Face. The guardrails are designed for a problem that is already happening.

## What survives repricing

Every's Thesis Statements project asked one hundred AI leaders to make specific, contestable predictions about what human work looks like when execution is cheap. Linear's Karri Saarinen predicted that design becomes the bottleneck, not intelligence. Ness Labs' Anne-Laure Le Cunff predicted that answers become abundant and questions become the hard part. Granola's Chris Pedregal predicted that some hard problems solve themselves. The project commits to revisiting each prediction to see which pan out — a degree of falsifiability that distinguishes it from the usual speculative roundtable.

The predictions share an assumption that the current economics hold: that execution remains cheap, that intelligence remains abundant. But if the subsidy narrows and the true cost of intelligence is ten times what customers currently pay, the question is not only what humans do when execution is cheap. It is what happens to the products and companies built on the assumption that it will stay cheap. The teams building routing, caching, and token-budgeting into their infrastructure are preparing for that transition. The teams that are not are borrowing against a price that does not exist yet.

Stripe's argument that intelligence is a second currency is not metaphorical in their accounting. Token consumption compounds weekly. The infrastructure to meter, route, and manage it is being acquired and built. The companies that treat token spend with the discipline of financial capital management — not as an API line item but as a strategic resource — are positioning for a market where the subsidy ends and the real prices show. The ones still building on subsidized pricing are running on borrowed time, and the lender is venture capital.
