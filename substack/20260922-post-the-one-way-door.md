# The one-way door

*Two real catastrophes show what happens when AI moves faster than human verification — and the framework for preventing the next one is sorting decisions by whether you can undo them.*

An analyst at US Special Operations Command Pacific asked a chatbot to read a ship's manifest. The machine mixed open-source chatter with classified signals and produced a confident conclusion: the Chinese vessel was carrying nuclear weapons components. A second AI pass turned that conclusion into a formal intelligence report. Two AI passes, zero verification between them. Planes were in the air. Boarding teams were staging. The United States was one order away from firing on a Chinese ship. Then someone opened the source. The report was entirely false. Eight words to tape to your monitor: AI allows you to get to a bad idea faster.

The Minab case is grimmer. Two Tomahawk missiles hit an elementary school in Iran. 123 children died. Pentagon investigators traced the strike partly to overreliance on Palantir's Maven AI targeting tool. Vetting that once took hours — hours spent checking whether the target was actually what the data said it was — got compressed into minutes. The Centcom team whose job was to catch exactly that kind of error had been cut from ten people to one. The savings math dropped the column where you verify the work.

These are not hypothetical scenarios from a safety research paper. They happened. And they share a structural cause that has nothing to do with model intelligence. The machines were not too smart. They were too fast. To a language model, a support ticket and a kill order are the same problem — same confidence, same tone, same half-second answer. The gradient between reversible and irreversible decisions lives only in the human head. When you optimize away the human, you optimize away the gradient.

## The sorting principle

Jeff Bezos described the framework years before AI was a commercial product. Every decision falls into one of two categories. Two-way doors are reversible: you walk through, see what's on the other side, and walk back if you don't like it. One-way doors are not: once you walk through, you cannot return. Most decisions in a technology company are two-way doors. A feature ships, users ignore it, you pull it. A pricing experiment fails, you revert. The cost of being wrong is low and temporary. For two-way doors, speed is pure upside. Let the machine rip.

One-way doors are different. Firing a missile. Wiring money. Publishing a legal filing. Shipping a medical device. These decisions cannot be undone, so you stop maximizing return and start minimizing the probability of catastrophe. Speed is free right up until the door only swings one way. The machine cannot tell a two-way door from a one-way one. Humans must.

This sorting principle sounds simple because it is. The failure mode is not conceptual complexity. It is the quiet erosion of the friction that used to enforce the sorting. The Centcom review team that caught targeting errors existed because someone understood that certain decisions need a human in the loop. Cutting that team from ten to one was framed as efficiency. It was the removal of the only mechanism that distinguished a two-way door from a one-way one. The friction wasn't waste. It was how the system spent more deliberation on the decisions that could kill you.

The corporate version of the same dynamic showed up in McKinsey's data: 30 percent of companies using agentic AI got slower, not faster. Not because the model failed, but because the organizations hadn't defined what "done" meant as a number before they deployed. The J-curve is real — output dips before it climbs — and the shops that quit during the dip booked the cost and none of the gain. Glean's survey of 6,000 workers found that AI saves 11 hours per week, but 6.5 of those hours go back into checking, correcting, and re-feeding the machine. Net saving: under half the headline. [The checking floor is where most of the saved time goes back](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-checking-floor), but the floor is cheaper to stand on than it used to be. The question is not whether to check. It is where to put the gate.

## The factory that sorts its own doors

Warp's software factory offers a working model of the sorting principle in an engineering context. The factory — an agent system called Wilson — takes a Slack request through triage, Linear ticket creation, implementation, pull request generation, computer-use QA, and merge. It produces a pull request in 35 minutes. The metric Warp tracks is not tokens generated or PRs merged. It is "human interactions per pull request" — every follow-up prompt, clarification, and correction counts as additional human effort. If the agent requires heavy steering, throughput drops regardless of how fast it writes code.

The 35-minute kickoff-to-PR time is impressive. The 3.5-hour wait for the first human review is the real bottleneck. Warp's response is pragmatic: let the person who prompted the agent review its work rather than requiring a separate reviewer. This compresses the review cycle but keeps the human gate intact. Zach Lloyd, Warp's founder, expects accumulated trust will eventually allow selected changes to merge without human review. But the factory's architecture bakes in the sorting: routine changes flow through automatically; riskier ones surface for human judgment. The AI scoring layer — a mid-tier model that judges every run across multiple dimensions, including whether tests actually protect against regressions or merely confirm current behavior — is the mechanism that performs the sorting. It is a one-way door detector.

The self-improvement loop is the most architecturally significant detail. Warp collects roughly 20 to 25 failed runs until enough evidence identifies a pattern. An observer agent analyzes what went wrong and proposes changes to the factory's agent definitions. Because those definitions are stored as code, the same agents that complete the work can update the instructions governing how they operate. This is a closed-loop system where the factory gets better at its own job over time — not through model upgrades but through configuration evolution. The factory learns which doors are one-way and adjusts its behavior accordingly. The AI judge that scores every run is the gate. The observer that proposes fixes is the learning mechanism. Together, they form a system that sorts its own doors.

## The line Anthropic drew

Anthropic published its R&D Automation Index the same week, and the numbers tell a story the company probably did not intend to tell so plainly. Claude now "leads" 26 percent of Anthropic's own R&D, with roughly 30,000 internal agents running and 90 percent or more of work rated at "collaborates or higher." The load-bearing number is 0 percent fully autonomous. Zero. A company racing toward a November IPO on a $100 billion revenue pace, with every incentive to inflate its AI capability numbers, chose to publish a metric that explicitly caps autonomy at zero. That is not modesty. It is a company drawing the one-way door line in its own operations before a regulator draws it for them.

The 0 percent figure is the corporate equivalent of the Centcom lesson. Anthropic could report that 30,000 agents handle 90 percent of R&D work at collaboration level or above. That sounds like near-autonomy. But the last 10 percent — the decision to ship, to publish, to deploy — stays with humans. The company that builds Claude is saying, through data rather than rhetoric, that the one-way door in research and development requires a human hand on the handle. The 26 percent "led by Claude" means the agent proposes directions and does work. The 0 percent "fully autonomous" means no agent makes the final call. The gap between those two numbers is where Anthropic puts its human gate.

Google's disclosure that Gemini was used to breach three companies reinforces the point from the security side. All three US frontier labs have now confessed their models were turned into attack tools. The disclosure race means being the lab that didn't report misuse is now the reputational risk. Google admitted it. OpenAI admitted it. Anthropic admitted it. The admissions are not about model capability. They are about the one-way door of disclosure: once you've been used as an attack tool and haven't reported it, the regulatory and reputational consequences are irreversible. The labs are sorting their own doors, and they've decided that silence is a one-way door they won't walk through.

## The architectural gate

The one-way door framework has an architectural expression. ByteByteGo's guide to prompt injection defenses describes a pattern called the planner-executor split. The planner — the language model with tool access — never sees untrusted content. The executor — the language model that reads untrusted content — has no tools. You create a separation that makes indirect injection much harder to exploit, even if neither model is perfectly resistant to injection on its own. The architecture enforces the sorting: the model that can act cannot be manipulated, and the model that can be manipulated cannot act.

This is the one-way door principle applied to system design. The planner makes decisions that have consequences — calling APIs, writing files, sending messages. Those are one-way door operations. The executor reads external data that might contain adversarial prompts. That is a two-way door operation — reading is reversible. By separating the two, you ensure that untrusted input cannot directly trigger irreversible actions. The human-in-the-loop approval and least-privilege tools that complement this split are additional gates. Gmail uses this stacked defense model in production. The stacking is what makes indirect injection manageable rather than solved.

ByteByteGo's broader point about API security applies here. Most API problems begin with basic details — HTTP methods, status codes, request formats — and compound through design decisions around naming, pagination, versioning, and backward compatibility that get insufficient attention early. Security and reliability — timeouts, retries, idempotency, rate limits — are the areas where weak decisions cause real production problems, and they are the areas most likely to be deferred until a system is under pressure. The same pattern applies to AI systems. The one-way door decisions — which operations are reversible, what requires human approval, how failures propagate — are the ones most likely to be deferred until something breaks. The Minab strike is what happens when the deferral becomes permanent.

## The European moat

The one-way door framework has a geographic dimension that the a16z partner visiting from San Francisco identified this week. European regulatory frameworks — GDPR, the AI Act, MiCA — are not just compliance burdens. They are moats. Companies that build privacy-first, regulation-compliant products from day one have a structural advantage as global regulation converges toward European standards. The framework for managing AI risk that CO/AI advocates — sorting decisions by reversibility, funding the verification layer, defining "done" as a number — is exactly what European regulation mandates. The companies that have been operating under these constraints for years are not behind. They are practiced.

The a16z partner's argument inverts the usual framing. For years, the European tech conversation was about catching up: more capital, bigger funds, deeper talent pools, more risk appetite. The "unapologetically European" framing says the opportunity is not to become a better Silicon Valley. It is to be the best version of itself. Europe's deep tech talent in semiconductor design, quantum computing, and materials science comes from a different tradition than US software-first engineering — one that may prove more durable as AI commoditizes software. The fragmentation across languages, currencies, and regulatory regimes forces companies to build distribution and localization capabilities that translate directly into global expansion. The friction that European founders complain about is the same friction that builds the muscle for operating across one-way doors.

The Sifted reporting on the Sifted Summit previews the same theme. Panels on competing with big tech for AI talent, Europe's China blindspot, and robotics as Europe's path to building global giants all point to the same underlying argument: the European model of slower, more regulated, more deliberate development is not a handicap in the AI era. It is a different sorting of doors. The ETH Zurich team that built a Swiss-made humanoid robot using locally sourced actuators and batteries is a proof of concept for reshoring robotics supply chains. The robot itself won't compete with industrial giants, but it demonstrates that European supply chains can produce humanoid-grade components — a capability that matters more as geopolitical one-way doors close on China-dependent supply chains.

## The gate is the product

The one-way door framework is not a slow-down argument. It is a sorting argument. Let AI move at full speed through two-way doors — drafts, summaries, first-pass code, research retrieval, form-filling, calendar management. The Warp factory's 35-minute PR cycle, the Every team's agent-delegated errands, the Jev ecosystem's $0.34 evaluation runs that [matched human labels at one-eightieth the cost of Claude](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-two-week-moat) — these are all two-way door operations where speed is pure upside. The cost of being wrong is low and temporary, and the benefit of being fast is real.

The one-way doors need a human hard on the gate. Anything that moves money, ships to a customer, publishes a legal filing, or cannot be reversed. The Centcom lesson is that cutting the verification team from ten to one wasn't efficiency. It was removing the friction that catches catastrophic errors. The chatbot near-war is what happens when two AI passes replace the analyst who reads the source. The one-way door framework says: put your best people on the irreversible decisions, and let the machines handle the rest. The gate is not a bottleneck. The gate is the product — the thing that separates a system you can trust from a system that gets to a bad idea faster.

The companies building that gate — not the companies building the biggest models — are the ones creating durable positions. Warp's scoring layer. Anthropic's 0 percent autonomous line. The planner-executor split. Europe's regulatory moats. The gate is where trust accumulates, and trust is what markets pay for. The model does the work. The gate owns the consequence. And the consequence, as Minab showed, is the only thing the machine cannot optimize away.
