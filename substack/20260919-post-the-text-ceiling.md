# The text ceiling

*AI spent three years in a chat box. Now it is learning to see, speak, and work in the worlds where text was never enough.*

Afterimage, a small creative studio, fed GPT-6 Astra footage from a real commercial shoot and asked it to do VFX. Not generate images from a prompt — actually composite 3D objects into handheld iPhone video, track motion, mask layers, and drive Nuke, the professional compositing software, through its Python scripting API. The studio's editors never touched Nuke themselves. Astra handled rigid geometry well: buildings, wineglasses, a Snoopy float composited into shaky phone footage. Organic forms defeated it; eyes on fingertips and cows in a field came out wrong in ways that no prompt fix could repair. But the output that worked revealed something the studio had not expected. In a pre-Astra world, they would never have thought to add visual effects to casual phone clips. The combination of ordinary footage and visual maximalism felt genuinely new. The breakthrough was aesthetic, not just technical.

This is one data point in a week that saw AI break through the text window in every direction. Nuance Labs raised $50 million with no product, no traction, and no benchmarks, on the strength of a single argument: the cascaded architecture that powers every AI avatar — speech to text, text to LLM, LLM to audio, audio to face — destroys the thing that makes conversation feel alive. Text preserves words. It throws away hesitation, gaze, rhythm, interruption, the half-second where someone's eyes move before they speak. Three former Apple researchers convinced Lightspeed, NVIDIA, and Define Ventures that the entire AI avatar industry is architecturally broken, and that the fix requires a unified model that predicts speech and movement as one continuous stream. NVIDIA validated the thesis with its own benchmark, then invested.

## The interface reinvented

Google's Gemini 3.8 Live Extended Thinking addressed the same problem from a different angle. Current voice AI suffers from an awkward silence: you speak, the model processes, then responds. Gemini 3.8 Live speaks while it reasons. It acknowledges the user, keeps the conversation moving, and does slower thinking in the background. In a live demo, an insurance claim agent could see a damaged car through a phone camera, talk through the claim, switch to Hindi mid-call, and draw annotations on screen — all in real time, all simultaneously. The demo is open-source on GitHub. Voice AI crossed from turn-taking novelty to something that feels like collaborating with a person who happens to be processing a complex task while talking to you.

The common thread is that text was never the right interface for most of what humans do. The chat box was a convenient starting point — easy to build, easy to evaluate, easy to productize. But conversation is not text. Visual effects are not text. Scientific discovery is not text. Financial advice is not text. For three years, the AI industry compressed every domain into a text prompt and accepted the loss. That compromise is ending.

Inherent, a London-based lab founded by three Google DeepMind alumni, is building AI for scientific discovery — not answering questions about science, but figuring out which questions are worth asking. Its platform, Faraday, beat Anthropic and OpenAI models at independently reproducing published scientific findings without prior knowledge of the results. The company treats its own organizational design as a research experiment: employees wear headsets and narrate their stream of consciousness while working. The office may be redesigned by a Hollywood set designer. A linguist may be hired to figure out how agents should communicate. Matt Clifford, the former UK AI tsar, joined as an adviser. Index Ventures and Radical Ventures led a $50 million seed round. The premise is that building AI for science requires rethinking how the humans around the AI work — the interface between researcher and machine is the product, not just the model.

Anthropic made the same bet from the commercial side. Claude for Financial Advisors is the company's first product built around an entire job title. It ships as a Cowork plugin, not a chatbot — a complete product designed for independent registered investment advisors, distributed by Schwab and BlackRock to more than 16,000 firms. OpenAI launched ChatGPT for Financial Services four days earlier, targeting the same industry. The two companies took opposite approaches: OpenAI paired GPT-6 Astra with premium hosted data from Daloopa, PitchBook, and LSEG, building a data platform. Anthropic built a vertical product on top of Cowork, designed for a specific job and distributed through existing industry channels. Three vertical launches in the same week signal that Anthropic is building complete products for specific professions rather than offering a horizontal chatbot and hoping users find their use case.

## What changes when the output is not text

The specification problem — saying precisely what good looks like — [gets harder when the output is a video, not a paragraph](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/what-good-looks-like). Tulsee Doshi, who runs product for Google's Gemini models, described the practice of taking twenty prompts where a win and a loss are obvious. That exercise works because text outputs are easy to compare. Two paragraphs, side by side, and you can pick the better one. Two VFX shots, side by side, and the judgment requires aesthetic sensibility that no benchmark captures. Afterimage's conclusion that Astra's output "felt genuinely new" is not a metric. It is a creative judgment made by people who have spent years in the field. The specification infrastructure that companies are building — personal benchmarks, eval loops, golden datasets — was designed for text. The companies that figure out how to specify what good looks like in video, voice, and professional judgment will have a head start that text-era benchmarks cannot measure.

The infrastructure underneath is also straining. ByteByteGo's guide to RAG pipelines made the point that similarity does not equal correctness — a cosine score of 0.85 does not mean 85 percent answer probability. Metadata filtering by region, date, and version is what makes retrieved passages eligible evidence. That problem exists in text. It gets worse in multimodal systems. A voice agent retrieving insurance claim procedures needs to match not just the semantic content but the jurisdiction, the policy version, and the filing date. Gemini 3.8 Live's insurance demo worked because the retrieval was scoped. A voice agent that retrieves the wrong version of a claims procedure, reads it aloud confidently, and switches to Hindi mid-sentence is a more dangerous failure mode than a text chatbot that cites the wrong source.

The engineering patterns for changing production systems without breaking them are mature in text-based applications. ByteByteGo's migration guide covered the three core strategies — shadow table swaps, expand-contract, and change data capture — that let teams change a working system while the service keeps running. GitHub built gh-ost after trigger-based migrations deadlocked on billions-of-rows tables. Squarespace migrated from Postgres to CockroachDB with no write downtime using CDC. The four backfill invariants — idempotent, resumable, throttled, primary-key ordered — are the difference between a migration that finishes and one that falls over. These patterns exist because migrations fail. They will need to be reinvented for multimodal systems where the schema includes model weights, embedding indices, and real-time audio pipelines.

## The dispatch problem in new dimensions

[The dispatch problem — organizing work for agents that can do almost anything — has a new dimension when the agents work in non-text media.](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-dispatch-problem) Nous Research's refactoring of Hermes with 1,393 agents and $19,000 in token spend is an operating manual for agent swarms in a text codebase. Plan the work, shard the codebase, dispatch agents in parallel, verify results. The pattern scales because code is text, and text is shardable. A VFX pipeline is not shardable in the same way. A voice conversation is not shardable at all — it is a single continuous stream. The dispatch patterns that work for code refactoring will need different primitives for visual, auditory, and physical workflows.

Every's "Show Us Your Folders" series revealed Kieran Klaassen's workspace architecture — Tuin for goals and records, Erf for dispatching tasks to the right folder, a Mac mini for always-on compute. The principle is that the folder is the agent: everything the AI needs lives in one place, and switching tools does not require migrating context. That architecture works because the context is text files. When the context is video footage, audio streams, and real-time sensor data, the folder metaphor breaks down. The infrastructure for multimodal agent context does not exist yet at the same maturity.

Dan Shipper's argument that companies should burn more tokens — that low token spend is a smoke signal for thinking too small — applies here with a twist. Randy Counsman burned 4.5 billion tokens trying to make a 3D model of his face and got a "very janky" result. The failure revealed an "unruly swarm" of subagents passing growing context back and forth. That kind of expensive failure is how the industry will discover what multimodal agent architectures can and cannot do. The token spend will be higher. The failures will be more visible. And the specification infrastructure to learn from them — personal benchmarks, eval loops — will need to handle outputs that cannot be diffed in a text editor.

Matt Pocock's concept of "memento-driven development" — optimizing a codebase for an agent that wakes up with no memory every session — is a text-era insight with multimodal implications. Software fundamentals have been trying to make codebases understandable to a fresh reader for decades. An agent that starts each session with no memory is the ultimate fresh reader. But a VFX project, a voice conversation, or a scientific experiment generates context that is not text. The memento problem gets harder when the artifact is a video clip, not a source file.

Google's Dream-RSI adds another layer. The technique replays a model's own past runs, keeps the good moves, and achieves up to 162x fewer agent calls. Recursive self-improvement went from existential risk boogeyman to product category in a single week — a Chinese startup raised $50M for self-evolving models, 35 researchers published a five-level RSI roadmap, and AMD posted a job req for a "Recursive Self Improvement" scientist. If the model can improve itself by replaying its own runs, the human specification becomes a starting point. But replaying text runs is tractable. Replaying multimodal runs — video generation attempts, voice conversation sessions, scientific experiments — requires storing and indexing outputs that are orders of magnitude larger than text logs. The self-improving specification infrastructure for multimodal AI has not been built.

OpenAI's overtaking of Anthropic in developer spend — breaking a 133-week Anthropic lead in two weeks — reflects a market that is still optimizing for text. Developers route through OpenRouter because they can switch models without changing code, and price-performance drives routing decisions. GPT-6 Astra shifted the calculus. But the routing layer is text-centric. When the dominant workloads are video generation, voice agents, and professional practice automation, the routing decisions will depend on factors that text benchmarks do not capture. The market that formed around text APIs will reorganize around multimodal interfaces, and the companies that own the specification and evaluation infrastructure for those interfaces will have the same structural advantage that OpenRouter has today in text.

Vercel's reduction of its inbound sales team from 10 people to 1.25 — with 90 percent automation and a 32x return — is a text-era automation success. The AI-SDR promise from 2023 came true in 2026. But the 0.25 person remaining is the one checking the machine. Oversight cost is the hidden budget line. In multimodal domains, oversight is harder. Checking a text email for accuracy is one thing. Checking a voice agent that switched to Hindi mid-call, or a VFX composite that looks "genuinely new" but might have a subtle tracking error, requires expertise that cannot be reduced to a probability score. TypeSafe's Jev model — returning calibrated probabilities instead of prose — works because text outputs can be scored. Scoring a video or a voice conversation is a different problem entirely.

The text window taught the industry how to talk to machines. The machines are now learning to work in the media where text was never enough — video, voice, science, professional practice. The infrastructure for specifying, dispatching, verifying, and improving work in those media is being built now, piece by piece, by studios like Afterimage, labs like Inherent, and companies like Anthropic and Google. The chat box was the training ground. The work that matters happens in the world.
