# The thirty-trillion-dollar cron job

*While Anthropic pitches a $30 trillion future to IPO investors, the engineering work that actually compounds looks remarkably like it always has: cron jobs, memory bandwidth, and test migrations.*

Anthropic's leaked IPO roadshow deck contains a number that deserves a second look. The company pitches a $30 trillion total addressable market — roughly 650 times its current business — atop a slide that originated from a Coatue analysis. The figure is not dishonest in the way TAM numbers seldom are. It has a job: signal to investors that the opportunity is vast enough to justify whatever valuation the bankers dream up. The real tell, when the S-1 lands, will be revenue concentration and unit economics, not the headline number. But the $30 trillion figure reveals something useful about the gap between what AI companies sell to investors and what engineers actually do all day.

That same week, Asana finished migrating 4,000 Enzyme test files to React Testing Library. The work took two weeks and cost roughly $12,000 in API calls. Before AI-assisted migration, the same project was estimated at $6 million and five years of engineering time. Uber migrated 600,000 JUnit tests — 15 million lines of code — from JUnit 4 to JUnit 5 in four months with two engineers. Bun rewrote 530,000 lines from Zig to Rust in two weeks for $165,000. These are not $30 trillion opportunities. They are cron jobs. Tedious, mechanical, previously uneconomical work that nobody wanted to fund because the return on investment never cleared the bar. AI did not make them possible. It made them worth doing.

## The pitch and the plumbing

The contrast matters because it tracks two completely different conversations about AI happening simultaneously. One conversation happens in boardrooms and on IPO roadshows, where the subject is total addressable markets, compute deals, and acquisition prices. Nvidia agreed to buy Hugging Face for $12.9 billion, consolidating a position that spans silicon, model distribution, and developer community. Anthropic struck a $45 billion deal to rent compute capacity from UK-based Nscale. Stripe acquired Clerky, the legal operating system behind $140 billion in startup fundraising — a switching-cost play that gains the attorney distribution channel Atlas could never build alone. Revolut launched a euro stablecoin with only €369 in reserves but 80 million banking customers, outsourcing issuance to Stripe's Bridge while keeping the brand and customer relationship. These are distribution plays, not technology plays. The underlying AI becomes a reason to own the channel, not the product itself.

The other conversation happens in engineering channels and Slack threads, where the subject is memory bandwidth, speculative decoding, and whether your cron job should become a distributed worker pool. ByteByteGo's breakdown of speculative decoding is a good example: GPU compute utilization drops to 20-40 percent during text generation because a 70-billion-parameter model must read roughly 140GB of weights for every token, while the actual arithmetic is trivial. Speculative decoding pairs a small draft model with the target model, generates candidate tokens, and verifies them in a single forward pass — converting wasted compute into 2-3x speedup with provably identical output. This is not a $30 trillion insight. It is a hardware constraint that has existed since the first transformer model, and the fix is old engineering applied to new silicon.

Casey Muratori made a related point on the Pragmatic Engineer podcast. The conventional approach to performance optimization — profile, find hotspots, optimize — finds local minima, not global ones. Great optimizers start from what the hardware can theoretically do and work backward. Learning to read assembly requires maybe 20-30 instructions. The phrase "premature optimization is the root of all evil" has been misused for decades to delay performance work until architectural decisions are locked in and cannot be fixed. None of this is new. But it cuts against the current AI narrative, where the assumption is that throwing more compute at a problem is cheaper than understanding the compute you already have.

## What the migrations actually teach

The migration stories from Asana, Airbnb, Uber, and Bun share a structure that the IPO deck does not capture. AI handled the mechanical transformation — rewriting test assertions, converting API calls, translating one syntax into another. Engineers handled the planning, the verification loops, and the orchestration. At Airbnb, 75 percent of 3,500 Enzyme files migrated in four hours once the retry loops were built. The remaining 25 percent required human judgment about edge cases the model could not infer. The engineers did not disappear. They moved up the stack, from writing the migration to designing the system that executes it.

This distinction — between work that can be automated and work that requires judgment — showed up across the week's other stories. Ramp rejected off-the-shelf coding agents and built Inspect, an in-house tool running on remote sandboxes with internal data access, because third-party harnesses could not support parallel agents, lacked frontend tooling, and did not offer remote development environments. Block built Goose. Stripe built Minions. Shopify built River. The pattern is not that these companies are building AI tools. It is that they are encoding their own engineering workflows into systems that off-the-shelf vendors cannot match, because the vendors do not know their codebase, their data model, or their deployment constraints.

Every is doing something similar with expertise. KateBench, a copyediting agent trained on 30,000 historical edits, runs a first pass before human review, with tracked changes feeding back to self-improve. The next step is cloning expertise company-wide: UX feedback, logistical planning, social positioning. At Walleye Capital, a $10 billion hedge fund, AI use is mandatory for all 400 employees. The CEO compares refusing AI to refusing the internet in 1995. Linas Beliūnas demonstrated the same principle with consulting frameworks: twelve Claude Skills built on Minto's Pyramid Principle, Playing to Win, and the CIA's Analysis of Competing Hypotheses lifted Fable 5 from 38 percent to 97 percent on a partner-level checklist. The $37 billion consulting industry sells the method, not the person — most engagement work is done by associates with under two years of experience. The constraint was never access to the frameworks, which are all published. It was the two years of internalization under partner supervision. Encoding those frameworks as executable skills compresses that timeline.

The thread connecting these examples is not AI capability. It is the encoding of human judgment into repeatable, auditable systems that compound over time. Every's overhaul of its ChatGPT and OpenClaw guides reflects the same maturation: ChatGPT now has a dedicated Work mode for knowledge tasks, Projects for organizing work, and Scheduled Tasks for automation. The OpenClaw guide candidly acknowledges that maintenance burden grows with team size — credentials expire, integrations break, instructions go stale — driving a shift toward shared company agents over individual ones. A stronger model cannot fix expired credentials or broken integrations. Human maintenance is required, and the companies building shared agent infrastructure are the ones taking that cost seriously.

## The measurement problem

Here is where the gap between the $30 trillion pitch and the engineering reality gets uncomfortable. Companies spending $100 million a year on AI models often have no idea whether those models save employees time or produce trustworthy work. Public benchmarks measure general capability, not performance on the specific tasks a company needs. Every's KateBench shows 85-90 percent acceptance on copyediting, but the number hides measurement problems: a 40-suggestion cap, non-determinism, and residual editor work that the acceptance rate does not capture. The practical prescription is concrete: pick one recurring job, write five failure modes with gold-standard examples, and run models against those cases. A leaderboard will tell you less than five well-chosen test cases.

The measurement gap extends to security. Researchers from MATS, ELLIS Institute Tübingen, and Max Planck demonstrated that encrypted reasoning blocks from frontier AI models can be replayed into cheaper models in the same family to extract hidden chain-of-thought in plaintext. Providers encrypt reasoning traces for competitive and safety reasons. The attack exploits shared family-level parameters — the cheaper model accepts, decrypts, and continues from the encrypted block — and proposed fixes are limited because the client must still decrypt reasoning to display summaries. This is not a hypothetical. Real-world cases of inadvertent exposure were found in published session logs. The security model assumes the encryption boundary holds. It does not.

Fable 5's market performance reinforces the point. Anthropic's most expensive model flatlined at roughly 11 percent of business spending two months after launch, per Ramp data tracking 70,000 companies. Anthropic's own cheaper Opus 5 has passed Fable 5 in usage. An Accel partner put it bluntly: most people do not need to operate at the frontier, and the era when they thought they did "was not a durable era." The reflex of defaulting to the newest, most expensive model has broken. Companies are routing work to cheaper models, open-weight models, and local hardware — not because they are cheaper for their own sake, but because the frontier model does not justify its cost for most tasks. Apple's $899 Mac mini, built to run open-weight LLMs locally with up to 512GB of unified memory, is a consumer appliance that runs what used to require a data center. Open-weight models processed 29 percent of tokens through Vercel's AI Gateway in June, accounting for less than 4 percent of spend.

## The work that compounds

The IPO will price Anthropic at whatever the market bears. The $30 trillion TAM will be forgotten by the time the S-1 reveals revenue concentration and margins. What will persist is the engineering work that happened this week — the migrations that cleared backlogs, the speculative decoding implementations that cut inference costs, the internal evals that told companies whether their AI spend was producing value. The agent infrastructure layer is consolidating: AgentSky launched a unified API putting Claude Code, Codex, and Hermes behind one endpoint; Monid offers an "OpenRouter for agent tools" with 1,700-plus tools. The infrastructure is becoming standardized, which means the differentiation moves elsewhere — into the workflows, the evals, and the judgment that companies encode around the models. As [the two-week migration](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-two-week-migration) showed, the companies clearing their engineering backlogs are not waiting for the next model release. They are building the plumbing.

Stanley Druckenmiller, the billionaire investor, admitted he writes everything using AI now. "For the same reason I use a calculator when I do math problems," he told the Wall Street Journal, which defended him. The Financial Times, by contrast, banned its columnists from using AI. The split is instructive. The WSJ is comfortable with the calculator analogy because the calculator did not replace the mathematician — it replaced the arithmetic. The FT is uncomfortable because, for writers, language is the work, not the scaffolding around the work. [The calculator defense](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-calculator-defense) captured this tension: language is being commoditized and judgment is not. Authorship is migrating from producing words to owning the thought. The companies that understand this difference — and build their internal tools, evals, and workflows around it — are the ones that will compound. The ones that buy the $30 trillion pitch without building the plumbing will discover that the pitch and the product are different things.
