# The checking floor

*When the model can do the work, the bottleneck is proving the work is right — and the companies building that proof are creating the next moat.*

Vercel reduced its inbound sales team from ten people to 1.25. Ninety percent automation, a 32x return, and the entire annual infrastructure bill in the single-digit thousands. The AI-SDR promise from 2023 — the one every venture capitalist rolled their eyes at — quietly started working. But the number that tells the real story is not the 1.25. It is the 0.25. The fraction of a person who remains is the one checking the machine. That quarter-person is Vercel's checking floor: the minimum human oversight required to trust automated output. Glean surveyed 6,000 workers and found that automation saves 11 hours per week, but 6.5 of those hours go back into checking, correcting, and re-feeding the machine. Net saving: under half the headline. String ten agent steps together at 95 percent reliability each, and the chain finishes clean only 60 percent of the time. The checking floor is not a bug. It is the cost of delegating to systems that are probably right.

The moat has moved. For three years, the competitive question was who had the best model. Now the model is good enough, and the question is who can prove it. TypeSafe's Jev does not generate prose — it returns calibrated probabilities, structured answers that code can branch on directly. Mike Taylor at Every tested it across 11 experiments and found it could make 1,709 judgments for less than a cent. At a quarter-cent per 777 judgments, running checks multiple times during a task costs nothing. The alternative, as Taylor noted, is not checking at all. CO/AI framed it bluntly: stop shopping for a bigger brain. If you cannot write the score for "correct," you are not ready to automate the task. The leverage moved from the model to the checker.

## The verifier as product

The checking floor looks different across domains, and the differences reveal where verification is easy, where it is hard, and where it is genuinely unsolved. In text, the problem is tractable. Jev returns probabilities. ByteByteGo's guide to LLM evaluation laid out the architecture: golden datasets, automated metrics, LLM-as-a-judge, human review, production monitoring. An LLM is healthy if it consistently generates useful results across accuracy, safety, speed, reliability, and cost — not just if it returns a correct answer once. The evaluation stack is a private regulatory framework. No external body mandates it. Companies build it because the cost of not building it is worse. [The sovereigns and the tenants](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-sovereigns-and-the-tenants) traced how companies with proprietary data and an honest eval loop are building in-house AI while everyone else rents. The eval loop is the dividing line. The checking floor is its economic expression.

In code, the verification problem has a different shape. Nous Research refactored Hermes — its own AI agent platform — using 1,393 parallel agents and approximately $19,000 in token spend. The pattern is plan, shard, dispatch, verify. The first three steps are solved. The fourth — verification — is where the work lives. [The dispatch problem](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-dispatch-problem) covered the organizational cost: without a checking infrastructure, 1,393 agents produce 1,393 unverified changes. Matt Pocock, on The Pragmatic Engineer, arrived at the same conclusion from a different direction. His concept of "memento-driven development" — optimizing a codebase for an agent that wakes up with no memory every session — is a verification strategy. The agent cannot rely on remembered context. The codebase itself has to be legible enough that a fresh reader can verify what is happening. Pocock asks agents to produce proof that their code works, with or without TDD. The proof is the verification layer. The codebase is the specification.

In financial advice, Anthropic built the checking into the product. Claude for Financial Advisors is not a chatbot for wealth managers — it is a complete product built around a single job title, distributed by Schwab and BlackRock to more than 16,000 independent registered investment advisors. OpenAI launched ChatGPT for Financial Services four days earlier, targeting the same industry with a different architecture: premium hosted data from Daloopa, PitchBook, and LSEG. Both products bake verification into the workflow rather than leaving it to the user. The advisor does not run a separate eval loop. The product is the eval loop. The checking floor is embedded in the distribution channel.

## Where the floor resists

The checking floor gets harder when the output is not text or code. Afterimage, a small creative studio, fed GPT-6 Astra footage from a real commercial shoot and asked it to do VFX — compositing 3D objects into handheld iPhone video, tracking motion, masking layers, and driving Nuke through its Python API. Astra handled rigid geometry well. Organic forms defeated it: eyes on fingertips looked tacked on, cows in a field did not integrate. The verdict — that the output "felt genuinely new" — is not a metric. It is a creative judgment made by people who have spent years in the field. [What good looks like](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/what-good-looks-like) described Tulsee Doshi's practice of taking twenty prompts where a win and a loss are obvious. That exercise works because text outputs can be compared side by side. Two VFX shots side by side require aesthetic sensibility that no benchmark captures. The checking floor for creative work is not zero. It is a person with taste.

Voice AI has the same problem in real time. Gemini 3.8 Live Extended Thinking speaks while it reasons — acknowledging the user, keeping the conversation moving, and doing slower thinking in the background. In a live demo, an insurance claim agent could see a damaged car through a phone camera, talk through the claim, switch to Hindi mid-call, and draw annotations on screen. The verification question for a voice agent is not whether the text output is correct. It is whether the conversation, in real time, handled the jurisdiction, the policy version, and the filing date correctly while switching languages. ByteByteGo's RAG pipeline guide made the point that similarity does not equal correctness — a cosine score of 0.85 does not mean 85 percent answer probability. Metadata filtering by region, date, and version is what makes retrieved passages eligible evidence. A voice agent that retrieves the wrong version of a claims procedure, reads it aloud confidently, and switches to Hindi mid-sentence is a more dangerous failure mode than a text chatbot that cites the wrong source. The checking floor for voice is real-time, continuous, and much harder to instrument.

The hardest verification domain is the one Inherent is tackling. The London-based lab, founded by three Google DeepMind alumni, is building AI for scientific discovery — not answering questions about science, but reproducing published scientific findings without prior knowledge of the results. Its platform, Faraday, reportedly beat Anthropic and OpenAI models at this task. But "beat" here means something specific: the agent reproduced findings already validated by the original researchers. The verification problem for novel scientific discovery — claims no one has confirmed — is categorically different. You cannot run a golden dataset against a result that does not exist yet. The checking floor for scientific discovery is the peer review process, and no one has automated that.

## The recursive hope and its ceiling

Google's Dream-RSI offers a way to push the checking floor down. The technique replays a model's own past runs, keeps the good moves, and achieves up to 162x fewer agent calls. Recursive self-improvement went from existential risk boogeyman to product category in a single week — a Chinese startup raised $50 million for self-evolving models, 35 researchers published a five-level RSI roadmap, and AMD posted a job req for a "Recursive Self Improvement" scientist. The model verifies itself by replaying its own experience and keeping what worked. But self-verification only works when the model can distinguish good moves from bad ones. In text and code, that distinction is increasingly automatable. In VFX, voice, and science, the distinction requires external judgment. Dream-RSI can optimize what it can score. It cannot score what no one has defined.

Dan Shipper's argument that companies should burn more tokens — that low token spend is a smoke signal for thinking too small — applies to the verification economy in a specific way. Randy Counsman burned 4.5 billion tokens trying to make a 3D model of his face and got a "very janky" result. The failure revealed an "unruly swarm" of subagents passing growing context back and forth. That failure was a verification exercise: spending tokens to discover what the system could not do, then describing the failure precisely enough to fix it. Every is building infrastructure for spending transparency — personal benchmarks for every employee, AI spending P&L statements, model routing strategies. The infrastructure is not for the model. It is for the checker. The person who can see the failure and name it is the person who pushes the checking floor down.

The market is already organizing around this shift. OpenAI overtook Anthropic in developer spend on OpenRouter for the first time in 133 weeks, breaking a lead that had held since February 2024. The reversal took two weeks. Developers route through OpenRouter because they can switch models without changing code, and price-performance drives routing decisions. The routing layer is the verification layer in disguise: developers choose the model that delivers the best trade-off between capability and cost, and the choice is made by checking results, not trusting brands. When the dominant workloads shift from text to multimodal, the routing decisions will depend on factors that text benchmarks do not capture. The companies that own the specification and evaluation infrastructure for those interfaces will have the same structural advantage that OpenRouter has today in text.

The checking floor is not going to zero. Glean's data — 6.5 of 11 saved hours going back to oversight — suggests that even with perfect automation, more than half the time saved gets reinvested in verification. Jev and tools like it push the floor down by turning checking from a human cost into an infrastructure cost. Dream-RSI pushes it further by letting the model replay its own experience. But the floor exists, and the companies that pretend it does not are the ones Gartner predicts will be dead by 2027. [The accountability gap](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-accountability-gap) asked who answers when AI acts. The checking floor is the operational answer: the person or system that verifies the output answers for it. The companies building that verification infrastructure — not the companies building the biggest models — are the ones creating the next moat. The model does the work. The checker owns the trust. And trust, not capability, is what markets pay for.
