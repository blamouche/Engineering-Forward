# The good enough threshold

*Standard models crossed a competence line this month, and the frontier's premium started shrinking at a rate that should worry anyone betting on peak intelligence as a business.*

Alex Kantrowitz published a confession. He had dismissed Meta's Muse personal agent as another underpowered chatbot wrapped in marketing. He was wrong. Muse works. It handles real tasks — shopping, scheduling, research — and it does so without frontier intelligence. Meta built a production-grade agent on standard models, the tier below the frontier, and the result is good enough that Muse spent time at number one on the App Store. The admission matters less than the data behind it. Ramp's corporate spend tracking shows that the frontier share of AI dollars dropped from 53 percent in early August to 45 percent in early September. Eight percentage points in one month. The arrow points in one direction, and it is not the one that justifies hundred-billion-dollar IPO valuations.

The economics are blunt. GPT-6 Astra costs $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output. Muse Spark costs $1.25 and $4.25. The 8-to-12x gap is not a temporary promotion. It is the structural difference between a frontier model that requires massive training compute and a standard model that trades a few benchmark points for an order-of-magnitude cost reduction. Companies have stopped what the industry calls tokenmaxxing — defaulting to the most expensive model for every request — and adopted model routers that direct spend to cheaper models unless the task genuinely demands frontier intelligence. [The two-week moat](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-two-week-moat) described how 133 weeks of developer loyalty evaporated in 14 days when OpenAI cut prices. The current shift is different. It is not about switching between frontier models. It is about switching away from the frontier entirely for most work.

## What good enough enables

The standard-model threshold enables a stack that was economically impractical at frontier prices. Jev, TypeSafe's classification model, runs at 4.2 cents per million input tokens with free output. It does not generate text. It makes decisions — yes or no, urgent or not, spam or not, safe or risky — and returns calibrated probabilities. ByteByteGo's EP227 lays out nine use cases where Jev replaces LLM calls: model routing, guardrails, gating tool calls, triage, reranking, evals, bulk labeling, real-time decisions, and confidence gates. The operating principle is the 10-second rule: if a judgment call would take a human less than ten seconds, a decision model should handle it at scale. Every's deep dive on Jev walks through the practical decomposition — breaking "is this email urgent?" into discrete checks the model evaluates separately and you combine in code.

The cost differential compounds. At 4.2 cents per million tokens, Jev is roughly 600 times cheaper than GPT-6 Astra for judgment calls. Mike Taylor's email board experiment and Jack Cheng's intent-based canvas demo — where Jev interprets voice and hand gestures on a tldraw canvas in real time, interactions that would take an LLM one to two seconds — both follow the same pattern. The human defines the decision structure. The model executes it at a cost that makes checking viable at volumes that were previously unthinkable. [The checking floor](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-checking-floor) described verification as the hidden cost of delegation. The checking floor just got 600 times cheaper to stand on.

ByteByteGo's guide to model customization fills in the next layer. The ladder runs from prompting through few-shot examples, retrieval-augmented generation, supervised fine-tuning, and parameter-efficient techniques like LoRA and QLoRA. The architectural insight is that LoRA keeps the base model frozen and trains small adapters — compact matrices that learn adjustments without touching the original weights. One base model, many specializations. QLoRA goes further, storing base weights in 4-bit form while keeping adapters at higher precision, making it possible to customize a larger model within a fixed memory budget. The practical guidance reflects hard-won experience: establish a prompt-based baseline first, prepare clean train and validation sets, watch for overfitting, and evaluate the combined model as a whole because an adapter can improve one behavior while weakening another even though the original weights remain untouched. Specialization used to require custom training runs and PhD-level expertise. It is now a configuration exercise with open-source tooling.

## The frontier's narrowing claim

If standard models handle production agents and decision models handle judgment calls and fine-tuning adapts base models to specific tasks, the frontier's claim narrows to the set of problems that genuinely require maximum intelligence. Boris Cherny's formal verification work with Opus 5.5 and Lean — 16 bug-fix PRs for the Claude Agent SDK, produced by a model guided by a proof system — is an example of what the frontier is for. Formal verification, concurrency analysis with TLA+, and problems that require deep reasoning across complex contexts are where the premium still holds. The question for OpenAI and Anthropic heading into IPOs is how large that set is, and whether it is growing or shrinking as standard models and specialized adapters improve.

Index Ventures partner Georgia Stevenson's observation that "moat" has vanished from the firm's investment committee memos is the capital-side version of the same squeeze. If data and distribution are interchangeable — because models can access any API and retrieval works across any corpus — defensibility moves to things that are harder to manufacture: talent, speed, brand, network effects. [The adapter economy](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-adapter-economy) described how the layers that shape intelligence to a specific context are where value compounds. The death of the moat concept at a major VC firm confirms the pattern from the investment side. The companies that attracted talent capable of working at Anthropic but chose lower pay and higher risk are the signal that cuts through AI-generated pitch homogeneity. Everything else — data access, distribution channels, model access — is increasingly available to anyone.

Anthropic's charm offensive across European capitals is the frontier lab's response to the squeeze. At the third Claude Founder House in London, the company's head of EMEA startups told 250 founders that companies like Legora and Wordsmith thrive alongside Anthropic's own vertical tools. The sincerity matters less than the strategy: Anthropic needs founder mindshare and developer loyalty in Europe because the model alone is no longer enough to retain them. The Claude Founder House series is a direct investment in ecosystem relationships — the kind of moat that does not show up in benchmark scores but shows up in developer retention when a cheaper alternative appears.

## The data counterpressure

The frontier's narrowing claim creates a paradox. If model capability is no longer the primary differentiator, the differentiator moves upstream to the data that trains the model. CO/AI reported that more than 50 tons of used books have been shipped from Japan to the United States, likely for AI training. Used bookstores across Japan received machine-timed bulk orders for philosophy, law, medicine, and Edo-era history, all routed to a single warehouse in Okayama. The trade follows Anthropic's 2025 playbook: buy millions of used books, cut the bindings, scan them, discard the physical copies. A federal judge ruled it fair use. The internet has been scraped. What remains is physical archives.

The reality TV math is where the data frontier gets tangible. Warner Bros. Discovery's cable networks produce 3,000-plus hours of programming per year. At a 100:1 shooting ratio, that is 300,000 hours annually of raw human behavior footage. A decade of archives equals millions of hours of unscripted interaction. YouTubers already sell unpublished footage for $1 to $4 per minute. The reality TV tape, now owned by Paramount carrying roughly $80 billion in debt from its $111 billion WBD acquisition, is orders of magnitude larger. Debt that size goes hunting for assets nobody has priced.

Meta's glasses are the frontier beyond archives. Muse is coming to Ray-Ban glasses with live video, AI on by default, voice recordings kept for up to a year, and no opt-out from training. First-person video, captured continuously in every setting, is the purest training data trove yet — and the least consented. Everyone in the room never signed a release. The training data frontier is moving from the public internet to private archives to continuous capture, and the regulatory framework for each stage lags further behind the last. [What intelligence consumes](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/what-intelligence-consumes) traced the physical costs of AI — silicon, paper, server racks. The data frontier is the same pattern at the input side: intelligence needs fuel, and the easy fuel is gone.

## The skill shift

The human side of the good enough threshold is where the least progress has been made. Lenny Rachitsky's field notes from inside an AI-native company — a millennial raised in pre-AI tech navigating a workplace where AI augmentation is the default — describe a disorientation that cheaper models do not solve. The CPU shortage is an engineering problem. The data lifecycle is an architecture problem. The transition from pre-AI to AI-native work culture is a human problem, and it does not solve itself with better benchmarks.

Maggie Appleton's observations from GitHub Next name the friction precisely. She starts projects in a physical notebook because the idea is still there the next day. With Claude Code, dozens of prompts later, the original intent becomes hard to recover. AI tools optimize for iteration speed at the cost of idea persistence. Her concept of capability gaslighting — when frontier models convince you they are experts, then fail the same task the next day — identifies the trust problem that makes the good enough threshold dangerous. Standard models that are good enough most of the time and wrong in unpredictable ways may be harder to manage than frontier models that are excellent most of the time and wrong in predictable ways. The manager who learns to trust a model that is usually right and occasionally catastrophic is building a risk profile that no benchmark captures.

The skills that matter when good enough is the floor are not the skills that mattered when the frontier was the ceiling. Evals went mainstream — nearly half of product manager job openings that Lenny Rachitsky shared required eval-writing experience, and Sentry went from near-zero eval runs to 1,800 per month. Microsoft runs 16,000 internal evals. The differentiator is no longer which model you use but whether you can define what correct means for your specific context. Linas's playbook for Claude Code Projects reframes AI-assisted development as engagement management — scoping work, splitting threads, setting review gates, managing budgets. The firm model maps the coordinator to an engagement lead, threads to workstreams, shared memory to institutional knowledge. The people who set up projects this way get dramatically better results. The people who paste in a vague goal get merge conflicts and pull requests that do not fit together.

The good enough threshold does not eliminate the frontier. It redefines the frontier's role. The frontier handles the problems that standard models cannot — formal verification, deep reasoning, novel problem-solving. The standard tier handles production agents. The decision layer handles judgment calls at 600x lower cost. Fine-tuning adapts base models to specific tasks. Evals define what correct means for each context. The management layer governs the whole stack. The frontier is no longer the default. It is a specialized tool for specialized work, and the companies that built their valuations on being the default are the ones that need to answer the question every investor is now asking: what percentage of AI spending is frontier-required, and is that percentage growing or shrinking?
