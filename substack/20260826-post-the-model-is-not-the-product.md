# The model is not the product

*When the frontier model flatlines at 11 percent of spending, the advantage lives in what you build around it — encoded methods, internal evals, and custom tooling.*

Fable 5, Anthropic's most expensive model, has flatlined at about 11 percent of what companies spend on Anthropic's tools. Two months after launch, the number is not climbing. An Accel partner with a billion dollars riding on Anthropic told the Financial Times that most people do not need to operate at the frontier, and the era when they thought they did "was not a durable era." Anthropic's own cheaper Opus 5 has already passed Fable 5 in usage. Nvidia is telling customers server prices will rise roughly 15 percent next year. The shovel gets more expensive while the gold it digs gets cheaper.

The number is striking, but the pattern beneath it is more interesting. For two years, the reflex was automatic: a new frontier model drops, everybody defaults to it. That reflex broke. And the break exposes something the market has been circling around without naming directly: the model was never the product. The product is the methodology encoded around the model, the internal evaluation that tells you whether the model works for your specific job, and the custom tooling that integrates the model into your actual workflow. The companies that understood this are pulling ahead. The ones still shopping for the best model on a leaderboard are buying raw material and calling it a finished product.

## The method is the moat

Ramp's decision to build its own coding agent, Inspect, rather than buy off-the-shelf is the clearest case study. Engineers and designers at the fintech company found third-party coding harnesses limiting. They wanted to run many agents in parallel, which local machines cannot support. They needed remote development environments with access to internal data sources. They wanted better frontend tooling. So they built Inspect: a coding agent running on remote sandboxes with Postgres, Redis, RabbitMQ, and Temporal available inside each one, spinning up in five seconds through pre-warming and snapshot restoration. More than 150 people at Ramp have contributed to it. All sessions are public, with no opt-outs allowed.

The point is not that Ramp built something better than Codex or Claude Code. The point is that the advantage is not in the model driving the agent — it is in the sandbox architecture, the internal integrations, the cultural choice to make every session collaborative. Block built Goose. Stripe built Minions. Shopify built River. The common thread across all of them is that non-AI-frontier companies can build more efficient tooling than frontier labs offer when the tooling is tightly integrated with their specific systems and workflows. [The companies that know what not to do](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-companies-that-know-what-not-to-do) framed this as strategic discipline — knowing what to refuse. The Ramp story adds a corollary: knowing what to build yourself is the other half of the same equation.

The same pattern appears in a completely different domain. Linas Beliunas built twelve Claude Skills based on published consulting frameworks — Minto's Pyramid Principle, Lafley and Martin's Playing to Win, Kaplan and Norton's Balanced Scorecard, Heuer's CIA handbook on competing hypotheses. Companies paid McKinsey, BCG, and Bain about $37 billion last year for advice, and most engagement work was done by associates with less than two years of experience. The client was buying the method, not the person. The method was never secret — the constraint was the two years it takes to internalize it under partner supervision. Encode that method as executable procedures in a Claude Skill, and the model jumps from 38-67 percent to 95-97 percent on a partner-level checklist.

The frontier model did not get smarter. The methodology did not become more sophisticated. What changed is the encoding: steps in order, a checkpoint at each one, the tests a partner applies, a worked example. The model was always capable of running that procedure. It just needed someone to write it down in a form it could execute. The skill is not in the model. It is in the specification.

## Knowing whether it works

If the method is the moat, the eval is the audit. Katie Parrott's reporting in Every surfaces a problem that executives are encountering with increasing frequency: companies spending $100 million a year on AI models know what they pay and where models sit on leaderboards, but often do not know whether the models save employees time or produce work people trust without rechecking. Mercor CEO Brendan Foody says he meets executives in exactly this position. Box CEO Aaron Levie echoed the point: enterprises will not be able to go just on vibes.

Public benchmarks can tell you one model is generally more capable than another. They cannot tell you whether it caught the clause your lawyers care about, preserved your house style, or spared an employee another round of checking. Every built KateBench internally — an AI copyeditor trained on roughly 30,000 past edits by editor-in-chief Kate Lee, tracking which suggestions editors accept or reject. The 85-90 percent acceptance rate hides measurement problems: a 40-suggestion cap that discards good edits on long essays, non-deterministic output that makes scores fluctuate without real changes. The practical prescription is concrete: choose one recurring job, write down five ways the current model gets it wrong with one gold-standard example each, and run models against those cases. That is the beginning of an eval, and it tells you more than another afternoon comparing leaderboards.

The deeper insight is that diminishing returns on intelligence may be real for many tasks, but companies building evals that capture residual work — the work a human still has to do after the agent completes — may find that progress has not slowed. The measurements were missing it. This connects to [the true price of intelligence](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-true-price-of-intelligence-is-showing) and the argument that token spend requires capital-grade discipline. The eval is the accounting system that makes that discipline possible. Without it, you are spending blind.

ByteByteGo's analysis with Sonar CTO Andrea Malagodi adds a structural dimension. Google's DORA research found delivery stability dipped as teams adopted more AI. A METR controlled trial showed AI-assisted tasks took 19 percent longer despite developers believing AI made them faster. A study across more than 100 models found AI-generated code introduced a known security flaw in roughly 45 percent of cases. The verification pipeline — type checkers, linters, unit tests, human review, production monitoring — remains structurally the same but must adapt in capacity. Sonar's three-loop model is becoming the industry consensus: an agentic loop where agents self-correct within a sandbox, a CI verification loop with multi-layered quality gates, and a code maintenance loop that continuously remediates technical debt. The subtle risk: when both the code writer and the code reviewer are similar models, they share the same blind spots. Independent verification — deterministic tools, human judgment, and models from different training lineages — becomes more important, not less.

## What stays yours

The research on reasoning trace extraction sharpens the question of what is actually proprietary. Researchers from MATS Research, the ELLIS Institute Tübingen, and the Max Planck Institute demonstrated that encrypted reasoning blocks from frontier AI models can be replayed into cheaper models in the same family to extract hidden reasoning traces in plaintext. The attack exploits compatibility between models in the same family: the cheaper model accepts the encrypted block, decrypts it using shared family parameters, and uses it as a prefix to continue generating text that reveals the original reasoning. Four attack vectors were identified, and published session logs contained real-world cases where encrypted reasoning was inadvertently exposed.

The implication is stark. Providers withhold reasoning traces for competitive and safety reasons — the traces reveal problem-solving strategies, could enable distillation into competitors, and may contain information about how models circumvent safety guardrails. If the encrypted reasoning is extractable, the model's internal process is not a durable secret. What remains proprietary is not what the model thinks but what the company built around it: the sandbox architecture, the encoded methodology, the internal evals, the integration layer. The model's intelligence is rented. The workflow is owned.

This is the thread that connects Hugging Face's reported $13 billion sale talks to the rest of the picture. Hugging Face has become the de facto repository for open-source AI models, hosting hundreds of thousands of models from labs and individual researchers. The strategic value is not in any single model but in the distribution layer — the plumbing that determines which models reach production. [AI's third act](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/ais-third-act-is-about-who-owns-the-plumbing) argued that the operational layer matters more than the model layer. A $13 billion valuation for a model-agnostic distribution platform confirms the thesis from the market side. The question for every startup and enterprise that has built on the Hub's open ecosystem is whether a sale preserves neutrality or turns the platform into a walled garden tied to one parent's strategy.

The broader market signals point in the same direction. Together Compute's DeepSWE benchmark shows GLM-5.3 solving 87.6 percent of tasks for approximately $16, compared to Fable 5's 69.7 percent for $21.63. The Financial Times reported the same day that Anthropic's most powerful model is losing ground to cheaper alternatives. Grok 4.6 finished alongside GPT-5.6 Sol at the top of blind evaluations run by Claire Vo, ahead of both Sonnet 5 and Opus 5. The model layer is commoditizing in real time. The frontier has more credible competitors than at any point in the last two years, and the price-performance gap between the most expensive option and the best option is widening.

Nvidia's $6 billion deal to license Poolside's model factory without acquiring the company follows the same logic. Nvidia makes GPUs. It has no business running a model lab. The six billion bought access, not ownership. Broadcom parked up to $100 billion in off-balance-sheet financing through Apollo and Blackstone to put chips in Anthropic's hands. Harvey built a legal model by taking a Chinese open-weight base and post-training it on law. The discipline across all four deals is the same: know what you own, know what you rent, and do not confuse the two.

Every's Thesis Statements project asked 100 AI leaders to make specific, contestable predictions about what human work looks like when execution is cheap. Linear's Karri Saarinen predicted that AI's biggest problem will be design, not intelligence. Ness Labs' Anne-Laure Le Cunff predicted that answers will become abundant and questions will become the hard part. Granola's Chris Pedregal predicted that some hard problems will simply solve themselves. The predictions share a structural assumption: execution remains cheap, intelligence remains abundant. But the more interesting question is not what humans do when execution is cheap. It is what companies build when the model is not the product. The teams encoding their own methodologies, building internal evals, and integrating agents into proprietary workflows are the ones whose advantage survives the commoditization of the model layer. The ones still shopping for the best model on a leaderboard are buying flour and calling it bread.
