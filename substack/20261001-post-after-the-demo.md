# After the demo

*The week's stories were not about what agents can do but about what people, companies, and banks do because agents exist.*

Sam Altman wakes up and talks to his Dot. The agent — he calls it Dotty — has been running overnight, triaging issues, and when Altman opens his phone, it tells him what needs attention and what can wait. He leaves voice notes with feature ideas before drifting off and wakes up to find the feature built, plus the next seven steps completed. He uses only Ultrafast mode, citing Bret Victor's principle that creators need an instant connection to what they are making. In a conversation with Dan Shipper at DevDay 2026, Altman called the agent "a significant improvement to my life."

This is not a demo. The CEO of OpenAI, the company building the agent platform, has restructured his morning around a product his company shipped weeks ago. Shipper has his own Dot, named Boo, which tracks commitments across apps and has become his default way to use ChatGPT despite bugs. [The operating system bet](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-operating-system-bet) described OpenAI's DevDay as a platform play — ChatGPT as the operating system for work. The platform is now the CEO's daily routine. The distance between launch and habit was measured in days.

## The plumbing and the craft

DoorDash shipped something less photogenic but more consequential than any DevDay announcement: an Agent Gateway that processes millions of tool calls per week across 200 registered MCP servers and 30 agents. The gateway separates three concerns that were previously tangled — access (identity and permissions), tool-surface curation (which tools an agent sees), and operations (monitoring, rate limits, costs). Credential injection keeps raw downstream credentials — vendor keys, OAuth refresh tokens — out of agents entirely. Discovery and execution are separate: a tool appearing in a discovery response does not authorize its invocation. The architecture reads like the access-control systems that enterprises built for human employees, because that is exactly what it is.

The gateway exists because DoorDash reached a scale where ad-hoc agent tool access stopped working. When 30 agents and thousands of employees are making tool calls against 200 MCP servers, the question of who can use what — and who is accountable when something breaks — requires infrastructure, not configuration. The four credential arrangements map to real organizational patterns: vendor keys that agents should never see, user-granted access that requires consent, team automation with short-lived credentials. DoorDash built the access-control layer for agents that enterprises built for humans decades ago. The difference is that agents move faster, make more calls, and cannot be trained on a security policy in a quarterly workshop.

Peter Mattis, co-founder and CTO of Cockroach Labs and the original creator of GIMP, said something in his Pragmatic Engineer interview that lands differently coming from someone who has spent three decades writing systems software. He predicts that engineers will stop reviewing code. Not stop writing it — stop reviewing it. "The agents are getting better; you're having to give less and less scrutiny. We're materially going to stop looking at the code in the same way we don't look at assembly anymore." Mattis has been back in the code himself, brought out of management by AI assistance. He describes the flow as "maybe a bit less intense, but you're managing more things cognitively — like a college professor with a whole swarm of research assistants."

The assembly analogy is precise. Engineers stopped reading assembly not because assembly disappeared but because compilers got good enough that the higher-level representation became the review surface. Mattis is predicting the same transition for source code: agents write it, agents check it, and the human review moves to a higher level of abstraction — the spec, the architecture, the intent. Non-engineers at Cockroach Labs built roughly 1,000 internal apps in a couple of months using an internal platform that Mattis compares to "internal Lovable." OpenAI observed the same pattern: non-engineers moved token spend from ChatGPT to Codex in four months. The barrier to building software is no longer engineering knowledge. It is knowing what to build.

ByteByteGo's taxonomy of LLM hallucination adds the technical constraint to Mattis's prediction. Factual errors contradict reality. Faithfulness errors contradict supplied evidence. Fabrication invents sources. The categories overlap, and conflating them leads to misplaced confidence. The critical insight is that an explanation is not proof: a model can produce a plausible chain of reasoning that justifies a wrong answer, making the error harder to catch. Verification must be structurally separate from generation, using different evidence or a different model. If agents write code and agents review code, the checking layer must be independent — instrumented, out-of-band, and using different evidence than the generation pass. Mattis's prediction about code review assumes this separation exists. DoorDash's gateway is one version of it. The checking layer is becoming infrastructure because the generation layer is becoming autonomous.

## The money and the market

Apollo's Torsten Slok issued a warning that got less attention than it deserved. AI agents like Meta's Muse could sweep household cash from checking accounts paying 0.1 percent into fintech accounts paying 3.3 to 5 percent. Banks would lose a large share of the cheap deposits they rely on to make loans. The mechanism is simple: an agent comparing account yields in seconds does what a human would put off for months. The inertia that kept money in low-yield checking — the hassle of switching, the uncertainty of a new bank, the time cost of comparison — evaporates when an agent handles it in one query.

This is the same force that repriced Goldman Sachs' consumer inertia basket — Schwab, Allstate, Planet Fitness — by seven percent in six sessions. [The agents that eat the walls](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-agents-that-eat-the-walls) described that selloff as the first time Wall Street priced agent access as a valuation variable. Slok's warning extends it: the threat is not just to companies that profit from customer inertia but to banks that profit from deposit inertia. Most cash would never leave the banking system. What changes is who gets paid for holding it. Banks and fintechs are splitting over agents the same way Amazon and Shopify split when Muse launched — one side owns the friction, the other side removes it.

The Anthropic IPO prospectus leaked the same week, and the numbers reframe the conversation. The $2 trillion listing is a way to pay for $518 billion in compute commitments. The headline $42 billion loss is mostly an accounting entry rather than cash out the door. Nearly half of Anthropic's sales run through cloud giants — Amazon and Google — that also own a piece of Anthropic and compete with it. The frontier lab is simultaneously the most valuable AI company outside OpenAI and a customer of its own investors. The IPO is not about valuation in the conventional sense. It is about funding the compute that the model requires, at a scale where only public markets can absorb the capital needs.

ElevenLabs doubled its valuation to $22 billion in a tender offer, cementing its position as one of Europe's most valuable AI companies. The Sifted 250 ranking, launched the same week at the Sifted Summit in London, placed health-tech and security companies at the top — Healf at 1,403 percent two-year revenue CAGR, Neko Health, Aikido Security. The EU's €5 billion Scaleup Fund was cleared to back UK and US companies despite no British capital committed yet. Nick Clegg, now at Hiro Capital, called LLMs "monstrously inefficient technology" and argued Europe should focus on AI for the physical world — robotics, defence, world models. General Catalyst's Zeynep Yavuz Wilson made the opposite case: AI value will be captured at the application layer, favouring Europe's services-heavy economy. The disagreement is not academic. It determines where European capital flows next.

## The habit and the institution

The Lenny & Friends Summit surfaced the same tension from the human side that DoorDash's gateway surfaces from the technical side. Lenny Rachitsky's five reflections from the event begin with the loneliness of agent-mediated work. The more we work with agents, the more isolated the work becomes, which creates a compensating demand for in-person connection. The summit sold out because the audience craved the texture of human collaboration that agents automate away. Ami Vora, Anthropic's CPO, said: "We don't know the answer. We don't think anyone knows the answer." Tamar Yehoshua from Atlassian offered the only honest position: "It depends what type of product it is. It depends what phase it is." The product community is not performing confidence. It is openly acknowledging that the old playbooks are broken.

[The reporting line](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-reporting-line) described agents getting their own box on the org chart. DoorDash's Agent Gateway is the technical version of the same institutional shift: agents get credentials, permissions, scopes, and audit trails — the infrastructure of employment, applied to software. Mattis's prediction about code review is the craft version: the engineer's relationship to code changes the way the engineer's relationship to assembly changed. Altman's morning routine is the personal version: the agent is not a tool you use. It is a staff member you manage. Each story describes the same transition at a different scale. The agent stops being a capability and starts being an institution — something you build governance around, change habits for, and reprice markets against.

Manus 2.0's Cue app gives each agent its own email, phone number, wallet, and computer. Multiple agents can be put in a group chat to split work across research, shortlisting, outreach, and deliverables. The agents can take calls, send messages, and make approved payments. This is the logical endpoint of the personal-agent trajectory: if agents act in the world, they need the same infrastructure humans use. CO/AI's analysis of Higgsfield's one-billion-dollar ARR offers the commercial framework: the only durable position in AI is being the sole road to a behavior that did not exist yesterday. Higgsfield spends $10,000 per employee per month on inference to produce studio-grade video nobody else can match. The spend is rent, not R&D — the monthly cost of being the only one who can do a thing. Most of what shipped in 2026 — report-writing AI, summarisation tools, code completion — has switching costs of zero because they do old chores faster. The model line is not the asset. The behavior is.

The question that none of these stories answer is whether the institutions are moving fast enough. DoorDash built a gateway because it had to. Banks are being repriced because the market expects them to be. Anthropic is going public because compute demands it. Altman changed his morning because the product was good enough. The adaptation is happening at the edges — in engineering teams, in trading desks, in CEO schedules — before it happens at the center. [The good enough threshold](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-good-enough-threshold) described standard models crossing a competence line. The week's stories describe what happens on the other side of that line: the capability is settled, and the work is everything else.
