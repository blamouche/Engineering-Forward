# The sovereigns and the tenants

*When three top law firms build their own AI and the best-funded legal AI vendor climbs off the frontier, the market splits between who owns intelligence and who rents it.*

Kirkland & Ellis committed $500 million to build its own AI. Morgan & Morgan pledged $1 billion over ten years. Latham & Watkins is buying its own Nvidia servers to fine-tune open-weight models on its own contracts. Three of the biggest law firms in the world, in the same quarter, decided to stop renting intelligence and start owning it.

The signal that makes this a trend rather than a headline: Harvey, the best-funded legal AI vendor on the market — the company that raised $550 million at a $15.5 billion valuation — is moving off the frontier models it was built on. When the vendor and the customers both back away from renting intelligence in the same breath, something structural has shifted. CO/AI framed it as a clean split: sovereigns and tenants. Firms with proprietary data and an honest evaluation loop walk off the labs' land. Everyone else rents good-enough thinking by the sip. The frontier is a genuine moat — the best intelligence on earth — but it is a Formula 1 engine that almost nobody needs. Contract review, intake memos, and support queues run fine on a good open-weight model that the firm controls. Pay the frontier premium for the 2 percent of work that demands it. For the other 98 percent, you are renting something you could own.

## The eval loop that decides who stays

The dividing line between sovereigns and tenants is not data alone. It is the eval loop — the practice of grading AI output against a clear definition of done until the work is right. Alexandr Wang, now running AI at Meta, put it bluntly: a well-built agent swarm out-produces 100 senior engineers, and the machine underneath is "just markdown files, cron jobs, goal, metrics, data." The magic is not the model. The magic is the loop.

Gartner projects that 40 percent of agentic AI projects will be dead by 2027. The killer is almost never the model. It is that nobody could define the win. Companies pulling ahead are the ones with an honest eval loop and the data to prove it. Kirkland has decades of contract data. Latham has sensitive client documents that cannot leave the building. Morgan & Morgan has a decade of case files. The frontier model cannot train on any of it. An open-weight model fine-tuned on their own servers can.

This is where TypeSafe's Jev model enters the picture. Jev does not generate prose. It returns probabilities — structured answers that code can branch on directly. Mike Taylor at Every tested it across 11 experiments and found it could make 1,709 judgments for less than a cent. It caught 6 of 7 deliberately introduced defects; Fable 5.1 caught all 7. But at a quarter-cent per 777 judgments, running checks multiple times during a task costs nothing. The alternative, as Taylor noted, is not checking at all.

Jev is a tool for the eval loop. It makes the sovereigns' path cheaper. A law firm building in-house AI can run Jev across thousands of contract clauses, flagging the ones that need human review, at a cost that rounds to zero. The model is not the product. The checking infrastructure is. ByteByteGo's guide to LLM evaluation laid out the same architecture from a different angle. An LLM is healthy if it consistently generates useful results across accuracy, safety, speed, reliability, and cost — not just if it returns a correct answer. The evaluation stack combines golden datasets, automated metrics, LLM-as-a-judge, human review, and production monitoring. No external body mandates it. Companies build it because the cost of not building it is worse. The sovereigns are companies that invested in this infrastructure. The tenants are companies that outsourced it to the vendor.

## The factory that eats its own dog food

OpenAI is doing internally what the law firms are doing externally. Gergely Orosz visited the company's headquarters and found that Codex has become the backbone of virtually everything — not just for engineers but for finance, legal, and marketing teams. Codex adoption went from near zero to 90 percent across non-engineering organizations in four months, without a mandate from above. IDE usage is declining. Pull requests per engineer are growing like a hockey stick — roughly a 10x increase in CI/CD load in six months.

The agentic software factory OpenAI built is a nine-step pipeline: human defines outcome, Codex gathers context, Codex implements, CI builds and tests, multi-specialist agents review the change, an SRE agent deploys, production monitors run, and a Perf Factory feeds back. Engineers are becoming more like product managers. Judgment, prioritization, and taste matter more than traditional systems engineering. OpenAI is not renting intelligence from itself. It owns the entire stack — the model, the harness, the eval loop, the deployment infrastructure. The company is the ultimate sovereign. And it is extending that sovereignty outward: the Codex harness is now a public API with no platform fee. OpenAI is positioning itself as the default substrate for agentic applications, giving away the infrastructure layer and charging only for model and tool usage. The strategy mirrors AWS: own the infrastructure, let applications compete on top.

But OpenAI is also becoming something else. The launch of ChatGPT for Financial Services — pairing GPT-6 Astra with premium hosted data from Daloopa, PitchBook, LSEG, and Crunchbase — is a bet that the data layer matters more than the model. Morgan Stanley and Evercore helped build the workflows. OpenAI is competing with Bloomberg and FactSet, not with Anthropic. The company that builds the agent also owns the data the agent reasons over. That is sovereignty extended to the information layer.

## The pricing event

The Anthropic IPO puts a public number on this split. Anthropic picked Nasdaq. Bankers discussed a raise of as much as $100 billion — which would surpass SpaceX as the largest IPO in history. Q2 revenue rose 14-fold year-over-year to $11.5 billion. Annualized revenue reached $65 billion at the end of July. Investors expect $120 billion annualized by year-end.

The IPO prices positions that already exist. Five of the ten stocks best positioned to benefit already hold Anthropic equity. Three more hold compute contracts worth a combined $24.6 billion. Two cloud providers have booked over $300 billion in Anthropic spending commitments. The IPO converts private positions into publicly priced assets. Here is the tension. Dario Amodei published an essay calling for the industry to slow model capability improvement. A slowdown would save Anthropic billions in training costs and make the profit numbers easier to sustain. But it directly conflicts with the compute contracts that underpin the stocks benefiting from the IPO. The companies selling training compute carry that risk. The companies holding equity do not. The slowdown is simultaneously a genuine safety concern and an economic strategy that protects the IPO's margins. Both can be true.

The White House AI czar called the slowdown a cartel. Amodei, Altman, and Musk — three rivals who agree on nothing — aligned behind "pacing the frontier" within hours. OpenAI simultaneously asked Congress whether an industry-wide slowdown would be legal under antitrust law. You do not send lawyers to check whether your conscience is a felony. The $32 trillion question — what the U.S. economy pays annually for intelligence and labor — is the prize. The slowdown freezes the field while labs try to climb from selling tokens by the sip to owning the outcomes intelligence produces. Open source and China are the two things standing in their way.

## What computer use changes

The sovereigns' calculation shifts again when agents can operate real software. Mike Taylor at Every upgraded GPT-6 Astra from "wouldn't use it daily" to "starting to believe Astra is AGI" in under two weeks, driven entirely by computer use — clicking, typing, and scrolling through actual applications. Every's team is now outsourcing school forms, calendar management, book link verification, video editing, marketplace listings, and slide deck creation to AI agents. Computer use is the capability that makes agents feel like assistants rather than tools. When an agent can operate the same applications you use, the barrier to delegation drops. Douglas Brundage at Every built a workflow: design two or three reference slides, give Codex the copy, and let it build the full deck in Google Slides. The iterative rule-building means the agent gets better over time.

For the sovereigns, computer use means the in-house agent can operate the firm's existing software stack — the document management system, the billing platform, the case database — without custom integrations for each one. For the tenants, computer use means the rented agent can do the same, but the work product runs through infrastructure the tenant does not control. The Ninth Circuit ruled that AI agents act on behalf of users, not developers. When your agent signs, sends, or deletes, that is on you. The [accountability gap](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-accountability-gap) between who builds the agent and who uses it gets sharper when the agent can touch every application on your desktop. The [land grab](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-land-grab) described companies buying the rails underneath intelligence. The sovereigns are doing the inverse: keeping their data, building their own rails, and renting intelligence only when they cannot replicate it.

The Anthropic IPO prices a market where the most valuable customers are the ones most motivated to leave. The law firms going in-house are not the long tail. They are the crown jewels — the clients with the highest-value data, the most complex workflows, and the budget to build alternatives. ByteByteGo's deep dive into LLM memory explains why the sovereigns can pull this off technically: the model does not possess persistent memory. The application does. A firm that owns the application layer — the context management, the entity extraction, the conversation history — controls the memory the agent works with. A firm renting the frontier model hands its memory to the vendor. The sovereigns keep their memory on their own servers. The tenants' memory lives in someone else's cloud.

The market will not split evenly. The tenants who stay on the frontier will pay more for intelligence that their competitors got for free by building in-house. The sovereigns who left will pay nothing for the 98 percent of work that an open-weight model handles, and pay the frontier premium only for the 2 percent that demands it. Nvidia's new Rubin racks cut the cost of agentic inference by 67x, turning "burn a million tokens checking the work" into a viable plan. The labs keep the frontier. They watch the highest-value work get built in-house on cheaper models. The IPO prices the moment before the tenants figure out what the sovereigns already know.
