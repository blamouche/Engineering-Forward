# The half-life of what you know

*When the harness evolves every week and the model changes every quarter, the fundamentals you skipped become the expertise you can't download.*

The Codex harness — the scaffolding around OpenAI's coding agent — is always slightly ahead of the model it serves. Tibo Sottiaux, who built Codex, told The Pragmatic Engineer that the harness provides "crutches": guardrails, safety checks, developer messages injected at the start of each turn. As models improve, crutches are discarded. New ones appear. The harness evolves. The model evolves. What you learned about the harness last quarter is already partially wrong.

This is the knowledge depreciation problem, and it is not limited to Codex. The 12 Claude Code features that ByteByteGo catalogued this week — CLAUDE.md, Plan Mode, Checkpoints, Skills, Hooks, MCP, Plugins, Subagents — represent an infrastructure layer that changes every release. A developer who mastered Claude Code's Subagents API in June is working with a different tool in September. The mastery depreciates. The features that mattered three months ago have been replaced, renamed, or absorbed into something else.

## What compounds

The OECD ran a study across 760,000 students in 91 countries and found that students who skip AI outperform students who lean on it. The finding sounds paradoxical. The answer is cognitive struggle. Learning is not the output. Learning is the process that produces the output. When the AI handles the struggle, the output appears but the learning does not. The student who skips AI builds the mental model. The student who leans on AI gets the answer and loses the architecture.

ByteByteGo's networking guide traces a single API call through DNS resolution, TCP handshake, TLS negotiation, load balancer dispatch, and application server processing. Most developers never think about these layers. They call an HTTP client and the infrastructure is invisible. When latency spikes or connections fail, the developer who understands the full request path fixes the problem. The developer who does not spends hours guessing. DNS works the same way it worked twenty years ago. The TCP three-way handshake is the same handshake. The fundamentals compound because they apply to every system you will ever build.

The git revert article from the same newsletter makes the point from a different angle. Git revert causes conflicts not because the tool is broken but because it applies an inverse diff onto a codebase that has moved on. The developer who understands why conflicts happen — the directed acyclic graph of commits, the inverse diff applied to changed lines — can resolve them under pressure during a hotfix rollback. The developer who memorized the commands without understanding the model cannot. The commands change. The model does not.

The Pragmatic Engineer reported that Uber cut cost per AI request by 34 percent and cost per session by 52 percent through open weight models, optimized model selection, cheaper subagents, and context compaction. Pinterest achieves cost per transaction at less than 8 percent of comparable closed models. AT&T cut its AI bill by 56 percent with a 2 percent quality loss. The specific tools — LiteLLM routing, open weight models, context compaction at 400K tokens — will change. The discipline behind them will not. Knowing which tasks are simple and which are hard, classifying complexity before dispatching, and measuring quality after routing are fundamental engineering practices. The routing rules depreciate. The classification skill compounds.

Every CEO Dan Shipper and head of evals Mike Taylor built personal benchmarks for every employee: five to ten recurring tasks per person, scored against pass/fail criteria the employee defined. GPT-5.6 Luna, the smallest and cheapest model, outperformed Fable 5.1 and GPT-5.6 Sol on Mike Taylor's personal benchmark. Public leaderboards had ranked Luna well below its larger siblings. The insight — that a personal benchmark surfaces information a public one cannot — is not model-specific. It compounds. [The constraint premium](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-constraint-premium) described how constraints improve output quality. The personal benchmark is a constraint that is also a compounding asset: every iteration makes the next comparison sharper.

## What depreciates

The writers who shared their AI-assisted workflows with Every — Kevin Roose, Maggie Appleton, Dan Shipper, Alexandra Samuel — have something in common that none of them stated directly. Each one built their workflow on years of self-knowledge about their own creative process. Roose knows his research process well enough to identify where AI serves as an "outboard brain" and where it would compromise his sources. Appleton knows her thinking well enough to use dictation for brain-dumps and Claude for surfacing connections she missed. Shipper knows his writing process well enough to design a plugin that integrates AI at every stage without ceding control. Samuel knows her blocks well enough to use AI as a conversational partner that generates momentum. The AI-specific techniques — the prompts, the plugins, the particular models — will change. The self-knowledge that tells them where to apply the AI and where to stop will not. It compounds with every piece they write.

Jack Cheng, who hit Claude's weekly usage limits and found the constraint productive, proposed a practice that captures the principle: write down why a question matters before bringing it to the model. The deliberate pause forces articulation of intent. The question itself may be answered by the model and forgotten. The discipline of articulating intent before asking improves with every repetition. The model gets better at answering. The discipline of asking gets better at asking. They are different kinds of improvement, and only one of them is yours.

Linas's agentic transition playbook, built on Bessemer's study of 20+ engineering organizations, identifies that the binding constraint is organizational structure, not individual productivity. Companies that made engineers 10x faster still could not push organization-level output past 50 percent. The top 1 percent of engineers ship 46x the median's AI-written code. The playbook's seven concrete plays — a $1K per engineer per month token floor, a hiring filter that replaces years of experience with agent fluency, two numbers to plot every engineer on — are specific to this moment. They will change as the tools change. But the organizational diagnosis compounds: the bottleneck is the shape of the organization around the engineer, not the speed of the engineer.

OpenAI's decision to productize the Codex harness as a public API with no platform fee is a bet that the infrastructure layer matters more than the model. The harness — managed compute environments, tool routing, subagent coordination, context compaction — is what makes an agent reliable enough to delegate to. The specific harness features depreciate with every model upgrade. But the design principles behind the harness — designing for failure, containing blast radius, routing by complexity — are the same principles that American Express applied to its cell-based payment architecture. Each cell is self-sufficient with no synchronous cross-cell dependencies. A few hundred milliseconds of wasted compute buys complete failure isolation. [What the review becomes](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/what-the-review-becomes) traced this principle into code review. The harness features depreciate. The architecture principle compounds.

## The fundamentals tax

ByteByteGo's article on LLM error handling identifies a failure category that traditional software does not have: semantic failures. The API returns 200. The model generated text. The text is wrong. Conventional error handling covers network timeouts and rate limits. It does not cover the case where the system succeeds and the output is incorrect. The specific error patterns — hallucinations, tool-call failures, idempotency gaps — are particular to this generation of models. But the discipline of naming failure modes, designing for them, and accepting the costs of containment is as old as reliability engineering. The failure taxonomy depreciates as models improve. The discipline of building one compounds.

The AI risk conversation that Alex Kantrowitz tried to reframe this week — moving past the polarized extremes of "AI will kill us all" versus "warnings are just marketing" — has its own depreciation schedule. The specific risk percentages change. The researchers come and go. Dario Amodei goes silent; Evan Hubinger puts 10 percent odds on extinction; Jacob Coxon resigns. The specifics depreciate. But the structural dynamic Kantrowitz identified — that both labs are pursuing recursive self-improvement without external constraints, and that the prisoner's dilemma means no one who could slow down can afford to — does not depreciate. It compounds, because every quarter that passes without regulation makes the dilemma sharper. CO/AI's parallel observation, that the $400K which funded the 9/11 attacks now represents the cost ceiling for AI-enabled infrastructure attacks, is a specific claim that will be refined. The underlying pattern — that AI lowers the barrier to sophisticated operations previously requiring state-level resources — compounds.

The financial infrastructure stories follow the same logic. Chime's $590M acquisition of Stride Bank was shaped not by the charter itself but by the Durbin Amendment's $10B asset threshold — a regulatory fundamental that has not changed since it was written. Meta's acquisition of Stilla to complete its agentic commerce loop — Muse for the buyer, Stilla for the seller — is a specific deal that will be superseded by the next one. But the structural insight, that owning the rails matters more than owning the interface, is the same pattern that drove Stripe's acquisition of OpenRouter and Robinhood's decision to build its own prediction market infrastructure. The deals depreciate. The pattern compounds.

Sequoia's venture scout program expanding across Europe, the EU's €5bn Scaleup Europe Fund becoming the dominant force in late-stage European tech, Samsung and Nvidia backing Mistral's €3bn Series D — these are specific events that will be replaced by next quarter's funding rounds. But the structural shift they reveal — that European capital markets are building the growth infrastructure they lacked for a decade — is a compounding asset. Bending Spoons' $1.36bn acquisition of Miro proves European companies can execute large-scale M&A. That capability, once demonstrated, does not depreciate.

The cache bug that Claude Code fixed this week is a small example with large implications. Tool definitions were being re-rendered after OAuth token refreshes, triggering prompt-cache misses roughly once per hour. For a 20-minute session, the cost is negligible. For a multi-hour agent session, the cost compounds into a quiet leak nobody notices until the billing alarm goes off. The bug was not in the model. It was in the interaction between authentication infrastructure and the caching layer. A developer who understands caching, OAuth token schedules, and infrastructure audit practices catches this bug. A developer who only knows how to prompt the model does not.

The companies that thrive in the agentic era are not the ones with the best models or the most tokens. Those are inputs, and inputs depreciate. The companies that thrive are the ones whose people understand the layers underneath the model — the networking, the caching, the failure modes, the organizational structure, the routing logic, the self-knowledge to know where the machine helps and where it hurts. That knowledge arrives the way it always has: through the struggle the OECD study measured, through the debugging the ByteByteGo articles describe, through the deliberate pause Cheng proposed before asking the model anything at all. The half-life of tool-specific expertise is shrinking. The half-life of fundamentals is not.
