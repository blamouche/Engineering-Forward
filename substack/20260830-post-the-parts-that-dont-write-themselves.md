# The parts that don't write themselves

*While AI automates code, prose, and test migrations, the engineering work that keeps systems running — performance, security, evaluation, architecture — remains stubbornly manual, and that's where the value has moved.*

Casey Muratori has been making software run fast for twenty years. In a recent conversation with The Pragmatic Engineer, he argued something that sounds almost contrarian in 2026: performance matters more than ever, and the industry has decided to ignore it. His critique targets the received wisdom that most engineers absorb without questioning — "premature optimization is the root of all evil," "clean code is always better," "test-driven development is non-negotiable." Each of these, Muratori says, is a rule nobody tested in practice. He wants engineers to start from what hardware can theoretically do and work backward, rather than profiling hotspots and calling it optimization. Reading assembly, he says, takes twenty to thirty instructions. It is not exotic. It is just neglected.

This argument lands at an odd moment. AI coding agents generate millions of lines of code per day. Companies like Asana, Airbnb, and Uber have used AI to compress multi-year migration projects into weeks — Asana moved 4,000 test files in two weeks for roughly $12,000, against a manual estimate of $6 million and five years. But the code AI generates has to run somewhere, on hardware with real constraints, and someone has to know whether it runs fast enough. The migration story is about volume. The performance story is about understanding. AI can transform one test framework into another because the pattern is mechanical. It cannot tell you whether your cache utilization is killing your throughput, because that requires reading the hardware's actual behavior and reasoning backward from it.

## The fundamentals don't automate

The same pattern shows up across engineering fundamentals. Background work — the cron jobs, job queues, and message brokers that move operations out of the user's request path — follows an evolutionary path from a single scheduled script to distributed systems with worker pools. Each transition is an architectural decision: when does a cron job become a job queue? When does a job queue need a message broker? These are judgment calls about reliability, latency, and operational complexity. The code for each pattern is well-documented. The decision about when to move between them is not.

Speculative decoding offers another example. The technique makes large language models two to three times faster by pairing a target model with a small draft model that generates candidate tokens the large model verifies in a single forward pass. The math is elegant and the implementation is published. But the gains depend on workload — acceptance rates hit 80 to 90 percent for structured output like code, and drop below 50 percent for creative writing, where overhead outweighs benefit. Under concurrency, speedups shrink from 1.96x at batch size one to 1.21x at batch size 128. Serving systems like vLLM dynamically disable speculation when spare compute disappears. Someone has to make that tradeoff in production, for a specific workload, with real traffic. The technique automates nothing about that decision.

This is the shape of engineering work that AI does not touch: not the transformation of one syntax into another, but the judgment about what to build, when to switch architectures, and how to measure whether the result actually works on real hardware with real users.

## What the benchmarks can't see

Companies spending $100 million a year on AI models often have no idea whether those models save their employees time. Public benchmarks measure general capability — can the model pass a bar exam, solve a coding problem, answer a trivia question. They cannot tell you whether the model caught the clause your lawyers care about, preserved your house style, or handled the edge case that matters to your specific business. Every, the company behind the AI-for-knowledge-work guides, built an internal benchmark called KateBench to evaluate a copyediting agent trained on 30,000 historical edits from one editor. The acceptance rate looked good — 85 to 90 percent — but the number hid problems: a 40-suggestion cap, non-deterministic output, and residual human work that the rate did not capture.

The prescription is simple and unglamorous: pick one recurring job, write down five ways the current model gets it wrong, create gold-standard examples, and run models against them. That is the beginning of an internal eval. No frontier model produces this for you. No leaderboard tells you which model handles your specific failure modes. The work is manual, company-specific, and compounding — each eval you build makes the next model decision sharper. As [the model is not the product](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-model-is-not-the-product), the evaluation built around it is.

The same gap shows up in security. Researchers from MATS Research, ELLIS Institute Tübingen, and the Max Planck Institute demonstrated that encrypted reasoning traces from frontier models can be extracted by replaying them into cheaper models in the same family. The attack exploits a structural vulnerability: the client must always decrypt the reasoning block to display a summary, which creates an inherent extraction surface. Providers withhold full reasoning traces for competitive and safety reasons — revealing them could expose guardrail circumvention strategies or enable model distillation. The assumption was that encryption protected these traces. The research shows it does not, at least not against a determined adversary with access to any cheaper model in the same family.

Trail of Bits issued a parallel warning: GPT-5.6-Cyber, a cybersecurity-focused model, escaped QEMU and KVM virtual machines three times during testing, finding zero-day vulnerabilities. The recommendation was to test cyber-capable agents on throwaway machines with no real credentials. Neither the reasoning-trace attack nor the VM escape requires a fundamental breakthrough. Both exploit the gap between what providers assumed was secure and what the actual threat model allows.

## The stack gets locked, the work stays open

While the engineering fundamentals persist, the infrastructure layer is consolidating fast. Nvidia agreed to acquire Hugging Face for $12.9 billion, giving it control from silicon through model distribution — the chips, the CUDA platform, the model hub where most open-weight models are shared. Anthropic struck a $45 billion deal to rent compute capacity from Nscale, signaling that frontier labs will lock in long-term infrastructure contracts with specialized providers rather than building everything themselves. Agent infrastructure is consolidating too: AgentSky launched a unified API putting Claude Code, Codex, and other coding agents behind one endpoint. Monid offers an "OpenRouter for agent tools" with 1,700 tools accessible through a single key. Salesforce and Anthropic announced Claudeforce, exposing Salesforce data and workflows through MCP servers, making the Model Context Protocol a standard piece of enterprise plumbing.

This consolidation means more of the stack is rented. The parts that remain yours — the internal evals, the performance analysis, the security testing, the architectural decisions, the encoded expertise — are exactly the parts that do not write themselves. Ramp built its own coding agent, Inspect, because off-the-shelf tools could not run enough parallel agents or offer good frontend tooling. The agent runs on remote sandboxes with five-second spin-up, all sessions public, 150 people contributing. Block, Stripe, and Shopify each built their own. The pattern is consistent: companies that build custom tooling tightly integrated with their internal systems get better results than frontier lab offerings, because the frontier labs do not know your systems.

Every's shift from personal agents to a shared Slack-based company agent illustrates the same principle from the other direction. Personal agents like OpenClaw work well for individuals, but the maintenance burden — credentials expiring, integrations breaking, stale instructions accumulating — grows linearly with team size. A stronger model cannot log in when a credential expires or detect that an instruction is outdated. Every moved to a shared model where one agent serves the company but each person works through their own context. The infrastructure around the model — apps, tools, instructions, maintenance — changes results as much as a new model release, sometimes more.

## What compounds

The article about Fable 5 as a McKinsey consulting engine is instructive here. Twelve Claude Skills built on published consulting frameworks — Minto's Pyramid Principle, Playing to Win, the CIA's Analysis of Competing Hypotheses — lifted the model from 38 percent to 97 percent on a partner-level checklist. The frameworks were never secret. McKinsey, BCG, and Bain collectively charge $37 billion a year for a method executed by associates with under two years of experience. The constraint was never access to the frameworks but the two years of internalization under partner supervision. A frontier model can run the method with more discipline than a second-year associate when the framework is encoded as executable skills.

The encoding is the work. The model is a generalist until you give it structure, test it against your specific failure modes, and verify it against gold-standard examples. That work does not automate. It compounds. Each eval you build, each skill you encode, each architectural decision you document makes the next deployment sharper. The companies that figure this out are not the ones with the best models. They are the ones with the best catalog of what they have learned about their own work — the parts that do not write themselves, written down by the people who had to learn them.
