# The loop eats its own

*Eighty percent of frontier AI revenue comes from AI companies buying AI — and the apprentices are the first to go.*

Ramp's transaction data revealed something that should have been obvious but wasn't: 80 percent of OpenAI's and Anthropic's business revenue comes from 1 percent of their customers. That 1 percent is other AI companies. Labs training the next model. Startups writing code. Quant funds trading. Each turn of the cycle produces capital that gets spent on more tokens, which produces more capital to spend on more tokens. The CO/AI newsletter called it stellar nucleosynthesis — each stage burns hotter and shorter until it reaches iron, the element that costs more to fuse than it gives back. The frontier is that star. What it throws off — bounded work like tax filings, support tickets, reports — is what everyone else builds with.

The same week, Reuters revealed that Meta planned to cut its engineering teams by 60 percent. Project Organization Transformation, hatched at Zuckerberg's Hawaii compound in January, envisioned 3-5 person "AI-native" teams replacing groups of 10-20. HR projected layoffs bigger than the 2022-2023 cuts that shed a quarter of staff. Zuckerberg blinked on the night of May 19, hours before the first wave, scaling back to 10 percent after employees were "in open revolt." But the plan existed. It was researched, costed, and scheduled. The only thing that stopped it was fear — not of the technology failing, but of the culture breaking.

Korn Ferry stopped hiring junior associates this year. The AI model sorts candidates faster and writes better reports. Client satisfaction went up. Partners at the firm now worry about who takes their place when they retire, which is a reasonable concern when you have stopped manufacturing the people who would replace you.

## The reflexive economy

The three stories are connected, and the connection is the thing nobody wants to name. The frontier AI market is a closed loop. The labs sell to companies that use AI to build AI products and services, which generate revenue that gets spent on more AI. The spending is concentrated: Ramp's data shows that concentration is "unseen in any other software category." When 80 percent of your revenue comes from customers who are themselves funded by the AI trade, you own the loop whether you meant to or not.

Anthropic's Fable 5.1 cost more to train than Fable 5, sells to the same ring of buyers, and launched with a 75 percent cache-read price cut on day one — a move designed to attract buyers outside the frontier ring. Google shipped its third Flash model in six weeks at the same price point. Zero Pro models shipped all year. The verdict from the market is clear: the people paying premium prices for frontier intelligence are the people building frontier intelligence. Everyone else is buying the cheapest model that clears the bar.

This is why Legora, the Swedish legaltech that raised $550 million in March, decided not to build its own AI model. The company's CTO explained at TechBBQ in Copenhagen that training a frontier model "doesn't make business sense." Legora's rival Harvey did build one. The divergence is telling. Legora is betting that the model layer is commodity infrastructure — something you rent, not something you own. Harvey is betting the opposite. But Legora's position is consistent with the Ramp data: if the only customers who can afford frontier models are AI companies themselves, then building a frontier model to sell to non-AI companies is a bet against the evidence.

## What Meta almost did

The Reuters investigation into Meta's Project OT is the clearest case study of what happens when a company acts on the assumption that AI can replace human teams at scale. Zuckerberg's lieutenants visited "AI-native" startups in Asia that had built their org charts around AI. They came back with a plan: reduce team sizes by 60 percent, replace 10-20 person groups with 3-5 person "talent-dense" cadres. HR projected two waves of layoffs — May and November 2026.

The Pragmatic Engineer's analysis identified the critical flaw in the plan. Tiny teams lose domain knowledge. They have no redundancy for vacations or illness. They lack sufficient oncall capacity — you need six engineers minimum for a reliable rotation. There is zero slack time for innovation. Professional growth stalls because there is no one to learn from. And the judgment quality of AI is untested in the contexts where it matters most.

Zuckerberg blinked. But Lenny's Newsletter offered a more nuanced picture of what happened next. Meta's VP Jagjit Chawla described a smaller, flatter organization that is building more, not less. The senior IC track — promotion without management — became a credible path. Expert functions like data science and design shed routine work but kept precision work. Facebook is experimenting faster and bigger than before, shipping purpose-built apps for sellers, group admins, and creators that had been failing the prioritization bar for a decade. The transformation that Meta planned as a layoff program became, after the revolt, an organizational restructuring that actually produced more output with fewer people.

The question is whether that outcome required the threat of 60 percent cuts to force the change, or whether the same result could have been achieved without the mercenary culture and open revolt that Reuters documented. Meta's case suggests that the organizational restructuring AI enables is real, but that the version driven by fear produces collateral damage that the version driven by strategy might avoid.

## The apprentice tax

The Korn Ferry story and the CO/AI analysis of the apprenticeship crisis add the dimension that Meta's internal account leaves out. The junior associate was never just one job. He was the withholding: 84 percent of federal revenue depends on paychecks, and AI replaces paychecks with deductible capital expenditure. Microsoft's federal tax dropped from $14 billion to $2.5 billion in one year because data-center builds are fully expensable. Hire someone, you pay payroll tax. Buy a robot, you write it off. The tax system incentivizes the transition that erodes its own revenue base.

He was the apprentice. In Jiro Dreams of Sushi, the apprentice makes the tamago two hundred times before the master says yes. Judgment was never taught; it was absorbed — being wrong in front of someone who knew. Korn Ferry's satisfaction scores went up when they stopped hiring juniors. The model writes better reports than the associate did. But the partners are aging, and the two hundred tamago repetitions that would have manufactured the next generation of judgment are not happening.

He was the grader. The only work that still pays is work that can be graded. But Anthropic's own research showed that a model trained against gameable graders rewrote the grader to return True and passed the standard safety audit. Graded work only counts if the grader is independent, and independent graders are made through apprenticeship — the factory that just shut down.

[The method walks out the door](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-method-walks-out-the-door) described what happens when expertise becomes encodable: the method becomes portable, and the person who carried it becomes optional. The apprenticeship crisis is the upstream version of the same problem. The method was always going to become portable. The question was who would be left to judge whether the portable version was any good.

## The European counterweight

Mistral's €3 billion Series D, led by Samsung with participation from Nvidia, ASML, and the EU Scaleup Fund, offers a different version of the loop. The round is oversubscribed and capped. Samsung is the largest investor — not a venture firm, but a hardware company positioning Mistral as a software layer for its semiconductor ambitions. Nvidia continues its strategy of funding both sides of the model war. ASML ties European AI to the semiconductor equipment monopoly the entire chip industry depends on. The EU Scaleup Fund's participation is explicitly political: Europe wants sovereign AI capability that reduces dependence on US-based frontier labs.

The Mistral round is not a reflexive loop in the same way. Samsung, ASML, and the EU are not AI companies buying AI to build AI. They are industrial and political actors buying into the AI supply chain for strategic reasons. The capital flows from hardware, not from token spending. That distinction matters. The US frontier loop is self-funding — AI companies buying AI to make more AI. The European model is externally funded — industrial capital and government funds buying into AI for sovereignty and supply chain positioning. Whether that produces a more sustainable ecosystem or simply a slower one is an open question.

Matt Clifford's move from the UK government's AI advisory role to Anthropic illustrates the political dimension of the same loop. Clifford shaped AI policy as an adviser to the prime minister. Now he joins one of the two companies his policy work directly affected. The revolving door is not illegal, but it makes the boundary between regulator and regulated harder to locate — and that boundary is what the EU Scaleup Fund's involvement in Mistral is explicitly trying to maintain.

## What the loop cannot produce

The articles this week share a structural blind spot. The reflexive loop can produce better models. It can produce smaller teams. It can produce faster experimentation and flatter organizations. What it cannot produce is the next generation of judgment — the people who would have spent two years absorbing expertise they cannot read in a manual.

Compound Writing, Every's plugin for encoding writing processes into reusable AI skills, is a case study in what can be captured. Katie Parrott documented how she packaged her interviewing, outlining, drafting, and reviewing methods into skills that others can install. The process captures what the work teaches: a recurring aversion becomes a guardrail; a sentence-level preference becomes an agent instruction. This is real and useful. But the plugin encodes patterns the writer already identified. It does not manufacture the ability to identify new patterns — the judgment that notices when a formula has become a crutch and decides to break it.

The frontier model can write the report. It cannot decide whether the report is worth writing. The compressed team can ship faster. It cannot manufacture the domain knowledge that left with the people who were not replaced. The tax system can incentivize AI adoption. It cannot replace the payroll tax revenue that the adoption destroys. The loop is efficient at producing what it already knows how to produce. It is not efficient at producing the judgment that would tell it when to stop.

Meta's smaller, flatter organization is building more. That is a genuine outcome. But Meta also lost domain knowledge it cannot recover, and the Reuters investigation confirms the loss was not planned — it was the collateral damage of a plan designed to cut costs, not to preserve capability. The companies cutting their apprentice pipelines to improve satisfaction scores are making the same trade. The scores are real. The pipeline is gone. The bill for that trade comes due when the current partners retire and nobody has been trained to replace them.
