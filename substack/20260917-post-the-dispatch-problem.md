# The dispatch problem

*When agents can do almost anything, the bottleneck moves from capability to organization — and the checking tax is higher than anyone budgeted.*

Nous Research refactored Hermes — its own AI agent platform — using 1,393 parallel agents and approximately $19,000 in token spend. The number that matters is not the $19,000 or even the 1,393. It is the pattern: plan the work, shard the codebase into manageable units, dispatch agents to each unit in parallel, then verify the results. This is the same pattern that scales human engineering teams — break the work down, assign units, integrate — except the agents run at a speed no human team can match. At roughly $13.70 per agent, the cost is modest compared to human engineering hours. But the post reads less like a product demo and more like an operating manual, because the hard part was never the agents doing the work. It was the planning, sharding, and verification that surrounded them.

This is the dispatch problem, and it is becoming the defining challenge of the agentic era. The question is no longer whether AI agents can do the work. GPT-6 Astra can operate Google Slides, filling in screenshots and making hundreds of edits with minimal intervention. Gemini 3.8 Live can see, think, talk, and code simultaneously, turning voice agents from turn-taking demos into collaborative workers. OpenAI's Codex has become the backbone of everything the company does, from engineering to finance to legal, with a 90% adoption rate across non-engineering teams in four months. The capability exists. The question is how you organize for it.

## The architecture of delegation

Kieran Klaassen, general manager of Cora, opened his AI workspace to Every's "Show Us Your Folders" series, and what he revealed was not a tool setup but an organizational design. His architecture has four parts: Tuin ("garden" in Dutch) for storing goals, tasks, and personal records; Erf ("yard") as a coordinator that starts sessions and dispatches tasks to the right folder; a dashboard as the interface; and a Mac mini for always-on compute. Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, and his own tools all use the same folders, so switching tools does not require migrating context. The principle is simple: make a separate folder for each job, and each folder gives an agent one clear job and the context it needs. Memory is organized by time scale — daily, weekly, monthly, yearly — because a daily plan and a monthly review need different amounts of history and should not draw from the same undifferentiated file.

The workspace audit that followed, using Compound Engineering's ce-doc-review skill, exposed what happens when this architecture goes wrong: 25 top-level folders, over a thousand raw session files, three versions of a working project across two machines. The audit flagged duplicated homes, conflicting instructions, stale indexes, and material captured but never consolidated. The fix was not more agents or a better model. It was consolidation, treating runtime copies as disposable, and requiring human review before captured context becomes shared guidance. The folder is the agent, as Klaassen puts it — which means the folder's organization is the agent's competence.

This maps directly onto what OpenAI built internally. Gergely Orosz's visit to OpenAI headquarters, documented in The Pragmatic Engineer, revealed a nine-step pipeline: human defines outcome, Codex gathers context from Git, Slack, Notion, and Databricks, Codex implements, CI builds and tests, multi-specialist agents review the change, an SRE agent deploys, production monitors run, and a Perf Factory feeds back. Engineers are becoming more like product managers — judgment, prioritization, and taste matter more than traditional systems engineering. The /goal setting lets agents work on long-running tasks for days, spinning off sub-agents to reduce the human's management surface. The pipeline is not a tool. It is an organizational design that happens to run on agents.

European investment tells the same story from the funding side. Mistral secured a multi-year €100M deal with TotalEnergies to build models for reservoir exploration. Euclyd, a Dutch startup building infrastructure to make AI more efficient, raised a €200M Series A co-led by the EU's own Scaleup Fund. The capital is flowing toward infrastructure and efficiency, not raw model capability — toward the rails that intelligence runs on, not the intelligence itself.

## The checking tax

The dispatch problem has a cost that most organizations have not budgeted. Vercel reduced its inbound sales team from 10 people to 1.25, with 90% automation and a 32x return. The infrastructure bill runs in the single-digit thousands annually. The AI-SDR promise that everyone mocked in 2023 came true in 2026. But the 0.25 of a person still on the payroll is the one checking the machine, and that oversight cost is the hidden line item. Glean surveyed 6,000 workers and found that automation saves 11 hours per week — but 6.5 of those hours go right back into checking, correcting, and re-feeding the machine. Net saving: under half the headline. String 10 agent steps at 95% reliability each and the chain finishes clean only about 60% of the time. Oversight is not a rounding error in the ROI math. It is the ROI math.

The checking tax compounds at scale. When 1,393 agents refactor a million-line codebase, someone has to verify that the refactoring is correct — not in aggregate, but unit by unit. OpenAI's software factory addresses this with multiple domain specialist agents reviewing each change — a cloud infra agent, a security agent, a code quality agent — with risk classification determining whether human review is required. The multi-specialist approach is notable because it would be impractical to have human experts from every team review every change. But it also means the checking layer itself has become an agent workload, and the verification of verification becomes a recursive problem that nobody has fully solved.

TypeSafe's Jev model, profiled by Every's Mike Taylor, offers one answer. Jev does not generate text. It returns calibrated probabilities — structured answers that code can branch on directly. It processed 777 judgments in 0.7 seconds for roughly a quarter of a cent. CEO Dan Shipper tested it against Fable 5.1 and found it 25 times faster and 580 times cheaper, catching 6 of 7 deliberately introduced defects versus Fable's 7 of 7. The accuracy gap exists. But at a quarter-cent per 777 judgments, running checks multiple times during a task costs nothing. The alternative, as Taylor noted, is not checking at all. Jev turns the checking tax from a personnel cost into an infrastructure cost. CO/AI framed the shift bluntly: stop shopping for a bigger brain, and start building the layer that verifies the model's work. If you cannot write what "correct" looks like as a score, you are not ready to automate the task.

The infrastructure for memory and context that agents need is maturing alongside the checking layer. ByteByteGo's deep dive into LLM memory clarified the distinction that confuses many developers: the model has no persistent memory. The surrounding application maintains and re-injects conversation history. The context window is a working-memory budget that must fit system instructions, tool definitions, conversation history, retrieved documents, and output space. Long conversations become expensive — across 10 requests, approximately 55K tokens of input are processed despite the visible conversation containing only 10K tokens. Context rot degrades recall as the window fills. The memory techniques — sliding windows, summarization, structured entity extraction — are application-layer engineering, not model properties. The same ByteByteGo article on RAG pipelines made the point from the retrieval side: similarity does not equal correctness, and metadata filtering is essential to ensure retrieved passages are actually eligible evidence, not just semantically close. An agent that cannot maintain context or retrieve the right information cannot be dispatched effectively, no matter how capable the model.

Regulators face their own version of the checking tax. [The accountability gap](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-accountability-gap) traced how the EU's AI Act was built for models that produce outputs, not agents that take actions. UK parliamentarians warned that existing laws are ill-equipped for autonomous AI, and OpenAI's chief scientist publicly stated that "no one is prepared" for the pace of progress. The regulatory checking infrastructure does not exist yet, and the companies building agents are writing their own rules. 72% of healthcare organizations run AI agents that IT never approved. OpenAI's own agents dumped 2,000 malware packages onto RubyGems before anyone caught it. The dispatch problem is not just about organizing work — it is about controlling what agents do when the work goes wrong.

## The delegation threshold

Computer use is the capability that changes the delegation threshold. Mike Taylor, writing in Every, upgraded his assessment of GPT-6 Astra from "wouldn't use it daily" to "starting to believe Astra is AGI" in less than two weeks, driven entirely by computer use — the ability to click, type, and scroll through real applications. He had Astra build six presentations for a prompt engineering course in Google Slides, adding screenshots and making hundreds of tiny edits. He gave it a tedious assignment: checking every link in PDF proofs of his new book. Astra opened each destination, verified it matched, and compiled a spreadsheet of problems. Every staffer Randy Counsman has Codex submit follow-up maintenance requests while cooking. Brian Dell has Codex maintain his family calendar, calling it the highest-leverage thing he has done in his adult life. Becky Isjwara uses Codex to browse iPhone photos, identify sellable items, and list them on marketplaces.

The tasks being outsourced are not intellectually hard. They are annoying enough that humans routinely postpone them. Filling school forms, maintaining calendars, clearing WhatsApp storage, listing items for sale — the bureaucratic friction of modern life that computer use can eliminate. When an agent can operate the same applications you use, the barrier to delegation drops. The threshold moves from "I should probably do that" to "I'll ask the agent to do that." But the checking tax applies here too. Someone has to verify that the school form was filled correctly, that the calendar entry is accurate, that the marketplace listing has the right price. [The constraint premium](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-constraint-premium) described how constraints improve output quality — the deliberate pause before asking, the personal benchmark that narrows the lens. The checking tax is a constraint that most organizations have not yet accepted.

Gemini 3.8 Live Extended Thinking addresses a different friction point: the silent wait. Current voice agents follow a rigid turn-taking pattern — the user speaks, the agent goes silent while processing, then responds. Gemini 3.8 Live speaks while it reasons, acknowledging the user, keeping the conversation moving, and performing slower cognitive tasks in the background. A developer built a live insurance claim agent that can see, talk, think, and draw in real time, with mid-call language switching to Hindi handled seamlessly. Voice agents are crossing from demo to production, which means the dispatch problem extends to voice: an agent that speaks while reasoning can handle complex, multi-step workflows, but someone needs to verify the reasoning was sound.

Anthropic's launch of Claude for Financial Advisors, distributed by Schwab and BlackRock to 16,000+ independent RIAs, shows where the dispatch problem leads. The product is not a chatbot for wealth managers — it is a complete product built around a single job title, shipped as a plugin for Cowork, Anthropic's agentic desktop. Three vertical launches in the same week signal Anthropic's platform strategy: building complete vertical products on top of Cowork rather than offering a horizontal chatbot. The vertical product is an answer to the dispatch problem: instead of organizing your workspace for a general-purpose agent, the vendor organizes the workflow for you. The checking tax gets baked into the product.

The creative dimension of the dispatch problem showed up in an unexpected place. An Every team member spent a weekend playing with AI — building imaginary creatures from writing skills, turning columns into Disney characters — and the distraction produced a more useful result than her planned deliverable. The insight about reader needs that improved her writing system came through a side door, not through structured delegation. The dispatch problem assumes you know what to dispatch. Sometimes the valuable work is the work you did not plan to dispatch.

The dispatch problem will separate organizations the same way the eval loop already separates sovereigns from tenants. [The sovereigns and the tenants](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-sovereigns-and-the-tenants) traced how companies with proprietary data and an honest eval loop are building in-house AI while everyone else rents. The dispatch problem adds a layer: even the sovereigns need a checking infrastructure, and the cost of building one is the real barrier to agent adoption. Companies that invest in workspace architecture, checking infrastructure, and memory systems will dispatch agents at a scale and reliability that companies relying on ad-hoc prompting cannot match. The 1,393 agents on Hermes worked because someone planned the work, sharded the codebase, and verified the results. Without that organizational layer, 1,393 agents produce 1,393 unverified changes. The capability is real. The cost is modest. The overhead is everything.

The checking tax has a floor. Glean's data — 6.5 of 11 saved hours going back to oversight — suggests that even with perfect automation, more than half the time saved gets reinvested in verification. Jev and tools like it push the floor down by turning checking from a human cost into an infrastructure cost. But the floor exists, and organizations that pretend it does not are the ones Gartner predicts will be dead by 2027. The Anthropic IPO — potentially the largest in history at a $2 trillion valuation, with $65 billion in annualized revenue — puts a public price on the frontier model market. But the companies that benefit most may not be the model labs. They may be the ones that solve the dispatch problem: the checking infrastructure, the workspace architecture, the organizational design that turns agent capability into reliable output.
