# The accountability gap

*When AI moves from suggesting to acting, the framework for who answers breaks down — and the companies building the agents are the ones writing the rules.*

The EU's AI Act came into force with a clear assumption: AI systems produce outputs, and the organizations deploying those outputs are responsible for them. That assumption lasted about as long as it took autonomous agents to go from demo to mainstream deployment. As Sifted reported this week, UK parliamentarians and frontier AI builders are now sounding alarms that the regulatory framework was built for a world where AI generates text, not a world where AI takes actions. The central question — if an agent acts autonomously, who is accountable? — has no answer in current law, and the gap widens every time a new agent capability ships.

This is not a regulatory problem alone. The same week EU lawmakers discovered their framework does not fit agents, the companies building those agents were making moves that compound the accountability problem. Dario Amodei published an essay arguing AI companies should deliberately slow model improvement, endorsed within hours by Sam Altman and Elon Musk. As CO/AI reported, the real story behind this unprecedented alignment is not safety but economics: $32 trillion in annual U.S. spending on intelligence and labor. The labs cannot justify their valuations selling tokens, so they need to own outcomes. "Pace the frontier" freezes the field while they climb from tokens to outcomes, with safety as the public justification and market capture as the private strategy. The White House AI czar called it a cartel. The label stuck because the economics are plain.

## The convergence of ownership and rulemaking

The accountability gap has a structural dimension that makes it harder to close. OpenAI launched ChatGPT for Financial Services this week, pairing GPT-6 Astra with premium hosted data from Daloopa, PitchBook, LSEG, and Crunchbase. Morgan Stanley and Evercore helped build the workflows. As Linas's Newsletter reported, OpenAI is no longer just a model provider — it is becoming a data platform, competing with Bloomberg and FactSet for the information layer that financial decisions run on. The company that builds the agent also owns the data the agent reasons over. When the agent makes a mistake, the accountability question runs through infrastructure that a single company controls.

This convergence repeats across the industry. Meta acquired Stilla to complete its agentic commerce loop — Muse for the buyer, Business Agent for the seller, Stilla for post-sale operations. The acquisition was also a trust admission: Muse had safeguard failures, and enterprises needed Stilla's permission model before they would let agents touch real transactions. The company that builds the agent, owns the agent platform, and now owns the trust architecture that governs agent behavior. Accountability in this setup is not a regulatory question — it is a vendor relationship.

The legaltech consolidation covered by Sifted tells the same story from a different angle. Lexroom acquired companies in Bulgaria and France to expand across five European markets, entering a consolidation race where Legora ($5.6 billion valuation) and Harvey ($15.5 billion) are on buying sprees. The CEO of Lexroom called it a "land grab moment." When the companies that build AI legal tools also own the legal markets those tools serve, the accountability for errors, biases, or hallucinated citations sits inside a corporate structure that optimizes for market share, not public oversight.

## Building internal accountability where external frameworks do not exist

While regulators struggle to catch up, companies are building their own accountability systems. ByteByteGo's guide to LLM evaluation this week laid out the problem with unusual clarity: traditional software tests assume deterministic outputs, but LLMs generate different responses to the same input. Health is not pass or fail — it is multidimensional, covering accuracy, safety, speed, reliability, and cost. The evaluation stack ByteByteGo describes — golden datasets, automated metrics, LLM-as-a-judge, human review, production monitoring — is essentially a private regulatory framework. No external body mandates it. Companies build it because the cost of not building it is worse.

Every's editorial team is doing something similar at a smaller scale. They are building personal benchmarks — custom evaluations — for every employee, testing whether the AI tools they use actually improve their work. This is accountability at the individual level: instead of trusting the model's benchmarks, you build your own. The connection to the regulatory gap is direct. If the EU cannot hold agents accountable for their actions, the responsibility falls to the organizations deploying them, and those organizations are discovering they need evaluation infrastructure that looks less like a test suite and more like a continuous monitoring system.

The personal dimension showed up in two more Every pieces this week. One author found that hitting AI usage limits forced her to save big questions for deliberate "consulting the oracle" sessions, and that the constraints improved her thinking quality — she drew a parallel to the 1960s, when programmers waited their turn on mainframes and the waiting imposed a discipline that immediate access later removed. Another discovered that playing with AI — building imaginary worlds, turning columns into Disney characters — generated more useful professional insights than her planned deliverable, because the distraction surfaced the right questions about reader needs that structured planning had missed. Both pieces point to the same uncomfortable reality: the person most accountable for what AI produces is the person using it, and that accountability works better when it is deliberate, not automatic. The SpaceXAI designers profiled by Lenny's Newsletter demonstrated the same principle in creative work. They offloaded tedious design tasks to narrow, specialized agents — a self-updating personal website, a Figma assistant that takes voice memos from the gym — and shifted their own role toward judgment and creative direction. The agent does the work, but the human owns the outcome.

## The attribution problem

The accountability gap has one more dimension that makes it genuinely novel as a policy problem, one that traditional liability frameworks were never designed to handle. CO/AI's piece on the 25th anniversary of 9/11 drew a parallel between the $400K that 19 terrorists spent and the cost ceiling for AI-enabled attacks today. The more immediate finding came from an Anthropic abuse report: "sophistication has stopped being a reliable signal of who is behind an operation." A lone hacktivist executed an operation initially attributed to a nation-state. When AI agents can coordinate to hack infrastructure, run influence operations, and mimic state-level capabilities, attribution — the foundation of accountability — breaks down. You cannot hold someone responsible if you cannot identify them.

Big Technology's Alex Kantrowitz tried to bring some sanity to the broader risk conversation this week, arguing that the debate has polarized into unhelpful extremes. The "10% extinction risk" claims are speculative, not evidence-based. But dismissing all warnings as marketing ignores that they actively harm labs' commercial interests — AI researchers speaking about risk can trigger data center pauses and policy hearings that dent IPO momentum. The nuance Kantrowitz surfaces is that the slowdown call is simultaneously a genuine safety concern and an economic strategy. Both can be true. The accountability gap is not just about bad actors; it is about the fact that the same organizations are simultaneously raising alarms and profiting from the alarm-raising.

The agentic transition playbook from Linas, based on Bessemer's study of 20+ engineering organizations, shows the gap widening inside companies. Two engineers running AI agent fleets can outpace a 50-person R&D organization. Salesforce spends roughly $300 million on Anthropic tokens while freezing engineering hires. When output concentrates this brutally — the top 1% of engineers ship 46 times the median's AI-written code — the accountability for what ships moves from a team to an individual running agents. The organizational structures that distributed responsibility across reviewers, managers, and QA processes are being compressed into single-person workflows. The [land grab](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-land-grab) described how companies are buying infrastructure instead of betting on models. The accountability gap is the consequence: when one company owns the model, the data, the agent platform, and the trust architecture, the accountability for what the agent does sits inside a balance sheet, not a legal framework.

The accountability gap will close, but the question is who closes it and on whose terms. The EU is trying, but its framework was designed for outputs, not actions, and the agents already in production have moved past it. Companies are building internal evaluation systems, but those systems are private and optimized for commercial outcomes, not public safety. Individuals are developing personal accountability through constraints, benchmarks, and deliberate engagement, but that approach does not scale to the agent acting on someone's behalf in a financial transaction or a legal proceeding. The [approval economy](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-approval-economy) showed how trust and human judgment become the product when capability is commoditized. The accountability gap is the frame that connects that insight to the regulatory and economic structures above it: when you own the infrastructure and control the approval, you also own the accountability — whether you want it or not. The companies building the agents are writing the rules, building the evaluation systems, and owning the infrastructure. The rest of us are left to decide whether that arrangement is a feature or a bug, and how long the gap stays open before someone fills it.
