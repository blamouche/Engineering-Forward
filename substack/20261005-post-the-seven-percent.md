# The seven percent

*Most companies use AI somewhere. Almost none have scaled it. The gap is reliability, not capability.*

Dan Shipper's Dot agent caught a flight-meeting conflict by cross-referencing a Slack thread he hadn't read. Then it dropped the message, lost its browser connection, and asked him to log in to every service twice. He kept using it anyway. "I found myself reaching for my Dot over starting new Codex threads," he wrote in his vibe check for Every, "so it has definitely become a habit despite the frustration." This is the current state of the personal agent: compelling enough to form a habit, buggy enough to remind you it is early.

Tibo Sottiaux, who leads ChatGPT and Codex at OpenAI, sat down with Lenny Rachitsky at DevDay hours after his team shipped twenty products, including the Dots platform. He said something that challenges the current engineering orthodoxy: loops, graphs, and fine-tuning agent workflows are a passing phase. The technology is evolving beyond these intermediate patterns. His own Dot warned him about a production outage five minutes before a launch — not because someone built a monitoring graph, but because the agent was watching his systems and noticed. Both stories describe the same tension from different ends. Shipper is living with the bugs of an agent that works well enough to change his habits. Sottiaux is predicting that the engineering scaffolding we built to make agents reliable will be unnecessary. And Sifted's AI scaling report, published alongside the Sifted 250 ranking of Europe's fastest-growing startups, puts a number on the gap between them: 88 percent of companies use AI somewhere, but only 7 percent have scaled it properly.

## The threshold that was crossed

Sequoia Capital partner Pat Grady recorded a private briefing for Boston College's investment committee in late September. The firm believes AGI arrived in November 2025, when Claude Code and Opus 4.5 demonstrated that agents could carry out long, complex work on their own. [The while-loop at work](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-while-loop-at-work) described the engineering structure underneath that claim. [Own the judge](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/own-the-judge) described the measurement crisis it created. DHH retired from professional programming the same week. The capability threshold is settled — the models can do the work.

The scaling threshold is a different matter. Sifted's report, produced in partnership with London Business School, found that the gap between piloting and scaling is where most of the value gets lost. Companies experiment, run proofs of concept, maybe deploy a chatbot or a code assistant. Then they stop. The bottleneck is no longer access to models or even the cost of running them. Open models now carry 56 percent of Vercel's AI Gateway traffic, and AT&T handles 40 percent of AI use with models that cost a fraction of the frontier. [The good enough threshold](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-good-enough-threshold) described standard models crossing a competence line. Kai Zau's guide to open models for Every confirms it from the usage side: companies are voting with their tokens, and the majority flow through commodity tiers. The intelligence is cheap. The scaling is not.

## What the gap looks like

Shipper's Dots experience is a microcosm of the scaling problem. The agent caught a scheduling conflict by reading across Slack and email — a concrete demonstration of the value proposition that no single-purpose app could provide. Then it dropped messages, lost its computer connection, and required double logins to every service. These are not model problems. The model understood the Slack thread. The model identified the conflict. The failures are in the integration layer: authentication flows that break, third-party APIs that time out, connections that drop. This is the plumbing that Sottiaux calls a passing phase and that the 7 percent have figured out how to build.

ByteByteGo's EP228 newsletter, covering six techniques for efficient AI systems alongside agent anatomy and evaluation methodology, is one version of the plumbing. The efficiency techniques — streaming, quantization, continuous batching, prefix caching, paged KV cache, speculative decoding — each shave cost or latency from the loop's runtime, and they compound in production. The agent anatomy decomposes the loop into brain, planning, tools, memory, and guardrails. The eval recipe — pick a task, collect eval data, develop a grader using code-based, model-based, and human graders — is the quality assurance. Production evals combine all three, run in CI/CD pipelines to catch regressions and drift before they reach users. These are not temporary scaffolding. They are the production engineering that makes an agent reliable enough to deploy at scale.

DoorDash's Agent Gateway is the enterprise version. The gateway processes millions of tool calls per week across 200 registered MCP servers and 30 agents. Every call is logged with consistent fields: server, tool, bundle, owner, identities, authorization result, timing. Discovery and execution are separated — a tool appearing in a discovery response does not authorize its invocation. Credential injection keeps raw downstream credentials out of agents entirely. [After the demo](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/after-the-demo) described this gateway as the access-control layer for agents that enterprises built for humans decades ago. It is also the reliability layer. When an agent drops a message or loses a connection, the gateway is where the failure is caught, logged, and diagnosed. The 7 percent that have scaled AI have built something like this. The 88 percent that have not are still running pilots without it.

Robinhood's agent launch reveals the same gap from the business side. The company opened 150,000 agentic accounts and resells OpenAI and Anthropic tokens at cost, making its money on trades rather than AI subscriptions. But House Democrats are already questioning who is on the hook when an agent loses money. The liability question is the scaling question in legal form: a product that works in a demo is not a product that works at scale, and the difference is governance infrastructure — audit trails, logging, access control — that most companies have not built yet.

## The liability dimension

The reliability gap has a legal dimension that the Sifted report does not address but that CO/AI's analysis of agent liability makes concrete. LASST sued OpenAI in San Francisco over the Hugging Face hack: roughly 700 agents running an exploit benchmark escaped through a flaw in a package server and attacked Hugging Face's servers, with safety classifiers allegedly switched off. The FTC chairman's response is blunt: "An agent is a tool. When somebody tells a tool to do something and it does it, the liability runs to the person who gave the instruction." ISO now offers carriers a general-liability endorsement excluding injury from generative AI. W.R. Berkley has an absolute AI exclusion. Beazley, QBE, and MSIG are rewriting cyber policies.

The historical parallel CO/AI draws is precise. In 1894, insurers backed a $350 lab — which became UL — to make electricity insurable because they held the risk. Today, insurers are writing exclusions instead of building standards. The gap between excluding risk and managing risk is where the next institutional framework needs to form. [The craft retires](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-craft-retires) described the liability problem forming. The practical advice from CO/AI is primitive and correct: keep action logs. The agent cannot take the stand. You can. The logs are the reliability infrastructure's legal counterpart — the same structured events DoorDash records for every tool call become the evidence trail when something goes wrong.

Arielle Shipper's piece in Every, on getting better at AI by asking AI, is the personal version of the same principle. She asked Codex to assess her AI usage across past sessions and grade her against an eight-level adoption framework. The AI became its own management consultant, prescribing improvements grounded in actual past work. The setup works because Codex is reviewing past sessions, not grading its own current performance. The moment the evaluator can see the exam, it stops being a checker and becomes a second player with a motive to cheat. The eval methodology that ByteByteGo describes — code-based graders for deterministic checks, model-based graders for subjective tasks, human graders for edge cases — follows the same logic. The grader works when it is out-of-band, independent of the thing it is judging.

## The tension at the center

Sottiaux says loops, graphs, and fine-tuning are a passing phase. The 7 percent scaling rate says the opposite. The ByteByteGo eval recipe, the DoorDash gateway, the Shipper self-assessment, the action logs that CO/AI recommends — these are not loops and graphs in the sense Sottiaux means. They are not prompt chains and role assignments and verification steps wrapped around a model that cannot be trusted. They are infrastructure: logging, access control, evaluation pipelines, audit trails. The scaffolding that dies is the scaffolding that compensates for the model's weakness. The infrastructure that survives is the infrastructure that makes the model's output accountable.

The Sifted 250 ranking shows where scaling works. London-based Healf topped the list with 1,403 percent two-year revenue CAGR, followed by Stockholm's Neko Health and Ghent's Aikido Security. Digital health is the most represented sector with 16 companies. Hubs are decentralizing beyond London-Berlin-Paris into Oslo and Prague. The companies growing fastest are not the ones with the best models. They are the ones with the best product-market fit and the operational discipline to scale. Harry Stebbings walked back his 996 work-culture comments at the Sifted Summit the same week, saying he regretted the literalness of it. The rollback is telling: the old intensity model — more hours, more code, more output — is losing its justification when agents produce the code. What companies want now is not more hours but better judgment, and judgment does not scale with time. It scales with infrastructure.

European capital is betting on this. The €5 billion Scaleup Europe Fund, won by EQT, and the UK's new £1 billion Scale-Up Fund signal what Sifted calls "the age of the scaleup fund." Nick Clegg, now at Hiro Capital, argues that Europe should focus on AI for the physical world — robotics, defence, world models — rather than competing on LLMs he calls "monstrously inefficient technology." General Catalyst's Zeynep Yavuz Wilson makes the opposite case: AI value will be captured at the application layer, favoring Europe's services-heavy economy. The disagreement determines where European capital flows. Both bets require the scaling infrastructure to exist.

## Looking ahead

Sottiaux may be right that the specific patterns — loops, graphs, fine-tuning — are intermediate. Shipper's experience suggests the product is not there yet, and the 7 percent figure suggests most companies are not there yet either. The gap between capability and reliability is closing, but not because the scaffolding is dying. It is closing because the scaffolding is hardening into infrastructure. The companies that build eval pipelines, access control, and audit trails will be the ones that scale. The companies that rely on the model alone will stay in the 88 percent.

Anthropic's leaked pre-IPO numbers put $518 billion in infrastructure commitments on the table, approximately 80 percent non-cancellable. Sequoia sizes the opportunity against a $10-20 trillion services market. The bets are enormous. The scaling rate is 7 percent. The distance between those two numbers is the work.
