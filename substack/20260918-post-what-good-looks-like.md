# What good looks like

*When the model can build anything you describe, the scarce skill is describing it — and most teams haven't practiced.*

Tulsee Doshi has run product for Google's Gemini models for years, and the advice she gives to product managers is not about models at all. "Take twenty prompts," she says. Twenty examples where a win and a loss are obvious. That exercise — forcing a vague ask into something specific enough to align a room of researchers — is the hardest thing her team does. Not training the model. Not shipping the product. Saying, precisely, what good looks like.

This is the specification problem, and it is the bottleneck hiding behind every agent deployment, every model comparison, and every token budget debate. The capability exists. Google's Dream-RSI replays a model's own past runs and comes out 162 times more efficient. Gemini 3.8 Live speaks while it reasons. OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra operates Google Slides with hundreds of edits. The models can do the work. The question is whether anyone has described the work precisely enough for the model to do it right.

## The skill nobody trained for

Doshi's conversation with Nikhyl Singhal, published in Lenny's Newsletter, reveals that product management at a frontier AI lab is a different job from consumer or enterprise PM. No customer hands the team a requirements document. The team decides what the model should get better at, then describes it precisely enough for researchers to build toward. Benchmarks do not capture what a model is like to talk to — Doshi calls that gap "vibes" and treats it as product territory. A standing set of prompts, from asking for a joke to running an agentic task, gets tested against each new model and the competition. The scarce skill across the entire operation is not technical depth. It is the ability to say what good looks like in terms concrete enough to hill-climb toward.

Matt Pocock, interviewed on The Pragmatic Engineer, discovered the same principle from a different direction. Pocock, creator of the widely-used "grill-me" skill and the Total TypeScript course, noticed that AI agents try to build software layer by layer, which causes bugs between the layers. Reading The Pragmatic Programmer, he found the concept of "tracer bullets" — a technique for implementing a "golden path" through a system. When he told the agent to use "tracer bullets," the agent started producing better code. He now reads classic software engineering books to find what he calls "leading words" — concepts from Domain-Driven Design, A Philosophy of Software Design, and other foundational texts — that guide agents efficiently.

The bridge between Doshi and Pocock is specification by vocabulary. Doshi uses twenty prompts to force a vague ask into something specific. Pocock uses "leading words" from classic texts to give the agent a precise mental model of what to build. Both are solving the same problem: the gap between what you want and what you say. That gap has always existed in software engineering — requirements documents were never perfect — but agents make it expensive in a new way. A human developer fills in ambiguity with judgment. An agent fills it with whatever the model predicts. If you cannot describe what good looks like, the agent cannot produce it.

## The cost of ambiguity

Every's Dan Shipper consumes more than three times as many tokens as the next-highest user at the company. Rather than treating that as waste, Shipper sees low token spend as a smoke signal that teams are thinking too small. Head of video Randy Counsman burned 4.5 billion OpenAI tokens trying to make a 3D model of his face with Astra. The result was "very janky." But the failure revealed how his agent setup created a compute-hungry "unruly swarm" — an orchestrator deploying unlimited subagents that passed growing context back and forth even for simple status checks. He now has a benchmark and a leaner setup. The expensive failure was a specification exercise: spending tokens to discover what the system could not do, and then describing the failure precisely enough to fix it.

The token spend debate connects to the specification problem in a way that most coverage missed. Marcus Moretti's model routing strategy at Spiral is to match model to task: Sonnet for basic checks, Opus for scoped changes, Fable 5.1 for larger features. That routing decision is a specification — he has to describe the task's complexity precisely enough to know which model to assign. Every is building personal benchmarks for every employee, testing which models handle recurring tasks. The benchmark is a specification artifact: it says, for this specific task, here is what good looks like, and here is which model produces it. The companies building this infrastructure are practicing specification at scale. The companies that are not are the ones buying a bigger model and hoping.

The developer spend data tells the same story from the market side. OpenAI overtook Anthropic in developer AI spend on OpenRouter for the first time in 133 weeks. The reversal took two weeks. Linas Beliūnas noted that developer loyalty at the API layer is far more fluid than enterprise contracts — developers route through OpenRouter precisely because they can switch models without changing code, and price-performance ratios drive routing decisions. The implication is stark: developers will move to whichever model best fits their specification of "good," and that specification is measured not in benchmarks but in the work itself. The model is swappable. The specification is the asset.

## The self-improving specification

Google shipped Dream-RSI the same week, and it adds a wrinkle that changes the specification problem. Dream-RSI replays a model's own past runs, keeps the good moves, and dumps the bad ones — achieving up to 162x fewer agent calls. It is recursive self-improvement: the machine reliving the day until it is better at it. CO/AI noted that self-improving AI went from existential risk boogeyman to product category in a single day — a Chinese startup raised $50M for self-evolving models, 35 researchers published a five-level RSI roadmap, and AMD posted a job req for a "Recursive Self Improvement" scientist.

The wrinkle is this: Dream-RSI is the model specifying itself. It replays its own runs and learns which moves were good. The specification of "good" comes from the model's own experience, not from a human describing it. Richard Sutton, who wrote the textbook on reinforcement learning, is building something that runs on 20 watts and never stops learning, rewriting its own weights on your data, on your machine. The frontier labs are chasing a giant that runs on gigawatts. Sutton is building one that runs on a light bulb. The contrast matters for the specification problem: if the model can improve itself by replaying its own runs, the human specification becomes a starting point, not a continuous input. The companies that invest in saying what good looks like — the Doshi twenty prompts, the Pocock leading words, the Every personal benchmarks — are building the specification infrastructure that self-improving models will optimize against. The companies that skip that step will get self-improving models that optimize for the wrong thing.

Inherent, the London neolab profiled by Sifted, takes the specification problem to its organizational extreme. Founded by three DeepMind alumni, the company treats its own organizational design as a research experiment. Employees wear headsets and narrate their stream of consciousness while working — "I'm just waiting for this experiment to run, so now I'm checking Twitter" — creating a dataset of human-AI interaction that feeds the lab's research. Other experiments under consideration include abandoning conventional desks, hiring a linguist to determine the "right" language for communicating with agents, and bringing in a Hollywood set designer to rethink the office. The headset recordings are a specification exercise at a scale no traditional company would attempt: capturing the full texture of how humans think and work, so that the scientific agent platform Faraday can be pointed at the right questions. Inherent published a paper claiming Faraday beat Anthropic's and OpenAI's models at independently reproducing published scientific findings. The specification of "good" was not answering questions faster but figuring out which questions are worth asking.

## The engineering floor

The specification problem has an engineering floor that keeps it from being purely abstract. ByteByteGo's guide to production database migrations lays out the tension: schema changes need exclusive access to tables, but production needs continuous availability. Every intermediate step must work while ordinary business continues. The three strategies — shadow table swaps, expand-contract, and change data capture — are specification patterns. Each one defines what "done" looks like at each stage: dual-write, backfill, dual-read, cutover, verify. Stripe's variant adds an explicit verification step between each phase. GitHub built gh-ost after trigger-based tools caused deadlocks on tables with billions of rows. Slack built double-read diffing because dual-write alone caught fewer bugs than expected. The migration patterns are specification patterns: describe each step precisely enough that a failure at any step is detectable and reversible.

Pocock's "memento-driven development" reframes code quality as a specification problem. Since agents start fresh every session without memory, codebases need to be healthier than ever — optimized for readability by someone seeing the code for the first time. "Imagine you have a human who wakes up every morning and cannot remember who they are, like the guy from Memento," Pocock says. "We are trying to optimize our codebases for new starters, so we want the healthiest codebase we've ever had." The codebase is a specification. A clean codebase specifies what the system does clearly enough that an agent with no prior context can contribute. A messy codebase leaves the agent to guess, and the guess is the model's prediction, not your intent. The fundamentals that software engineering has been pushing for decades — clear naming, good architecture, clean separation of concerns — turn out to be the specification layer that agents need most.

Vercel's sales automation, detailed by CO/AI, shows the specification problem at the organizational level. The company reduced its inbound sales team from 10 to 1.25 people, with 90% automation and a 32x return. But Glean's survey of 6,000 workers found that automation saves 11 hours per week while 6.5 of those hours go back into checking, correcting, and re-feeding the machine. Net saving: under half the headline. String 10 agent steps at 95% reliability each and the chain finishes clean only about 60% of the time. The oversight cost is the specification cost: someone has to verify that the machine's output meets a standard, and that standard has to be written down. If you cannot write what "correct" looks like as a score, you are not ready to automate the task. That is a specification statement.

## The specification that compounds

The specification problem does not solve itself. Doshi's twenty prompts are a practice, not a deliverable. Pocock's leading words are a reading habit, not a library. Every's personal benchmarks are a continuous process, not a one-time setup. The specification of what good looks like is not a document you write and forget. It is a discipline you practice, and the practice compounds. Each iteration makes the next specification sharper. Each benchmark makes the next comparison clearer. Each failed experiment — like Counsman's 4.5 billion tokens — refines the specification of what the system can and cannot do.

The European funding stories tell the same story from the capital side. Mistral secured a multi-year €100M deal with TotalEnergies to build models for reservoir exploration. Euclyd raised a €200M Series A to make AI more efficient. Exein raised $270M for cybersecurity. Jack Parker-Holder, a DeepMind researcher, is nearing a $700M fundraise for Emulate, a new London neolab targeting a $4B valuation. The capital is flowing toward companies that can specify what they are building — efficient AI infrastructure, domain-specific models, scientific agents — not toward companies selling general-purpose intelligence by the sip.

The specification premium will separate companies the same way the eval loop already separates sovereigns from tenants. [The dispatch problem](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-dispatch-problem) described the organizational challenge of routing work to agents. The specification problem is upstream: you cannot dispatch what you cannot define. [The constraint premium](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-constraint-premium) described how constraints improve output quality. The specification is the constraint — the twenty prompts, the leading words, the personal benchmark, the migration pattern. [The half-life of what you know](https://engineeringforward.substack.com/p/the-half-life-of-what-you-know) described how fundamentals compound while tool-specific expertise depreciates. The skill of saying what good looks like is a fundamental. It compounds with every prompt, every benchmark, every migration plan. The models will keep improving. The question is whether the humans directing them can describe what they want precisely enough for the improvement to matter.
