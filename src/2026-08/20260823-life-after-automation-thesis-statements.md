## Synthesis
Every's Thesis Statements project tackles a question that has become unavoidable: when execution is cheap and intelligence is abundant, what's left for people to do? Rather than offering another abstract essay on the future of work, Every collected 100 specific, contestable predictions from AI leaders who are actively building the automation frontier — and committed to revisiting them later to see which actually came true.

The first 25 predictions are live and reveal a range of perspectives. Linear CEO Karri Saarinen argues that AI's biggest problem will be design — as execution commoditizes, the challenge shifts to deciding what to build and how it should feel. Ness Labs founder Anne-Laure Le Cunff inverts the traditional knowledge-work problem: answers will become abundant and questions will become the hard part, making question-asking the scarce skill. Granola cofounder Chris Pedregal offers an optimistic counterpoint: some of your hardest problems will simply solve themselves as AI capabilities expand.

The project is tied to Every's Thesis: 2027 conference in November, with new statements released weekly until the event. The commitment to revisit predictions — to check which actually panned out — is what distinguishes this from the usual round of speculative essays. By making predictions specific and contestable, Every creates a falsifiable record that can be evaluated over time.

The project also reflects Every's own frontier team structure: a small group given explicit permission to prioritize weird experiments over urgent work, on the theory that odd experiments are often how important discoveries happen. This organizational choice — protecting experimentation time even as the company grows to nearly 30 people — is itself a prediction about what matters in the age of automation.
