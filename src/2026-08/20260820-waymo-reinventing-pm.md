# To Invent Waymo, They Had to Reinvent PM
**Source**: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/cp/212081371
**Date**: 2026-08-20
**Author**: Nikhyl Singhal / The Skip Podcast
**Keywords**: Waymo, product management, autonomous driving, evaluation, AI products, leadership, career development, Saswat Panigrahi

## Elevator pitch
Waymo's chief product officer Saswat Panigrahi spent a decade on a problem you couldn't A/B test — and the product management practices the team invented to ship autonomous driving are becoming the playbook for anyone building AI products with year-long feedback loops.

## Takeaways
- Waymo built evaluation as a product in its own right, with dedicated eval PMs whose entire job is to increase metric fidelity so other teams can hill-climb — a pattern now relevant to any team shipping AI products where "what good looks like" can't be fully specified in advance.
- The mission was "semi-spiritual" (40,000 US road deaths/year) but the execution was brutally concrete checkpoints: "remove the test driver from just one car" unpacks into checkable weekly milestones.
- Leadership on impossible problems strips down to three traits: competence (specialists should be surprised you know their field), courage (run toward the biggest problem without waiting for planning cycles), and compassion (kill bets publicly with heroes named).
- Daily curiosity compounded is the real career moat: 1% per day compounds to 37x per year, and the difference between 20 years of experience and 1 year repeated 20 times is whether you pushed to failure every day.
- Waymo staffed PMs for range — PhDs in photonics who ran businesses, NASA safety people colliding with internet-era product people — because no single person spans the whole problem.

## Synthesis
Waymo's decade-long journey from Google research project to 220 million autonomous miles offers a masterclass in product management for problems that don't fit the traditional sprint-and-measure cycle. The conversation between Nikhyl Singhal and Waymo CPO Saswat Panigrahi reveals how the team had to invent a new PM discipline — one that is now becoming relevant to anyone building AI products.

The most transferable insight is the treatment of evaluation as a first-class product. Traditional product teams get measurement for free: ship the feature, watch the metrics. But when the question is "is the car driving well?" — and defining perfect driving would mean you've already solved the problem — you need a different approach. Waymo staffed dedicated eval PMs whose job was to keep the measurement honest, making the evaluator progressively harder as the driver improved. This is exactly the challenge now facing teams building AI agents: specifying what "good" looks like in domains where correct behavior can't be fully articulated in advance.

The leadership framework is equally striking. On a decade-long problem with no traditional milestones, titles became irrelevant. What built followership was competence (being dangerous enough in adjacent fields that specialists are surprised), courage (running at the organization's biggest known problem without waiting for a planning cycle), and compassion (celebrating failed bets publicly so the team stays "elastic with failures"). The insight that every organization already knows its biggest problem — ask the lunch line — but processes bury it in multi-quarter OKR planning, is a sharp critique of corporate dysfunction.

The career advice is practical and contrarian. Saswat's formula of 1.01^365 compounded over 20 years — pushing some skill to the point of failure every day — reframes experience as a daily habit rather than a calendar event. Waymo made this cultural: leaders present "what I learned last week" with almost no prep, treating learning as the one constant the team celebrates. The hiring screen listens for hunger paired with tool flexibility, because next year's tools will have been tried by the curious before anyone asks.
