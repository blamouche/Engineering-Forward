# The Case for Cloning Your Coworkers
**Source**: https://every.to/context-window/the-case-for-cloning-your-coworkers
**Date**: 2026-08-26
**Author**: Laura Entis / Every
**Keywords**: AI agents, expertise cloning, KateBench, Codex, self-improving skills, GPT-5.6, Walleye Capital, AI fluency

## Elevator pitch
Every is building agent clones of its team members' expertise—starting with a copyediting agent trained on 30,000 historical edits—turning individual judgment into a company-wide resource that compounds over time.

## Takeaways
- Every CEO Dan Shipper built KateBench, a copyediting agent trained on editor-in-chief Kate Lee's 30,000+ historical edits, using GPT-5.6; the agent now runs a first pass before human review, with tracked changes feeding back into Codex to self-improve the skill
- The next step is cloning expertise across the company: packaging UX feedback, logistical planning, and social positioning as skills so anyone can access a "Kate-level quality pass" without bottlenecks
- Billionaire investor Stanley Druckenmiller publicly admitted using AI to write his WSJ op-ed, sparking debate about whether a byline certifies authorship or ownership of an opinion; the WSJ defended him while the FT has banned columnists from using AI
- A self-improving Codex workflow: whenever the agent makes a mistake, you fix the immediate problem AND feed the error back as a targeted edit to Codex's instructions, preventing the same mistake from recurring
- At Walleye Capital ($10B hedge fund), AI use is mandatory for all 400 employees; CEO Will England views it as non-negotiable, comparing refusing AI to refusing the internet in 1995

## Synthesis
Every is systematically turning its employees' expertise into AI agent skills. The experiment started with KateBench—a copyediting agent built on GPT-5.6 that was trained on more than 30,000 historical edits from editor-in-chief Kate Lee. Before any article is filed, it now runs through KateBench, which produces suggested changes directly in a Google Doc. Human Kate then reviews, accepts, modifies, or rejects those suggestions. The tracked changes catalog her decisions, and Codex rewrites the skill so it compounds over time. The result: anyone on the team can get a Kate-level quality pass without clogging Kate's calendar.

The obvious extension is cloning the rest of the company. Dan Shipper wants to build versions of KateBench for COO Brandon Gell's UX feedback, head of operations Arielle Shipper's logistical brain, and head of social Becky Isjwara's social positioning. The first test is DanLens, a skill being built by Mike Taylor that clones Dan's marketing instincts. The challenge is that UX decisions make for messier datasets than copyedits—the team is still exploring which kinds of work can be treated this way.

Beyond Every, the newsletter covers two broader AI adoption stories. Stanley Druckenmiller admitted he used AI to write his WSJ op-ed criticizing Treasury Secretary Bessent, and rather than apologizing, he said he writes everything with AI now "for the same reason I use a calculator when I do math problems." The WSJ defended him, saying what matters is whether the piece reflects the author's genuine opinion. At Walleye Capital, AI fluency is a job requirement—England sent a firm-wide email that opened with "I used ChatGPT to write this email, you should be using it too, and be proud of it." His point: AI speeds up necessary tasks, freeing people to focus on big-picture questions. "People have this insecurity that if I didn't put my blood, sweat, and tears into it that somehow it's not real," he says. "But at the end of the day, results are what matter."
