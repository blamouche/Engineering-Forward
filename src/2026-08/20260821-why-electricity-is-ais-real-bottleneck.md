# The Power Shift: Why Electricity Is AI's Real Bottleneck
**Source**: https://linas.substack.com/p/why-electricity-is-ais-real-bottleneck
**Date**: 2026-08-18
**Author**: Linas Beliūnas
**Keywords**: AI infrastructure, electricity, data centers, GPU demand, energy, power grid

## Elevator pitch
The AI industry's constraint has shifted from GPU availability to electricity: global data centre power demand is expected to double from 485 TWh in 2025 to 950 TWh by 2030, and securing gigawatts of power now takes longer than securing the chips they would run.

## Takeaways
- Global data centre electricity demand grew 17% in 2025, with AI-focused facilities growing 50%, per the IEA's April 2026 report.
- The IEA projects data centre consumption to roughly double from 485 TWh (2025) to 950 TWh by 2030, driven primarily by AI workloads.
- A new AI campus needs turbines, transformers, grid connections, permits, and community buy-in — several of those inputs take longer to secure than the chips they support.
- The investment thesis is more nuanced than "AI needs electricity, so buy power": turbine makers repriced while power producers did not, and the strongest bear case is duration (long-term commitments) rather than demand.
- Behind-the-meter deals and capacity auctions are reshaping how power is actually priced for AI workloads, creating new financial structures that didn't exist before the AI buildout.

## Synthesis
For two years, the AI industry's supply anxiety centered on silicon — Nvidia allocation lists, TSMC wafer starts, and memory shortages dominated the conversation. This article argues that assumption no longer holds. The practical limit on AI infrastructure has shifted from how many GPUs a company can buy to how many gigawatts it can secure, connect, and afford.

The piece maps the power value chain: turbine makers (who repriced upward), power producers (who mostly didn't), behind-the-meter deals, and capacity auctions. The key insight is that the strongest bear case for power investments isn't demand (which is clearly growing) but duration — companies are committing capital for decades while their largest customers (hyperscalers) can slow spending within a quarter.

The article also covers the political backlash after Texas, the storage and flexibility markets forming in the gap between supply and demand, and the decisions founders, operators, and investors need to make. For the engineering-forward audience, the takeaway is clear: infrastructure planning now needs to account for power procurement timelines that can exceed hardware procurement timelines, and the economics of AI training runs increasingly include electricity as a first-class cost variable alongside GPU-hours.
