# In Defense of AI Writing
**Source**: https://every.to
**Date**: 2026-08-20
**Author**: Laura Entis / Every
**Keywords**: AI writing, content creation, LLMs, thinking, creativity, ghostwriting, Mike Taylor

## Elevator pitch
The common objection that "writing is thinking" and outsourcing drafting to AI means outsourcing thought oversimplifies the relationship between ideas and prose — having something compelling to say doesn't make you a great writer, and being a great writer doesn't guarantee you have something worth reading.

## Takeaways
- Mike Taylor, Every's head of tech consulting, has used AI to ghostwrite most of his published work and personal blog posts, making structural and sectional edits but not generating the majority of text himself.
- The most common objection to AI writing — "writing is thinking" — assumes that drafting is the only way to reason through ideas, but this conflates two distinct skills: having ideas worth sharing and expressing them well.
- Having something compelling to say and being a great writer are independent capabilities: many original thinkers are poor writers, and many skilled writers lack original ideas.
- AI writing tools create a separation between ideation and expression, allowing people with domain expertise but limited writing skills to participate in public discourse.
- The "AI writing is slop" critique, while often valid, fails to distinguish between low-effort AI-generated content and thoughtful human-directed AI writing with substantive editing.

## Synthesis
The debate over AI-assisted writing has become intensely polarized, with critics arguing that outsourcing the drafting process to LLMs means outsourcing the thinking itself. This article pushes back on that framework by pointing out a fundamental disconnect: the assumption that writing and thinking are inseparable ignores the many people who have valuable ideas but lack the craft to express them effectively.

The counterpoint is pragmatic. Mike Taylor's approach — using Claude to draft, then revising structure and sections — represents a middle ground that many AI-assisted writers occupy but few admit publicly. The stigma is strong enough that admitting to AI ghostwriting "makes the internet foam at the mouth," yet the practice is widespread. The article suggests this stigma is misplaced because it conflates two independent capabilities: the ability to generate original ideas worth sharing, and the craft of expressing them in prose.

The deeper point is about access. Traditional writing has been gatekept by craft: if you can't write well, your ideas don't reach an audience, regardless of their merit. AI writing tools disrupt this gatekeeping by separating ideation from expression. This doesn't mean all AI writing is good — much of it is indeed slop — but it means the evaluation criteria should shift from "did you write every word?" to "is the content valuable and is the thinking original?"

The article is ultimately a call for nuance in a debate that has become binary. The question isn't whether AI writing is good or bad, but whether the person directing the AI has something worth saying — and whether the editing process preserves the author's voice and intellectual contribution. The slop problem is real, but the solution isn't to ban AI assistance; it's to develop better discernment between content that uses AI as a tool and content that uses it as a substitute for thought.
