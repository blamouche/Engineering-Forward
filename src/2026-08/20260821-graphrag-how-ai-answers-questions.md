# GraphRAG: How AI Answers Questions Hidden Across Many Documents
**Source**: https://blog.bytebytego.com/p/graphrag-how-ai-answers-questions
**Date**: 2026-08-19
**Author**: ByteByteGo
**Keywords**: GraphRAG, RAG, knowledge graphs, AI retrieval, vector search, community detection

## Elevator pitch
Standard vector-based RAG excels at local queries where the answer resembles the question, but fails on global questions that require reasoning across an entire corpus; GraphRAG bridges that gap by building a knowledge graph with hierarchical community summaries that enable comprehensive, well-sourced answers to both types of questions.

## Takeaways
- Standard RAG relies on similarity search, which assumes answers resemble questions — a solid assumption for local queries but broken for global questions spanning many documents.
- GraphRAG builds a knowledge graph from documents by extracting entities and relationships, then clustering them into communities with hierarchical summaries generated by a language model.
- Graph extraction accounts for roughly 75% of total indexing cost, making it the most expensive stage and the first place to optimize.
- LazyGraphRAG cuts indexing cost to 0.1% of full GraphRAG by deferring language model work to query time, trading precomputation for per-query latency.
- Vector RAG remains stronger for local queries; agentic retrieval (routing between strategies per query) is emerging as the practical approach for systems that must handle both local and global questions.

## Synthesis
GraphRAG addresses a fundamental limitation of vector-based RAG: similarity search returns chunks that share vocabulary with the query, not chunks that collectively answer a question requiring synthesis across a corpus. When someone asks "which failure causes recur most often across all postmortems," the answer exists as a distribution across hundreds of documents, not in any single chunk.

The GraphRAG pipeline has six phases: chunking, entity/relationship extraction, entity merging, claim extraction, community detection (hierarchical Leiden clustering), and embedding. The result is a multi-resolution knowledge graph where each community at each level has a summary report. Local search expands from matched entities into their neighborhoods; global search runs map-reduce across community reports. Microsoft's testing showed GraphRAG outperformed baseline RAG on comprehensiveness, diversity, and supporting source material for global queries, while scoring similarly on faithfulness.

The cost story is significant: the dual language model passes over the entire corpus make indexing expensive. LazyGraphRAG and FastGraphRAG offer cheaper alternatives (deferring work to query time or replacing LLM extraction with traditional NLP), at the cost of more noise or higher per-query latency. The article concludes with agentic RAG, where a routing model selects the retrieval strategy per query — vector search, graph search, SQL, or web search — acknowledging that no single strategy wins for all question types.
