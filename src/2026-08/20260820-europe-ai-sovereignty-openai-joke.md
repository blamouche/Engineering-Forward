## Synthesis
Europe's AI sovereignty debate has been a background conversation for years, but the moment it became tangible was when the US briefly restricted access to Anthropic's latest frontier model. That single act of export control transformed an academic discussion into a national security question: what happens if Europe is denied access to the AI systems that increasingly power businesses, public services, and critical infrastructure?

The answer, according to this Sifted briefing, is that Europe is not prepared. The continent has no shortage of talent — world-class universities, ambitious startups, and deep engineering expertise — but producing a frontier model competitive with OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google requires a combination of compute, energy, and capital that borders on the extraordinary. The economics of frontier AI have moved beyond what any single European nation can fund, and even EU-wide consortiums like the Europa frontier model initiative (which Domyn leads) face structural challenges in matching the scale of US and Chinese investment.

This has forced a deeper reconsideration of what AI sovereignty should actually mean. The binary framing — either Europe builds a frontier model or it's dependent — may be a false choice. An alternative vision focuses on sovereignty through application-layer dominance: controlling the deployment, data, and regulatory infrastructure even if the underlying models come from US labs. This aligns with Europe's regulatory strength (the EU AI Act) and its industrial base in sectors like manufacturing, healthcare, and automotive where AI is applied rather than developed.

The internal AI slop problem is a microcosm of the sovereignty challenge. Startups discovering that uncontrolled AI content creation creates real losses — "you're losing so much" — mirrors the macro risk of a continent that consumes AI it doesn't control. The lesson is that sovereignty isn't just about building models; it's about governing the entire AI stack from infrastructure to application to internal usage patterns.
