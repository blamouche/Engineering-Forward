# Nvidia Paid $6B to NOT Own the Company: Jensen Knows His Limitations
**Source**: https://getcoai.com
**Date**: 2026-08-24
**Author**: Harry and Anthony (CO/AI Signal/Noise)
**Keywords**: Nvidia, Poolside, AI deals, licensing, Broadcom, Anthropic, Google, Spirit Airlines, circle of competence

## Elevator pitch
Nvidia paid $6 billion to license Poolside's model factory without acquiring the company, exemplifying a week where four major AI deals were defined by what the buyers refused to do rather than what they bought.

## Takeaways
- Nvidia paid $6B to license Poolside's model factory, $1B for equity, and acquired 109 engineers—without buying the company
- This is Nvidia's third such deal in eight months, after Groq and Enfabrica, consistently choosing capability access over ownership
- Broadcom is parking up to $100B in off-balance-sheet financing through Apollo and Blackstone to put chips in Anthropic's hands—keeping the debt off its own books
- Google bought bankrupt Spirit Airlines' data for $10M through bankruptcy court—hundreds of millions of emails and billions of flight-pricing records
- Harvey built a state-of-the-art legal model by taking a Chinese open-weight base and post-training it on law, at a fraction of frontier lab costs
- The pattern across all four deals: companies refusing to over-reach beyond their core competence, self-imposing discipline that regulators haven't required
- Etched went from $10.3B to $21B valuation in 26 days with no new product—valuations at the picks-and-shovels layer are pricing compute queue access, not businesses
- OpenAI paused some frontier RL training for alignment and shipped zero data retention for business accounts

## Synthesis
The CO/AI Signal/Noise column identifies a pattern across four major AI deals in a single week: the smartest companies are defining themselves by what they refuse to do. Nvidia won't become a model owner. Broadcom won't become a credit fund. Google will only organize data. Harvey won't cosplay as OpenAI. Each deal is a company handing back equity upside, financing spread, or licensing margin to stay inside its circle of competence.

Nvidia's Poolside deal is the clearest example. The company makes GPUs; it has no business running a model lab. By licensing Poolside's model factory for $6 billion plus $1 billion in equity and 109 engineers, Nvidia gets the capability it needs—better models that drive GPU demand—without the organizational complexity, regulatory exposure, and strategic distraction of owning a model lab. This is Nvidia's third such deal in eight months, suggesting a deliberate strategy rather than opportunism.

Broadcom's off-balance-sheet arrangement with Apollo and Blackstone is financially more complex but follows the same logic. Putting up to $100 billion in chips for Anthropic requires massive capital, but Broadcom's credit-default swaps blew out when it took that risk internally. By routing the financing through specialized credit shops, Broadcom keeps making chips while letting the credit shops carry the credit risk. The echo of Enron and 2008 is noted, but the structure is different: this is off-balance-sheet for a real asset, not a synthetic obligation.

Google's $10M purchase of Spirit Airlines' data through bankruptcy court is the most efficient deal of the four. Google already owns the world's flight data; now it owns what the most price-sensitive fliers actually do. The bankruptcy court provided a clean, cheap, legally unambiguous channel for data that would otherwise be impossible to acquire at any price.

Harvey's approach—taking a Chinese open-weight base model and post-training it on legal data—demonstrates that the open-weight ecosystem enables specialized models at a fraction of frontier lab costs. This is the same pattern that makes Nvidia's licensing strategy viable: when capability can be rented or built on open foundations, ownership becomes optional.

The column's conclusion is that this discipline is self-imposed, not regulatory. Nvidia could have fought the FTC and probably won. The porous nature of current AI regulation means the smartest players are setting their own boundaries, which raises the question of what the rules are actually for if compliance is free.
