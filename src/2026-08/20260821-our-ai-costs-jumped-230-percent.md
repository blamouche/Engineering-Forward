# Our AI Costs Jumped 230 Percent — I'm Not Setting Token Budgets Yet
**Source**: https://every.to/our-ai-costs-jumped-230-percent
**Date**: 2026-08-17
**Author**: Arielle Shipper (Every)
**Keywords**: AI costs, token budgets, AI operations, startup operations, GPT-5.6, AI spending

## Elevator pitch
When Every's daily AI credit usage surged from 11,520 to 26,685 credits after the GPT-5.6 Sol rollout, their head of operations chose loose parameters over strict token budgets — and shares four lessons for managing AI spend at a fast-moving, AI-native company.

## Takeaways
- Every's daily credit usage rose 2.3x after the GPT-5.6 Sol rollout, spending $31,300 on OpenAI credits in July alone ($26,800 after the Sol spike began).
- The author chose "parameters, not policies": loose operational guidelines instead of hard spending caps, because hard limits would have stopped Vibe Check benchmarks and Every Agent development mid-run.
- Three questions guide spending decisions: What did it cost? What did it buy us? What did we learn? One $480 run that built critical infrastructure was worth it; another $480/day on email checking was not.
- Making usage visible (everyone sees ChatGPT's usage dashboard) turns spend from a month-end surprise into a team learning loop — engineers self-optimize when they can see their own consumption.
- The broader lesson: at a 30-person AI-native company, trust and fast reaction work better than bureaucracy; introduce friction only after you've seen the problem it's meant to prevent.

## Synthesis
The article provides a rare inside look at how a small AI-native company manages rapidly escalating AI costs. Every's experience is instructive because their costs didn't creep up — they spiked overnight when the CEO used Sol (ultra) for a benchmark, and daily usage never returned to baseline.

Rather than imposing token budgets (which would require predicting how much each person should spend on models whose capabilities and pricing change weekly), the author implemented four principles: establish a circuit breaker (a Ramp card limit), make usage visible (shared dashboards), earn the friction (don't add restrictions until you've seen the problem), and change as evidence changes. The result is a system that trusts the team to spend responsibly while maintaining visibility and a hard stop when the card runs out.

The key insight is that responsible usage and cheap usage are not the same thing. A $480 run that stands up infrastructure and teaches the engineer techniques for future efficiency is responsible; a $480/day spend on automated email checking is not. Same cost, different answer. This distinction matters for any company adopting AI tools: the goal isn't to minimize spend but to maximize the value extracted from it. The article also notes that $31,300/month in AI costs is still cheaper than hiring enough people to produce the same output — a calculation that will increasingly drive adoption decisions.
