**Author**: Kai Nicol-Schwarz
**Keywords**: AI agents, startups, venture capital, Europe, market mapping

## Elevator pitch
A Sifted investor roundup highlights 11 AI-agent startups that VCs are watching, framing agentic AI as a fast-moving category where infrastructure, workflow automation, and end-user tooling are attracting early conviction.

## Takeaways
- The article is a market map rather than a deep technical teardown: it surfaces investor-selected companies seen as promising in agentic AI.
- The framing comes from four venture firms — Speedinvest, Creandum, Firstminute Capital, and Cherry Ventures — which makes the piece useful as a snapshot of current VC attention.
- The core signal is that agentic AI is broadening from general hype into a set of startup bets around practical workflows, software automation, and AI-native product experiences.
- Public snippets around the article reference companies such as Ankar AI, Manex AI, and Granola, suggesting the list mixes specialist agent builders with broader AI productivity plays.
- For operators, the value is less the exact ranking than the pattern: investors are looking for companies that turn language models into repeatable products, not just demos.

## Synthesis
This Sifted piece packages the AI-agent boom through an investor lens. Rather than arguing that “agents” are one coherent product category, it uses a curated list of 11 startups to show where venture attention is currently concentrating. The roundup is built from selections by investors at Speedinvest, Creandum, Firstminute Capital, and Cherry Ventures, which makes it less a definitive taxonomy than a directional signal about what experienced European tech investors think is worth tracking.

The article’s main value is that it reflects the current shape of the market. Agentic AI is evolving quickly, and startup coverage often swings between infrastructure-heavy narratives and consumer-facing copilots. A list like this sits in between: it signals which companies are beginning to look durable enough to stand out from the noise. Public references to the roundup mention startups such as Ankar AI, Manex AI, and Granola, pointing to a mix of AI-native workflow products and more horizontal tooling bets. That mix matters because it suggests investors are not only looking for raw model capability; they are looking for products that wrap models in distribution, interface design, and repeatable use cases.

Another useful aspect of the piece is what it says implicitly about the state of the category. If multiple investors can each produce distinct startup picks, the market is already fragmented into subsegments. That usually means the conversation has moved beyond “are agents real?” and toward “which implementation layers will matter?” In practice, that can include vertical workflow automation, enterprise copilots, AI-enhanced collaboration tools, orchestration infrastructure, and systems that turn models into persistent software workers. The startup watchlist therefore functions as a proxy for where early defensibility might emerge.

For readers tracking the engineering and business side of AI, the takeaway is straightforward: investor interest is shifting toward teams that can operationalise model capabilities into products people repeatedly use. The important question is not whether a startup says it builds agents, but whether it turns autonomy, memory, workflow integration, and reliability into something customers will keep paying for. That is the real filter behind this kind of roundup, and why lists like this are useful even when they are incomplete: they reveal where capital believes practical product value is starting to form.
