# Elon Musk Sold Investors The Future. Now SpaceX Has To Build It.
**Source**: https://www.bigtechnology.com/p/elon-musk-sold-investors-the-future
**Date**: 2026-06-15
**Author**: Alex Kantrowitz and Marty Swant (Big Technology)
**Keywords**: SpaceX, IPO, xAI, AI infrastructure, Elon Musk, Anthropic, neocloud, frontier AI, capital expenditure, market cap

## Elevator pitch
Big Technology's analysis of SpaceX's historic IPO—the first AI company to go public—reveals how folding xAI into the offering exposed the staggering economics of frontier AI development and created a new disclosure benchmark for the industry.

## Takeaways
- SpaceX hit a market cap above $2.4 trillion on its first full trading day, up 14%, placing it among the ten largest companies in the world
- xAI's financials were revealed for the first time: $12.7B in capex in 2025, $7.7B in Q1 2026, and quarterly operating losses that rocketed from $936M (Q1 2025) to $2.47B (Q1 2026)—a new disclosure benchmark for AI lab economics
- Anthropic agreed to pay SpaceX $1.25B/month for compute capacity through 2029, turning SpaceX into a neocloud—though either party can cancel with 90 days' notice
- Musk retained 82% of voting power post-IPO, giving him unilateral control over board composition, shareholder votes, and major financial decisions
- 20% of IPO shares went to retail investors (vs. the usual 5-7%), potentially making SpaceX more volatile and sentiment-driven

## Synthesis
Big Technology's deep dive into SpaceX's IPO is less a story about rockets and more a story about what happens when the first frontier AI lab goes public. By folding xAI into the SpaceX offering, Elon Musk gave investors—and the public—the first detailed look at the economics of competing in frontier AI. The numbers are staggering: $12.7 billion in capital expenditures in 2025, another $7.7 billion in Q1 2026 alone, and operating losses that nearly tripled year-over-year. These figures could become a new disclosure benchmark against which Anthropic's and OpenAI's eventual IPO filings are compared, rather than just Microsoft, Meta, and Google.

The Anthropic compute deal is particularly significant. SpaceX's IPO prospectus revealed that Anthropic agreed to pay $1.25 billion per month for compute capacity through 2029—turning SpaceX into a neocloud provider. But the 90-day cancellation clause means this revenue stream is far from guaranteed, and a cancellation would likely rattle the stock. This arrangement reveals a new layer of the AI infrastructure stack: frontier labs are becoming both customers and tenants of compute providers that sit inside publicly traded companies.

The retail allocation is a structural risk. By giving 20% of shares to retail investors—far above the typical 5-7%—SpaceX widened access but also introduced sentiment-driven volatility. The company's valuation now depends not just on launch cadence or Starlink revenue but on how retail investors feel about the AI trade, moon missions, and the broader narrative around Musk's ambitions. The article notes that it remains unclear how much of the early enthusiasm was FOMO versus genuine belief in the business.

For the engineering community, the xAI financials are the most consequential revelation. They quantify the cost of frontier AI competition in concrete terms and suggest that only entities with access to public-market capital (or equivalent deep pockets) can sustain the burn rate. This has implications for the open-weight versus closed-model debate: open models like GLM-5.2 and DeepSeek V3 may be the only viable path for organizations that cannot spend billions per quarter on compute.
