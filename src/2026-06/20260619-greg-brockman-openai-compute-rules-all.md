# Greg Brockman On OpenAI's Plan To Win: Compute Rules All
**Source**: https://www.bigtechnology.com/p/greg-brockman-on-openais-plan-to
**Date**: 2026-06-19
**Author**: Marty Swant and Alex Kantrowitz
**Keywords**: OpenAI, compute, infrastructure, strategy, Greg Brockman, datacenter, GPT

## Elevator pitch
OpenAI president Greg Brockman argues that winning the compute race is the key to winning AI more broadly, explaining the company's historic $122 billion fundraise and datacenter buildout strategy.

## Takeaways
- Brockman contends that if AI model capabilities continue improving fast and relatively in sync across frontier labs, the lab with the most compute will ultimately win
- OpenAI raised $122 billion earlier in 2026, with much of it earmarked for infrastructure buildout to serve agentic AI at scale
- Current ChatGPT has roughly a billion users but hasn't yet brought agentic power to that user base — the depth of usage is "tiny compared to where we're going"
- Competitors like Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei have chided OpenAI's spending as "YOLOing," but Brockman frames the buildout as a core element of how OpenAI plans to win over time
- The strategic thesis is that demand for compute will vastly outstrip supply, making infrastructure ownership the decisive competitive advantage

## Synthesis
At the Big Technology AI Summit, OpenAI president Greg Brockman laid out a clear strategic thesis: if frontier AI capabilities continue to advance rapidly and remain relatively synchronized across labs, then compute — not algorithms — becomes the decisive competitive advantage. The argument is straightforward: there simply won't be enough compute in the world to satisfy all demand, and the company that controls the most of it will capture the most value.

This framing explains OpenAI's unprecedented capital raise of $122 billion, one of the largest in tech history. Much of that capital is being deployed toward datacenter infrastructure designed to serve agentic AI workloads at a scale that ChatGPT's current billion-user base has not yet demanded. Brockman emphasized that current usage is orders of magnitude below what agentic AI will require — "the depth of usage is tiny compared to where we're going."

The counter-argument, voiced most prominently by Anthropic's Dario Amodei, is that OpenAI's spending is reckless — "YOLOing" capital into infrastructure before the demand curve is proven. But Brockman's view implies that waiting for proof is itself the risk: if compute is the binding constraint, early investment in capacity creates a compounding advantage.

This strategic divergence — compute accumulation versus capital efficiency — represents one of the defining philosophical splits in the AI industry today. It's not just an OpenAI question; it shapes decisions at every frontier lab about whether to spend ahead of demand or optimize for margin. The outcome will determine whether the next decade of AI is defined by infrastructure moats or by algorithmic differentiation.
