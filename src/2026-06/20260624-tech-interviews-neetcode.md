# Tech Interviews with NeetCode
**Source**: https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/tech-interviews-with-neetcode
**Date**: 2026-06-24
**Author**: Gergely Orosz (The Pragmatic Engineer)
**Keywords**: NeetCode, coding interviews, tech hiring, Amazon, Google, AI in engineering, systems thinking, career development, LeetCode

## Elevator pitch
NeetCode (Navdeep Singh) shares his journey from Amazon and Google to building one of the most popular coding interview prep platforms, and argues that deep expertise, systems thinking, and effort remain the differentiating skills in the age of AI.

## Takeaways
- LeetCode-style interviews persist not because they predict job performance but because they scale efficiently for large tech companies that need to train hundreds or thousands of interviewers
- AI-powered cheating tools are driving a return to in-person whiteboard interviews at Google, reversing years of remote-first hiring practices
- NeetCode finds AI most valuable as a tech-debt and refactoring assistant rather than a greenfield coding tool — validating his earlier decision to take shortcuts that could later be cleaned up
- Effort is becoming the key differentiator because AI made everything else cheap: you can prompt a design, feature, or answer, but you cannot prompt caring or the ability to defend a choice
- Despite dramatic AI model improvements, predictions of coding's death haven't materialized — NeetCode observes engineers doing more work than before, not being replaced

## Synthesis
In this Pragmatic Engineer episode, Gergely Orosz sits down with Navdeep Singh — better known as NeetCode — to discuss his path from software engineer at Amazon and Google to building NeetCode.io, one of the most popular coding interview preparation platforms. The conversation covers career decisions, the state of technical interviewing, and how AI is reshaping what it means to be a skilled engineer.

The episode opens with NeetCode's blunt assessment that companies have no real method for evaluating engineers, and likely never did. The LeetCode-style interview survived because it scales surprisingly well — a company like Google or Amazon can train thousands of interviewers to administer and evaluate a consistent coding problem. It is a logistics solution, not a performance predictor. This framing is important because it reframes the debate around interview reform: the problem isn't that the current method is bad at predicting performance, it's that no scalable method is good at it.

NeetCode's career trajectory itself illustrates several counterintuitive lessons. He left Amazon after just two months, finding the culture so intense that he became afraid to ask for help. Paradoxically, this self-reliance served him well at Google, where his manager read his independence as seniority and promoted him rapidly from L3 to L4. The NeetCode YouTube channel took off only after he announced he'd gotten into Google — the credential itself became the sales pitch, transforming his practice videos from a side project into a trusted preparation resource.

The AI discussion is nuanced. NeetCode finds AI most valuable for cleaning up years of technical debt on the NeetCode backend, not for writing new features from scratch. This validates his earlier decision to take shortcuts: he knew AI could later help refactor. On hiring, he notes that AI-powered cheating tools are driving Google back to in-person whiteboard interviews — a reversal that underscores how AI is not just changing the work but changing how the ability to do the work is assessed.

The most resonant observation is about effort. AI made competence cheap — you can prompt a design, a feature, or an answer. But you cannot prompt caring, cannot prompt the willingness to defend a choice, and cannot prompt the judgment that comes from going deep on hard problems. NeetCode argues that learning difficult things remains one of the best investments an engineer can make, because the judgment and expertise built through that effort transfer regardless of how tools evolve. Despite predictions of coding's death, he sees engineers doing more work than before, not fewer — AI is amplifying output rather than replacing the people who produce it.
