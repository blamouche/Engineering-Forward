# How to Get the Most Out of Fable 5: Mike Krieger on Building AI-Native Products

**Date**: June 10, 2026

**Keywords**: Claude Fable 5, Mike Krieger, Anthropic Labs, Instagram, asynchronous agents, verification, software engineering, productivity, AI-native products, overnight work

## Elevator pitch
Anthropic Labs head and Instagram cofounder Mike Krieger describes how Fable 5's capability is forcing him to rethink productivity, engineering, and creative agency — with overnight autonomous work, closing the gap between idea and reality, and a new focus on verification.

## Takeaways
- Fable 5 is the first model capable enough that you can hand it a complex task, walk away, and trust it will be completed by morning — changing the daily rhythm of work from doing to briefing
- When Fable hits obstacles like a remote service going down, it writes a workaround and forges ahead, demonstrating resilience that previous models lacked
- An Anthropic recruiter described the experience as "the first time in my life where the thing that's in my head and the thing that exists in the world are right next to each other"
- Software engineering is shifting: engineers spend less time writing code and more time setting direction, reviewing AI-built work, and making judgment calls when production breaks
- The divide between product managers and engineers has blurred as both roles converge on directing AI agents
- Krieger's verification approach combines regression testing on known workflows, visual checks including video captures, and mock backends for complex systems
- When a bug arrives via Slack, Fable 5 makes the fix, posts the pull request, then follows up hours later — autonomously

## Synthesis
Mike Krieger's conversation on the AI & I podcast offers a rare vantage point from someone who built one of the most popular consumer apps in the pre-GPT era and has had access to Fable 5 for months as head of Anthropic Labs. The most fundamental shift he describes is temporal: work now happens overnight. Fable 5 is the first model capable enough that you can hand it a complex task, walk away, and trust it will be completed by morning. When it hits obstacles — a remote service goes down, a tool stops working — it writes a workaround and forges ahead. This resilience has changed Krieger's daily rhythm: he now ends his workday by briefing the model on what needs to get done while he sleeps.

The second shift is about the collapsing distance between idea and reality. An Anthropic recruiter's description — "the first time in my life where the thing that's in my head and the thing that exists in the world are right next to each other" — captures what Krieger identifies as the most meaningful thing about the new model class: it allows non-engineers to create the exact products they need. This has profound implications for who can build software and how teams are structured. The divide between product managers and engineers has blurred, with both roles converging on directing AI agents rather than writing or specifying code.

The third shift is the heightened importance of verification. If we can delegate more to the model, checking what it has built becomes the critical bottleneck. Krieger's approach combines regression testing on known workflows, visual checks — including giving the model video captures of its own work so it can catch animation glitches that screenshots would miss — and mock backends for anything too complex to test live. When a bug arrives via Slack, Fable 5 makes the fix, posts the pull request, then follows up hours later. Engineers now spend less time writing code and more time setting direction, reviewing what AI agents have built, and making judgment calls — a transition Krieger describes as holding both loss and empowerment simultaneously.
