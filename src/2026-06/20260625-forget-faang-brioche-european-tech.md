## Elevator pitch
Sifted proposes BRIOCHE — Bolt, Revolut, Iceye, Oura, Celonis, Helsing, ElevenLabs — as Europe's answer to FAANG, arguing the continent needs a shared shorthand for the companies actually building European tech champions.

## Takeaways
- FAANG packaged US tech dominance into a memorable acronym; Silicon Valley has pushed "MANGOS" (Meta, Anthropic, Nvidia, Google, OpenAI, SpaceX) for the AI era, but Europe has never produced an equivalent shared narrative

## Synthesis
Martin Coulter's piece for Sifted begins with a simple observation about market psychology: acronyms package messy, competitive reality into a narrative that investors and media can rally around. FAANG did this for the consumer internet era. MANGOS — Meta, Anthropic, Nvidia, Google, OpenAI, SpaceX — is Silicon Valley's attempt to do the same for AI. Europe, despite producing a growing roster of genuinely competitive technology companies, has never managed to produce an equivalent shorthand.

The proposal is BRIOCHE: Bolt (mobility, Estonia), Revolut (fintech, UK), Iceye (satellite imaging, Finland), Oura (wearables, Finland), Celonis (enterprise software, Germany), Helsing (defence AI, Germany/UK), and ElevenLabs (AI voice, UK/Poland). The lineup spans seven countries and six technology sectors, from Tallinn to Munich, Helsinki to London. It is, as Coulter acknowledges, "a slightly indulgent but undeniably strong lineup."

The self-awareness about the acronym's imperfection is part of the argument. Coulter anticipates the objections: Mistral should be in, Synthesia deserves a spot, Spotify is an obvious candidate. The response is to invite the debate — "swap them in, improve it, break it apart" — because the deeper point is not about which seven companies make the cut but about the absence of a shared European tech narrative. The acronym is a provocation designed to surface the question of why Europe has never coalesced around one.

The piece situates BRIOCHE within a broader moment for European tech. The British Business Bank is reportedly seeking a stake in the EU's €5bn superfund, a signal that institutional capital is beginning to flow across borders. Europe's sovereignty drive — accelerated by AI export controls and geopolitical tension — is boosting nuclear energy startups like Proxima Fusion, currently in talks to fundraise. The same pressures that make European strategic autonomy a policy priority make the case for a recognizable European tech champion narrative more urgent.

The article is ultimately less about the specific companies and more about the narrative infrastructure of European technology. Acronyms are not just labels; they are coordination mechanisms that help investors, policymakers, and founders align around a shared sense of direction. Whether BRIOCHE catches on is almost beside the point. The value is in starting the conversation that Europe has been avoiding — and in naming, however imperfectly, the companies that give the conversation something to be about.
