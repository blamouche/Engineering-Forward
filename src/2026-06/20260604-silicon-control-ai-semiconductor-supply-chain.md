## Synthesis
Kerman Kohli's "Silicon Control" is an 8,000-word essay published on June 4, 2026, through Silicon Frontier, arguing against the prevailing narrative that AI infrastructure is in a bubble with overbuilt capacity. Instead, Kohli contends the industry faces a decade-long journey of being supply constrained, with the future being a silicon monopoly of fewer than 20 companies that control the means of production for machine intelligence.

The essay's foundational premise is that hardware is the real means of production in the AI race. Machine labor is far more scalable and cost-effective than human labor, and the demand for machine labor will likely be truly infinite. The limiting factor is not demand but the hardware to support it — specifically, the few dozen companies that have a monopoly on the future of semiconductor manufacturing. This is not a temporary bottleneck but a structural reality rooted in the extraordinary complexity of the semiconductor supply chain.

Kohli reframes the economic implications. GDP has typically been an approximate function of the number of people a country has multiplied by how productive they are. With AI, this maxim is entirely decoupled — productivity becomes independent of population size because machines have become a substitute for labor. Countries and companies with guaranteed access to advanced semiconductor manufacturing gain an enormous structural advantage, while those without it become dependent on supply chains they don't control.

The essay addresses common misconceptions investors have about the supply chain. The semiconductor supply chain's complexity spans time (years-long fabrication facility construction), capital (tens of billions per fab), and geopolitical constraints (export controls, talent concentration, rare materials). This complexity means that rapid capacity expansion is nearly impossible regardless of demand signals. Even with massive investment, the timeline from capital commitment to first wafers is measured in years.

The implication for the industry is a consolidation toward a silicon monopoly of fewer than 20 companies — those that control the fabrication facilities, packaging technologies, and design capabilities necessary for advanced AI chips. This consolidation has implications for sovereignty, competitiveness, and the distribution of AI's economic benefits. Nations and companies that don't have a guaranteed "silicon pipeline" will find their AI ambitions constrained not by software capability or talent, but by access to the physical infrastructure that prints intelligence. The essay argues this is not a transient phase but a structural reality that will persist for at least a decade.
