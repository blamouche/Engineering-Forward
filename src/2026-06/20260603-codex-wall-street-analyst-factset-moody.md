## Synthesis
On June 2, 2026, OpenAI did something stranger than ship a new model—it aimed Codex, the tool built to write software, at Wall Street, releasing finance plugins that turn the agent into a public equity analyst and an investment banker on demand. At first glance this looks like OpenAI catching Anthropic in finance. But two deeper questions emerge: why is a coding tool the product now doing banking, and while everyone argues over which lab has the better model, who is quietly positioned to capture most of the value?

The answer to the second question reorders who wins. The durable moat belongs to companies most people aren't watching: financial data providers like FactSet and Moody's. These companies own the proprietary, structured, validated financial data that any AI agent needs to produce trustworthy analysis. A coding agent can write the SQL and build the dashboard, but the data behind it—SEC filings, corporate actions, ratings, estimates, ownership data—comes from a handful of providers who have spent decades building relationships, regulatory compliance, and data pipelines.

Meanwhile, Anthropic confidentially filed its IPO prospectus with the SEC on the same day, beating OpenAI to the milestone. The Claude maker's IPO is critical because it tests whether the market values AI labs at the trillion-dollar valuations implied by private funding rounds. Anthropic has surpassed OpenAI in business clients, but faces growing corporate scrutiny over high AI costs—a survey revealed 40% of businesses experiencing cost savings below 10%.

The broader insight is that as AI agents commoditize financial analysis, the value shifts downstream to the data layer and upstream to the workflow layer. Financial data providers become AI infrastructure—their APIs are what every agent calls. The analysis itself, once a high-priced human activity, becomes a commodity. The winners are the companies that own the data the agents can't generate themselves.
