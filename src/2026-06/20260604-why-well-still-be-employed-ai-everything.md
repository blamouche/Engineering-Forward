## Synthesis
Laura Entis's "Why We'll Still Be Employed When AI Can Do Everything," published in Every's Context Window newsletter on June 4, 2026, offers a counterintuitive argument about AI and employment: even when AI can outwork people at well-run companies, running it will cost so much energy and compute that hiring a person is often cheaper. This grounded economic perspective cuts through both AI hype and AI doom, suggesting that the labor market won't collapse overnight even if technical capabilities match human performance.

The essay's most vivid section addresses the difficulty of enterprise AI product roadmaps, using Microsoft's trajectory with OpenClaw as a case study. Three months after OpenClaw launched in November 2025, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella described it as a "virus"-like security risk. By May, the company's "Project Lobster" was internally testing "ClawPilot," an OpenClaw-based desktop environment. At Microsoft Build, the company released Scout, a personal agent for work built on OpenClaw. For a company employing 100,000 engineers, this is blindingly fast. Unfortunately, it may already be too late.

The Google Trends graph for "openclaw" shows search interest spiked in January 2026, after users experimented with Opus 4.5 over the holidays, and began descending soon after. The decline was accelerated in early April when Anthropic ended support for subsidized Max plan usage, forcing everyone to scramble to get OpenClaw working on cheaper models. This doesn't mean OpenClaw is dead — the open-source project saw a recent uptick in downloads, is still under active development, and has millions of dollars of patronage from OpenAI, which hired its creator Peter Steinberger. AI agents as a category aren't dead either — traffic has moved to other agents like Hermes, Google has rolled out Gemini Spark, and Claude and Codex have both adopted more agentic features.

The roadmap problem is structural: companies do everything right — watch trends, pivot focus, explain to stakeholders, plan conference keynotes months in advance — and then a month after the internal beta, they're already behind the news cycle. Everyone has moved onto the next shiny thing. The lesson is that enterprise AI product planning cycles are fundamentally incompatible with the speed of AI tool evolution.
