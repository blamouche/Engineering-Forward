# The CEO of AWS on Why Amazon Is Hiring 11,000 Junior Employees
**Source**: https://www.platformer.news/matt-garman-aws-ceo-interview-ai-jobs/
**Date**: 2026-06-25
**Author**: Casey Newton (Platformer)
**Keywords**: AWS, Amazon, AI jobs, Matt Garman, entry-level hiring, cloud computing, AI economy

## Elevator pitch
AWS CEO Matt Garman argues that AI will change jobs rather than destroy them, pointing to Amazon's hiring of 11,000 interns and new grads — even as Amazon cuts 30,000 corporate jobs and plans to replace half a million positions with robots.

## Takeaways
- Garman calls replacing junior employees with AI "one of the dumbest things I've ever heard" and argues that never hiring junior people is a "non-starter for anyone trying to build a long-term company"
- Amazon is hiring 11,000 interns and new grads this year, despite cutting roughly 30,000 corporate jobs since October and CEO Andy Jassy's statement that AI will "reduce our total corporate workforce"
- The AI transition is happening much faster than the cloud transition: Garman notes that even 20 years into AWS, most workloads still run on-prem, but AI is transforming businesses in 20 months, not 20 years
- Enterprise AI adoption has moved from proof-of-concept experimentation (2-3 years ago, mostly without clear ROI) to production workloads with defined business value, though many companies still struggle to move beyond experiments
- Cloud and AI are compounding: without cloud infrastructure, AI adoption would be dramatically slower because the scaling, model deployment, and compute provisioning would all fall on individual companies

## Synthesis
Casey Newton's interview with AWS CEO Matt Garman provides a revealing portrait of one of the AI economy's most powerful optimists. Garman, who joined Amazon as an MBA intern in 2005 before AWS even launched and became CEO in June 2024, sits atop a roughly $150 billion-a-year business that underpins much of the AI boom — as the primary training partner for Anthropic, with compute deals with OpenAI worth up to $100 billion, and $200 billion in planned capital expenditures this year.

The central tension Garman embodies is stark. He is among the loudest voices arguing that AI will change jobs but not destroy them, calling the replacement of junior employees "one of the dumbest things I've ever heard." Amazon is hiring 11,000 interns and new grads this year. Yet Amazon has cut roughly 30,000 corporate jobs since October, CEO Andy Jassy has written that AI will "reduce our total corporate workforce," and the company plans to replace half a million jobs with robots. Garman is talking up the value of labor even as he sells the technology that may displace it.

The interview's most substantive technical insight is about the speed of the AI transition relative to cloud. Garman draws a direct analogy: when AWS started, they had to explain what the cloud was and why Amazon was part of it. Twenty years later, the vast majority of workloads still run on-prem. AI is different — it's transforming businesses in 20 months, not 20 years. He attributes this acceleration to the compounding effect of cloud and AI: without cloud infrastructure, the scaling, model deployment, and compute provisioning would all fall on individual companies, dramatically slowing adoption.

On enterprise adoption, Garman offers a clear-eyed assessment. Two or three years ago, companies ran huge numbers of proof-of-concepts without a plan for ROI. Most didn't show great returns because they didn't define what they wanted out. Now the shift is toward production workloads with defined business value — but many companies are still struggling to move beyond the experimentation phase. The key is moving from "see what this technology does" to "what business problem are we solving."

Garman's attempt to square the circle between job optimism and displacement uses a familiar historical analogy: Excel eliminated the jobs of people who calculated by hand, but those people learned to use computers and the labor force expanded. The question he doesn't fully address is whether the pace and breadth of AI-driven change will allow the same kind of adaptation — especially when Amazon itself is simultaneously selling the displacement technology and hiring entry-level talent. The tension is not resolved; it's simply held in place by the conviction that technology destroys individual jobs but creates new and better ones.
