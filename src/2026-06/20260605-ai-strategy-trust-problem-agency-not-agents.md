# Your AI Strategy Has a Trust Problem, Not a Tooling Problem
**Source**: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/cp/200821257
**Date**: 2026-06-05
**Author**: Elena Verna
**Keywords**: ai-strategy, organizational-design, agency, trust, autonomy, middle-management, ai-native

## Elevator pitch
Elena Verna argues that companies already have the AI tools they need to go faster — the real blocker is organizational structure: centralized power, approval cycles, and title-based hierarchies that scream "we don't trust you" and prevent employees from acting on AI-enabled context, making agency more important than agents.

## Takeaways
- The blocker for AI transformation isn't technology — it's org structures designed to prevent things from happening: power is centralized, employees are treated as risk vectors
- Access is gated by titles: companies say they want high-impact ICs, then hide information behind manager titles and private Slack channels
- AI-native orgs like Anthropic strip out status theater — visible structure is basically "member of technical staff" or "member of non-technical staff" because titles become permission structures
- Faster, cheaper decisions create a virtuous cycle: when context doesn't pass up-and-down the chain for every little thing, decisions are faster and the cost of wrong turns drops
- At Lovable, AI agents serve as departmental context tools with "parents" who keep them accurate — but the agent isn't the unlock; employee agency is

## Synthesis
Elena Verna's piece cuts to the heart of why most enterprise AI transformations fail. When companies talk about AI adoption, they focus on tools and skills. But even the best employees with the most magical tools will get trapped if the org structure and culture don't adjust. The problem is that most modern companies are built around a command-and-control model where each employee is treated like an assembly-line worker with one specific function. Power is centralized, and all team members are treated as risk vectors. Exhausting approval cycles, tight role boundaries, unbreakable hierarchies, and middle managers whose main job is keeping everyone in line — all of this screams "we don't trust you, stay in your lane."

Two specific mechanisms lock this in. First, access is gated by titles. Companies say they want high-impact ICs, then hide the information required to have impact behind manager titles. When you get a manager promotion, you gain access to separate Slack channels where information is shared that you had no access to before. Second, only bosses get to make decisions. Decisions are slow because they're expensive, and expensive because they're slow — creating a self-reinforcing cycle of bottlenecked autonomy.

AI-native organizations like Anthropic strip out the status theater. The visible structure is basically "member of technical staff" or "member of non-technical staff" — not because titles are evil, but because titles often become permission structures that decide who gets context, who gets heard, and who gets to make decisions. This leads to faster and cheaper decisions. If access to information is ungated and autonomy is encouraged, context doesn't have to pass up and down the chain for every little thing. And when adjustments happen same-day rather than at the next quarterly strategy meeting, the cost of a wrong decision falls — you can go down a route, turn around, and be back on course faster than traditional teams even start moving.

Verna's most pointed insight is about agency versus agents. At Lovable, AI agents serve as departmental context tools, each with a "parent" responsible for keeping it accurate. This removes the biggest excuse for centralized decision-making: "people don't have enough context." But the agent is not the unlock. The unlock is what happens when employees can actually act on that context. "Agents don't have agency. They wait to be told what to do. High-agency employees do the opposite: they find the signal, make the call, and push the work forward." The goal is not more agents. It's more agency.
