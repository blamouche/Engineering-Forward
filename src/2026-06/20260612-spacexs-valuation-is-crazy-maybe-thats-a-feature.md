# SpaceX's Valuation Is Crazy. Maybe That's A Feature?
**Source**: https://www.bigtechnology.com/p/spacexs-valuation-is-crazy-maybe
**Date**: June 12, 2026
**Author**: Alex Kantrowitz
**Keywords**: SpaceX, IPO, AI infrastructure, valuation, capital expenditure, frontier models, price war, retail investors

## Elevator pitch
SpaceX's record $1.77 trillion IPO valuation is disconnected from its current financials, but the willingness of investors to fund improbable bets may be the mechanism that allows both space exploration and the AI buildout to advance at their current pace.

## Takeaways
- SpaceX IPO'd at $1.77 trillion and rose to $2.1 trillion at close, despite losing nearly $5 billion the prior year and still not turning a profit on rocket launches.
- The company has been tossing money at AI infrastructure it does not fully know how to use, and has started selling its compute to rivals, building an unexpected AI cloud in the process.
- Retail investors accounted for roughly 20% of the IPO — far higher than the typical 5-10% — and placed over $100 billion in orders.
- A similar dynamic drives the AI buildout: some capital expenditures will be written down, but AI would not be advancing at its current rate without financial overexuberance.
- The risk may be the point: giving companies room to pursue low-probability breakthroughs, even if they incinerate cash along the way, is how satellite internet and space data centers became real.

## Synthesis
Alex Kantrowitz's Big Technology newsletter examines the disconnect between SpaceX's record-breaking IPO valuation and its underlying financials, arguing that the irrationality may be structural rather than accidental. When the company debuted at $1.77 trillion and closed at $2.1 trillion, it did so as a connectivity company with an unprofitable rocket arm and an AI question mark — having lost nearly $5 billion the previous year and still not making money on launches after more than two decades in business.

Kantrowitz frames this as a feature of the investment system rather than a bug. The willingness of investors to throw money at seemingly impossible bets and wait patiently for results is what allows improbable initiatives like satellite internet and space data centers to become real. There is waste, he acknowledges, but expecting perfect efficiency in the moonshot business is fantasy. The same logic applies to the AI buildout: evidence of a coming price war among frontier models, major data center initiatives going sideways, and SpaceX itself resorting to selling compute to rivals all suggest that some capital expenditures will be written down. Yet AI probably would not be advancing at its current rate without the financial overexuberance driving it.

The retail dimension adds a new wrinkle. Everyday investors accounted for somewhere in the low-20% range of the SpaceX IPO, much higher than the typical 5-10%, and they put in more than $100 billion in orders. SpaceX stock will also appear in index funds, meaning passive investors will own it too. Kantrowitz notes that actively investing savings in SpaceX comes with risk more akin to venture capital than traditional public market investing — a company that had no AI play a year ago now claims AI accounts for $26.5 trillion of its $28.5 trillion total addressable market. The article stops short of endorsing the valuation, instead arguing that the system that produces such valuations is the same one that funds the breakthroughs, and that picking bets wisely remains essential even when the overall appetite for risk is what makes the frontier possible.
