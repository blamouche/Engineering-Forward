# Runway's Big Week: AI Film Festival, Ron Howard, and Lionsgate Expansion
**Source**: https://www.bigtechnology.com/p/elon-musk-sold-investors-the-future
**Date**: 2026-06-15
**Author**: Alex Kantrowitz and Marty Swant (Big Technology)
**Keywords**: Runway, AI video, AI Film Festival, Ron Howard, Lionsgate, Sora, AI filmmaking, photorealism, creative AI, video generation

## Elevator pitch
Big Technology's coverage of Runway's fourth annual AI Film Festival reveals an AI video company maturing into a creative platform—1,000 attendees, a partnership with Ron Howard, an expanded Lionsgate deal with equity, and CEO Cristóbal Valenzuela's thesis on why video generation costs won't follow the LLM cost trajectory.

## Takeaways
- 1,000 people attended the fourth annual Runway AI Film Festival in NYC, featuring nearly a dozen AI-generated short films across genres chosen from thousands of global submissions
- Runway expanded its Lionsgate partnership: Lionsgate takes an equity interest in Runway, with a new joint development program starting with short-form episode series based on Lionsgate IP
- CEO Valenzuela argued AI video costs won't follow the LLM runaway cost pattern: video generation is usage-based and time-bounded—"the creative process is the answer itself"—unlike agents that run for hours chasing answers
- On Sora shutting down: Valenzuela said it validated Runway's focused thesis—"You can't just be doing 15 things"—companies trying to do video alongside other efforts lose on attention and prioritization
- AI video models crossed the photorealism threshold within the last year; filmmakers are now splitting between photorealism chasers and those deliberately moving away from it toward "something more raw or more strange"

## Synthesis
Big Technology's report from Runway's AI Film Festival captures a turning point for AI-generated video as both a technology and a creative medium. The festival's scale—1,000 attendees, Ron Howard on stage, thousands of submissions—signals that AI video has moved from novelty to cultural event. Howard's framing is notable: he compared AI to the transition from physical film editing to Avid Media Composer in 1989, positioning it as another tool in a long line of filmmaking transformations rather than a disruption.

The Lionsgate deal expansion is the most commercially significant detail. Lionsgate taking an equity interest in Runway represents a deeper commitment than the 2024 training deal. The new joint development program—starting with short-form episode series based on existing Lionsgate IP—shows a path from tool provider to content partner. This is the studio model adapted for AI: the AI company isn't just selling software to Hollywood, it's co-producing content and sharing in the upside.

Valenzuela's cost thesis is the most technically interesting argument. He pushes back on the idea that AI video will face the same runaway cost problem as agents and LLMs, arguing that video generation is usage-based and time-bounded. An agent might run for hours chasing an answer, but "the creative process is the answer itself" in video generation—the user generates, evaluates, iterates, and the cost is bounded by the creative session. If correct, this means AI video economics scale differently than LLM economics, which could make AI video a more sustainable business than AI text generation.

The Sora shutdown commentary reveals a competitive strategy. Valenzuela argues that OpenAI shutting Sora validated Runway's focused approach: companies trying to do video alongside many other things lose on attention and prioritization. The implication is that AI video is a full-time discipline, not a feature within a broader AI platform—a thesis that Runway's festival, Lionsgate deal, and product roadmap are all designed to validate.
