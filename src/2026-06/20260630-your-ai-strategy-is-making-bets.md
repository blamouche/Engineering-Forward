# Your AI Strategy Is Making Bets. Do You Know Which Ones?

**Author**: Dan Pupius (The General Partnership)

## Elevator pitch

A four-axis framework from The General Partnership's CTO Dan Pupius that helps AI startup founders make their implicit strategic bets explicit — covering token economics, model self-sufficiency, platform lock-in, and regulatory environment — so they can recognize when they're wrong before the market forces them to pivot.

## Takeaways

- The framework replaces vague labels like "AI wrapper" with four specific axes of strategic bets that founders are implicitly making
- Token economics axis: founders must decide whether they're betting on AI costs remaining scarce (a barrier to entry) or becoming abundant (requiring a different moat like proprietary data or distribution)
- Model self-sufficiency axis: companies augmenting model capabilities (memory, retrieval, orchestration) are betting the model won't eventually do that natively — a bet with an expiration date
- Platform lock-in axis: building deeply on one provider creates irreversible dependencies; founders should distinguish reversible from irreversible assumptions
- The goal isn't to predict the future more precisely but to recognize sooner when you're wrong and move before the market forces you

## Synthesis

Dan Pupitus, CTO at The General Partnership, argues that the rapid pace of AI change means strategic assumptions can shift mid-execution, causing carefully built bets to crumble. Rather than asking "What do we think will happen?", founders should ask two fundamental questions: What assumptions are irreversible? And what assumptions can we still revise? Building deeply on one model provider is irreversible — rewiring the product to move away is expensive. Assuming tokens will stay expensive is reversible — if costs fall, you adapt without rebuilding.

The four-axis framework maps the specific futures a company is betting on. The token economics axis (scarce ↔ abundant) determines whether falling AI costs help or hurt: if you're a token consumer, abundance is good for margins but dangerous for defensibility. The model self-sufficiency axis (needs scaffolding ↔ handles natively) determines the fate of "model wrapper" companies — if you're augmenting what models do, you're betting they won't do it natively someday. Harvey and Sierra are both counting on general models not being able to serve their niches.

The framework's core insight is that founders should stop using vague heuristics and instead identify the specific assumptions underlying their strategy. For token scarcity bets, founders must articulate why falling costs won't destroy their advantage — constant-running agents or latency-sensitive work like voice agents are examples where costs remain a barrier. For abundance bets, defensibility comes from proprietary data, domain expertise, distribution, or knowing what to build. Companies that reduce cost variance — capping maximum bills — are more defensible than those competing on average price.

The article is paywalled beyond the first two axes, but the accessible content establishes the framework's core logic: the goal is to recognize sooner when you're wrong and move before the market forces you — not to predict the future more precisely. This is a pragmatic shift from deterministic strategy to probabilistic bet management, tailored to an era where model capabilities and costs shift quarterly.
