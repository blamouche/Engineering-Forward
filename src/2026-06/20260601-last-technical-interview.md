# The Last Technical Interview
**Source**: https://steve-yegge.medium.com/the-last-technical-interview-bc13ddcf4564
**Date**: 2026-05-29
**Author**: Steve Yegge
**Keywords**: technical-interview, hiring, campfire-model, trial-days, Bar-Raisers, talent-assessment, AI-hiring, Silicon-Valley

## Elevator pitch
Steve Yegge declares the death of the technical interview after 35 years of conducting them, arguing the process was always broken and is finally dying on its own — with the "campfire" model (paid trial days on real codebases with real teams) emerging as what replaces it.

## Takeaways
- Technical interviews have been broken for decades — Amazon's Bar Raisers and Microsoft's As-Appropriate roles are tacit admissions that you can't trust interview teams to make good hiring decisions
- The process survives despite producing tons of false positives (unqualified hires) and false negatives (qualified rejections) because it worked just barely well enough to resist overhaul
- A new "campfire" model is emerging in Silicon Valley: companies hire candidates to work with them for a few days with real pay, on real codebases, with the real team
- AI is accelerating the shift — candidates can use AI tools during trials, and the evaluation shifts from "can you write code" to "can you ship and collaborate"
- The replacement is not a silver bullet; the transition will be difficult and messy

## Synthesis
Steve Yegge, a 35-year veteran of conducting technical interviews and former Amazon Bar Raiser (part of the elite "Bar Raisers Core" group tasked by Bezos and Dalzell with defining the BR role), declares the technical interview process is on its last legs. The piece is framed as an obituary for a process he has spent decades trying to improve.

The core argument is that talent evaluation is "embarrassingly busted" and has been for five decades. The Bar Raiser and As-Appropriate roles are themselves a tacit acknowledgement that interview teams can't be trusted to make good hiring decisions — if every interview loop needs a babysitter, the process is flawed. Despite countless band-aids (structured interviews, rubrics, calibration meetings, scoring systems), the process still produces high rates of false positives (unqualified hires) and false negatives (qualified candidates turned away). It survived because it worked just barely well enough to resist an overhaul, with inertia on the tech side preventing change.

The replacement is already emerging organically in Silicon Valley: the "campfire" model, where companies hire candidates to work with them for a few days with real pay, on real codebases, with the real team. This approach provides signal that no amount of whiteboard coding or system design rounds can match — you see how the candidate navigates unfamiliar code, communicates in natural working conditions, handles real constraints (tech debt, missing documentation, legacy decisions), and how the team feels about working with them after 8+ hours of actual collaboration.

AI is accelerating the shift. When candidates can use AI tools during trial days, the evaluation fundamentally changes from "can you write code from scratch" to "can you ship, collaborate, and make good decisions with the tools available." This aligns with how engineering actually works in 2026 — nobody writes code from scratch on a whiteboard, but everyone needs to navigate codebases, use AI assistants, and make architectural decisions under uncertainty.

Yegge is careful to note this is not a silver bullet. The transition will be difficult, expensive, and messy. Trial days require more time from the team than a standard interview loop, scheduling is harder, and IP/NDA concerns need addressing. But the fundamental insight is that the technical interview was never measuring what it claimed to measure, and the gap between interview performance and job performance was always wider than the industry admitted. The campfire model, by collapsing the evaluation into actual work, closes that gap by definition.
