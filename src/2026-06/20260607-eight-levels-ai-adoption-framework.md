# The Eight Levels of AI Adoption: From Chatbot to Orchestrator
**Source**: https://every.to/guides/the-eight-levels-of-ai-adoption
**Date**: 2026-06-07
**Author**: Mike Taylor, Laura Entis
**Keywords**: ai-adoption, framework, agents, orchestration, multi-agent, enterprise-ai, claude-code

## Elevator pitch
Every's Mike Taylor and Laura Entis map eight levels of AI adoption — from Level 1 chatbot to Level 8 orchestrator agent — with sample prompts and signals for when to move up, arguing that a higher level isn't automatically better; the right level depends on trust, cost of mistakes, and the specific task.

## Takeaways
- Level 1 Chatbot → Level 2 Copilot → Level 3 Agent → Level 4 Autopilot → Level 5 Workflows → Level 6 Assistant → Level 7 Multi-agent → Level 8 Orchestrator
- A higher level isn't necessarily better — the right level depends on how much you trust the AI and how costly a mistake would be
- Knowledge workers' sweet spot is Levels 1-4; engineers are more often in Levels 5-8 because they can build the scaffolding that makes newer systems usable
- Steve Yegge's "Gas Town" post about directing dozens of coding agents inspired the framework but didn't match the authors' real-world consulting experience

## Synthesis
The Eight Levels of AI Adoption framework from Every's consulting team provides a practical taxonomy for understanding where you stand with AI and what it takes to move up. Building on Steve Yegge's viral "Gas Town" post about directing dozens of coding agents, Mike Taylor and Laura Entis created a modified version that better matches their real-world enterprise consulting experience. The framework maps eight progressive levels of delegation to AI, each requiring more trust and autonomy.

The levels progress from basic interaction to full orchestration. Level 1 is the classic chatbot — you ask, it answers (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini). Level 2 is Copilot, where AI works inside your files alongside you (Cursor, Claude in Excel). Level 3 is Agent, executing tasks step by step with approval gates (Cowork, Codex). Level 4 is Autopilot, skipping approvals and letting the agent complete tasks independently (Lovable, Codex, Claude Code). Level 5 is Workflows, building systems that professionalize agent output (compound engineering, Claude Workflows). Level 6 is Assistant, where agents work proactively in the background without prompts (OpenClaw, Hermes Agent, Claude Managed Agents). Level 7 is Multi-agent, managing multiple long-running agents simultaneously. Level 8 is Orchestrator, where a manager agent runs a team of sub-agents on your behalf.

The key insight is that a higher level isn't automatically better. The most sophisticated AI users operate at several levels simultaneously, choosing the right level based on the specific challenge. The right level for a task is determined by how much you trust the AI to do a good job without intervention and how big a deal it would be if it messes up. For high-stakes tasks, you should either stay at a lower level to supervise, or invest the time, engineering resources, and tokens necessary to get quality at a higher level with less oversight.

The framework also includes a companion piece by Natalia Quintero — "An Executive's Guide to Implementing AI" — which addresses the real bottleneck: not the models, but the organization. Her five-step, 60-day framework is designed for executives who've bought the tools but aren't seeing returns, a gap Mike Taylor sees up close with enterprise clients.
