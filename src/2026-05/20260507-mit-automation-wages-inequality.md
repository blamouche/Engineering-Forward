# Study: Firms Often Use Automation to Control Certain Workers' Wages
**Source**: https://news.mit.edu/2026/study-firms-often-use-automation-control-certain-workers-wages-0507
**Date**: May 7, 2026
**Author**: Peter Dizikes (MIT News), covering research by Daron Acemoglu (MIT) and Pascual Restrepo (Yale)
**Keywords**: automation, inequality, wages, productivity, labor-economics, AI-economics, Acemoglu

## Elevator pitch
Nobel laureate Daron Acemoglu's latest research shows US firms have strategically used automation since 1980 to replace higher-earning workers rather than maximize productivity, accounting for 52% of income inequality growth while offsetting 60-90% of potential productivity gains.

## Takeaways
- Automation is responsible for 52% of the growth in US income inequality from 1980 to 2016 — a larger contribution than many economists previously estimated
- About 10 percentage points of that inequality growth comes specifically from firms targeting workers earning a "wage premium" — non-college-educated employees who had negotiated above-market pay
- Within affected worker groups, the biggest automation impacts hit the 70th-95th salary percentile, showing higher earners bear disproportionate burden
- Firms' incentive to reduce wage costs via automation has offset 60-90% of the productivity gains that automation could have produced
- The key insight: "Greater profitability is not the same as increased productivity" — firms can reduce costs while reducing productivity and still increase profits

## Synthesis
This paper, published in the May 2026 Quarterly Journal of Economics, represents the latest installment in Acemoglu and Restrepo's decade-long research program on automation's labor market effects. The granularity is impressive: they analyzed 500 demographic groups (by education, gender, age, ethnicity) across 49 US industries, linking employment changes to automation adoption patterns.

The central finding challenges the standard narrative that automation is primarily about efficiency. Instead, the data suggests firms often adopt automation strategically to "rent-dissipate" — specifically targeting workers who have managed to secure wages above market rates. Think of a call center where experienced agents have negotiated better pay; the firm has incentive to deploy chatbot technology not because it handles calls better, but because it eliminates those wage premiums.

This has profound implications for how we think about AI's economic trajectory. If firms deploy AI primarily as a wage-control tool rather than a productivity tool, we get the worst of both worlds: high inequality growth (capital owners benefit, labor loses) and mediocre productivity improvements (since the technology isn't being deployed where it would create the most value). Acemoglu invokes Solow's famous 1987 paradox — "you can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics" — suggesting the same dynamic may be playing out with AI.

The scale of the effect is sobering: 60-90% of potential productivity gains from automation were effectively wasted on wage arbitrage. Acemoglu frames this as a choice, not an inevitability. The same technologies could be deployed differently — in ways that complement workers rather than replace them, and in applications that genuinely transform productive capacity rather than just shift income from labor to capital.

For policymakers and business leaders, the implication is that "good automation" and "bad automation" get bundled together at the margin, and we lack the analytical frameworks to distinguish them. The paper is likely to become a central reference in debates about AI regulation, corporate governance of technology deployment, and the design of productivity-enhancing innovation policy.
