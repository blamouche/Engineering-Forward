**Keywords**: Claude, API proxy, transfer stations, grey market, China, KYC, biometric verification, AI safety, distillation, data harvesting

## Elevator pitch
A sprawling grey-market ecosystem of API proxies ("transfer stations") in China lets developers access Anthropic's Claude at 10% of the official price by routing through stolen credentials, model substitution, and harvesting users' prompts and outputs for resale as AI training data, exposing fundamental blind spots in AI safety frameworks.

## Takeaways
- Chinese "transfer stations" (API proxies) resell Claude access at 90% discounts by using bulk-registered accounts, SMS verification farms, and fake KYC bypasses — each new layer of Anthropic's geo-blocking creates a corresponding evasion infrastructure.
- The supply chain extends from account merchants and SMS platforms to AI-generated fake IDs and real human KYC harvesting operations in lower-income countries, mirroring Worldcoin's documented black market for biometric data.
- User prompts and outputs are systematically harvested and resold as AI training data, creating a secondary commodity market that undermines provider traceability and feeds into criminal networks.
- The White House and Anthropic both warned about "industrial-scale" distillation campaigns in 2026, but their framing misreads the broader grey economy: participants range from university students to hobbyists, not just state-backed labs.
- This ecosystem reveals that access-blocking and account monitoring are fundamentally inadequate as AI governance tools when the economic incentives for evasion are so large.

## Synthesis
The article, originally reported by Tom's Hardware based on research from Zilan Qian at the Oxford China Policy Lab, exposes a sophisticated and rapidly evolving grey market for Anthropic's Claude API in China. The centerpiece is the "transfer station" (中转站) — an API proxy server located overseas that accepts requests from Chinese developers, forwards them to Anthropic as if from a legitimate account, and returns the responses. Users pay in RMB via WeChat or Alipay at roughly 10% of official prices. These transfer stations are catalogued in community GitHub repositories and ranked by real-time price and uptime, operating almost entirely in the open.

The most revealing aspect of the investigation is the layered supply chain that makes these proxies possible. At the upstream level, account merchants bulk-register Anthropic accounts at scale using SMS verification platforms that provide foreign phone numbers. Reverse engineers analyze Anthropic's client code to find authentication shortcuts. When Anthropic introduced live biometric KYC verification in April 2026 — requiring government-issued photo ID and a live selfie — the grey market responded with AI-generated fake IDs and deepfake biometric verification. Even more disturbing, agents recruit real individuals in lower-income countries in Africa and Latin America to complete in-person verification, creating a documented black market for identity services that echoes the Worldcoin iris-scan harvesting operations in Cambodia and Kenya.

A critical insight from the research is the data harvesting dimension. Transfer stations don't simply relay API calls — they systematically log users' prompts and outputs, which are then resold as AI training data. This creates a secondary commodity market where the content of millions of developer interactions with frontier models becomes freely traded, with applications ranging from model training to targeted fraud. This undermines the entire premise of provider traceability that AI safety frameworks depend on.

The article also highlights a significant mismatch between how Western policymakers understand this ecosystem and what it actually is. Both the White House (April 2026 memo) and Anthropic (February 2026 report) framed the proxy networks as purposeful designs by selective Chinese frontier labs conducting "industrial-scale" distillation campaigns. Qian's research shows that while distillation certainly occurs, the participants are vastly broader — university professors, students, tech workers, individual developers, and hobbyists — all motivated by the simple desire to access more advanced AI tools rather than any coordinated state agenda.

The broader implication is sobering: every layer of control that frontier AI companies add — geoblocking, phone verification, credit card requirements, and now live biometric KYC — produces a corresponding and increasingly sophisticated layer of evasion. This suggests that access-blocking as an AI governance strategy faces fundamental limitations. The economic incentives are simply too large, and the evasion infrastructure too adaptive, for perimeter-based controls to be effective on their own. The article serves as a wake-up call that AI safety frameworks must account for the resilience and creativity of grey markets that emerge wherever access to valuable AI capabilities is artificially constrained.
