# Jury hands victory to Sam Altman and OpenAI in battle with Elon Musk
**Source**: https://theguardian.com/technology/2026/may/18/sam-altman-trial-victory-elon-musk-openai
**Date**: 2026-05-18 (published), 2026-05-19 (updated)
**Author**: Nick Robins-Early, Dara Kerr
**Keywords**: Sam Altman, Elon Musk, OpenAI, trial, jury verdict, for-profit restructuring, Silicon Valley, Microsoft

## Elevator pitch
A federal jury in Oakland ruled in favor of Sam Altman and OpenAI, rejecting Elon Musk's $134 billion lawsuit that accused Altman of breaking a founding contract and "stealing a charity" through OpenAI's for-profit restructuring.

## Takeaways
- The jury found Musk's 2024 lawsuit fell outside the statute of limitations — OpenAI argued Musk knew about for-profit plans as early as 2017, exceeding the three-year filing window — and delivered its advisory verdict after less than two hours of deliberation.
- Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers immediately agreed with the jury's decision and dismissed Musk's claims, stating there was "substantial evidence to support the jury's finding."
- Musk sought $134 billion in redistribution from OpenAI's for-profit arm to its non-profit, plus the removal of Altman and Brockman from their roles and the undoing of the for-profit restructuring. Microsoft, accused of aiding and abetting, was also found not liable.
- Musk announced plans to appeal to the Ninth Circuit, claiming the ruling was a "calendar technicality" that never addressed the merits of whether Altman and Brockman enriched themselves.
- The verdict clears the path for OpenAI's planned IPO later this year at approximately a $1 trillion valuation, removing the legal uncertainty that had been hanging over the company.

## Synthesis
The jury verdict in Musk v. Altman represents one of the most consequential legal outcomes in Silicon Valley history — not because of what it decided about OpenAI's founding promises, but because of what it cleared the path for. After a three-week trial that aired years of internal emails, texts, and testimony from tech's most prominent figures (Musk, Altman, Brockman, and Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella all took the stand), the nine-person Oakland jury needed less than two hours to side with OpenAI. The case was decided on a procedural question — the statute of limitations — rather than the substantive question of whether Altman and Brockman had violated OpenAI's original charitable mission.

Musk's legal theory was that Altman had induced him to co-found OpenAI in 2015 as a non-profit dedicated to benefiting humanity, only to later restructure it into a for-profit entity that enriched its leadership. His lawsuit sought $134 billion, the removal of Altman and Brockman, and the unwinding of the for-profit structure. OpenAI's defense rested heavily on timeline: internal documents showed Musk was aware of and even participated in discussions about for-profit restructuring as early as 2017. If the alleged harm occurred then, his 2024 filing came well outside California's three-year statute of limitations.

The trial itself was a spectacle of Silicon Valley dysfunction. Both sides used internal communications to paint unflattering portraits — Musk as a jealous former co-founder who tried and failed to take over OpenAI in 2018, Altman as someone whose grand rhetoric about benefiting humanity masked self-enrichment. Shivon Zilis, a former OpenAI board member and Neuralink executive (and mother of several of Musk's children), testified about the company's early history. The proceedings exposed the messy reality behind the polished public narratives of both men.

The immediate practical consequence is profound: OpenAI's path to an IPO at roughly $1 trillion valuation is now clear. A contrary verdict could have forced restructuring, leadership changes, or massive financial penalties — clouds that would have made going public nearly impossible. Musk's promised appeal to the Ninth Circuit introduces continuing uncertainty, but appellate courts are generally reluctant to overturn jury findings on factual questions, and the statute-of-limitations ruling is grounded in specific dates and documented communications.

OpenAI's lead attorney William Savitt framed Musk's case as a "hypocritical attempt to sabotage a competitor." Musk's attorneys framed the verdict as a "technical" decision that didn't clear Altman of moral culpability. Both are spinning, and both have a point. The jury never decided whether OpenAI's restructuring violated its founding principles — it only decided the claim came too late. That leaves the substantive question unresolved in the court of public opinion, even as it's settled in the court of law. For the AI industry, the verdict removes a major source of uncertainty around its most valuable private company. For the broader question of whether non-profit-to-for-profit transitions in AI constitute a betrayal of public trust, the debate continues.
