# The First Derivative of Inference
**Source**: https://tomtunguz.com/first-derivative-of-inference/
**Date**: May 15, 2026
**Author**: Tomasz Tunguz
**Keywords**: AI inference, SaaS, Twilio, Datadog, Anthropic, Google Cloud, power law, Saaspocalypse, venture capital

## Elevator pitch
The fastest-growing AI companies either sell inference directly or profit from customers buying massive volumes of it — "first derivative of inference" companies like Twilio and Datadog are riding the same wave, and any pre-AI SaaS company must ask itself how to index to inference to escape the Saaspocalypse.

## Takeaways
1. Inference is the largest and fastest-growing market in technology today, projected to hit $250B within seven years, surpassing the database market by 3x.
2. Companies selling inference directly (Anthropic at ~$10B/month, Google Cloud growing 63% at $80B run rate) are exploding in revenue.
3. "First derivative" companies like Datadog and Twilio don't sell inference but benefit enormously — Datadog's LLM observability spans tripled QoQ and AI customers represent 80% of ARR despite being only 20% of customers.
4. Extreme power-law concentration characterizes this cycle: a few AI-native customers can drive the majority of revenue for infrastructure platforms.
5. Every pre-AI company board must answer: how do we resell inference or benefit from our customers buying huge volumes of it? That's the only way out of the "Saaspocalypse."

## Synthesis
Tomasz Tunguz, a venture capitalist at Theory Ventures, frames the current AI moment with a compelling metaphor: the "first derivative of inference." Just as calculus reveals that the rate of change can be as informative as the value itself, companies that don't directly sell AI inference can still ride its explosive growth by providing essential infrastructure that AI builders depend on.

The raw numbers are staggering. Anthropic has booked approximately $9-10 billion in consecutive months, and Google Cloud is growing at 63% annually on an $80 billion revenue run rate. The inference market is projected to reach $250 billion within seven years, making it roughly three times the size of the database market — historically one of technology's largest categories. This isn't a niche; it's the new center of gravity for the entire software industry.

Tunguz's key insight is that this creates a gravitational pull that extends far beyond the hyperscalers and frontier model providers. He identifies Twilio and Datadog as standout examples among pre-AI public companies. Datadog's CEO Olivier Pomel reported that the number of spans sent to their LLM Observability product nearly tripled quarter-over-quarter, and while AI customers represent only 20% of total customers, they account for approximately 80% of annual recurring revenue. Twilio similarly benefits as "voice reimagined through the lens of AI" becomes an entry point for both AI-native startups and enterprises building voice agents.

This concentration is not a bug but a feature of the current cycle. Tunguz has previously written about the extreme concentration in NVIDIA's customer base, and the same pattern repeats: a small number of AI-heavy customers can transform the revenue trajectory of an entire platform company. For companies that predate the AI era, this is both an opportunity and an existential threat.

The article's stark conclusion is that for any pre-AI company, the board-level question must be: how do we either resell inference or benefit from our customers buying huge volumes of it? Tunguz calls this the only way out of the "Saaspocalypse" — the phenomenon where traditional SaaS companies face slowing growth as AI reshapes software economics. Those that find their "first derivative" relationship to inference can achieve spectacular growth; those that don't risk irrelevance. The message to founders and executives is clear: your relationship to the inference wave is now your single most important strategic variable.
