# How to Keep Your Writing Weird in the Age of AI
**Source**: https://every.to/working-overtime/how-to-keep-your-writing-weird-in-the-age-of-ai
**Date**: 2025-06-13
**Author**: Katie Parrott
**Keywords**: AI writing, lectio difficilior, textual criticism, voice, style, content marketing, creative writing, AI homogenization

## Elevator pitch
Katie Parrott draws on a 500-year-old principle of textual criticism — lectio difficilior potior (the harder reading is stronger) — to argue that AI is our era's "timid scribe" that smooths away the specific, weird edges that make writing memorable, and offers a framework for choosing when to preserve friction and when to let it go.

## Takeaways
- The principle of lectio difficilior potior — used by biblical scholars to identify original texts by preferring the more difficult reading — maps directly onto AI's tendency to simplify and homogenize writing
- AI consistently transforms specific, memorable language into generic alternatives: "altar-call energy" becomes "enthusiasm," "collecting screenshots like a doomsday prepper" becomes "documenting examples"
- The "timid scribe" framing positions AI not as malicious but as a well-meaning assistant that, like medieval copyists, smooths away difficulty in the name of clarity
- Parrott's counter-strategy involves loading AI with a personal style guide, writing samples, and explicit instructions about what to preserve versus what to polish
- The decision framework: ask what reaction you want (friction for thought-provoking work, smoothness for frictionless communication) and consider context (brand content vs. personal newsletter vs. column)

## Synthesis
Katie Parrott's essay is one of the more elegant conceptual frameworks for understanding what AI does to writing — and what writers can do about it. The central metaphor, drawn from the textual criticism principle of lectio difficilior potior ("the harder reading is stronger"), is both historically grounded and immediately practical. Biblical scholars facing two versions of an ancient text choose the more difficult one as original because they assume a later copyist — the "timid scribe" — smoothed away complexity. Parrott sees this dynamic playing out in real time with AI: given a distinctive sentence, the model reliably pushes it toward the statistical center of all similar writing ever produced.

The examples are visceral. LinkedIn's built-in AI transforms "Most Every subscribers don't know what they're paying for" into "Most Every subscribers may not be fully aware of what they're paying for" — adding hedges, softening language, and draining the sentence of the friction that makes a reader stop and think. Claude Opus 4, even with custom editorial instructions, pruned "deep in a rabbit hole" and "8 p.m. on a Tuesday" from her opening paragraph. The pattern is consistent: AI takes what's specific and makes it general, what's wrinkly and makes it smooth.

Parrott's response isn't rejection but negotiation. She loads her AI editor with extensive context: writing samples, style preferences, her beliefs about what makes writing work, and the specific crutches she wants the AI to detect. This creates a baseline closer to her voice, but the real skill is in the editing pass where she decides — case by case — which smoothing serves the piece and which destroys it. Her texture continuum model places different writing modes at different points: brand how-tos need frictionless delivery, personal newsletters embrace weird tangents, and her Every column sits in the middle.

The broader cultural argument is that as AI-generated content floods every channel, the writing that stands out will be writing that resists statistical prediction — that has edges, friction, and the specific gravity of a human mind. For marketers especially, this creates a paradox: professional writing has historically rewarded polish, but in an AI-saturated environment, polish is the commodity and weirdness is the premium.
