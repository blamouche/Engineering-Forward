# Researchers Asked LLMs for Strategic Advice. They Got "Trendslop" in Return.
**Source**: https://hbr.org/2026/03/researchers-asked-llms-for-strategic-advice-they-got-trendslop-in-return
**Date**: March 16, 2026
**Author**: Angelo Romasanta, Llewellyn D.W. Thomas, Natalia Levina
**Keywords**: LLMs, strategic advice, trendslop, AI trustworthiness, boardroom AI, generative AI, strategy formulation, AI evaluation

## Elevator pitch
When researchers tested LLMs on strategic business questions, they found the models produced polished-sounding but shallow "trendslop" — generic, buzzword-filled advice that mimics strategic thinking without delivering genuine insight or trustworthy recommendations.

## Takeaways
1. LLMs excel at producing fluent, confident-sounding strategic prose but their advice is often superficial, recycling common knowledge rather than generating novel insights
2. The term "trendslop" captures how LLMs blend trending buzzwords into seemingly coherent strategies that lack depth, context-specificity, and actionable substance
3. Leaders and consultants are increasingly integrating LLMs into executive decision-making workflows without sufficient evaluation of the quality of their outputs
4. The research highlights a critical gap between the apparent sophistication of LLM-generated strategy and its actual reliability for high-stakes business decisions
5. Organizations need structured evaluation frameworks to assess LLM strategic outputs rather than relying on surface-level plausibility

## Synthesis

As large language models become ubiquitous in corporate environments, a pressing question emerges: can they actually produce trustworthy strategic advice? Researchers Angelo Romasanta, Llewellyn D.W. Thomas, and Natalia Levina set out to answer this by systematically testing LLMs on strategic business questions. Their findings are sobering for anyone who sees these tools as boardroom-ready advisors.

The core finding is that LLMs generate what the researchers call "trendslop" — output that sounds impressive and strategically sophisticated on the surface, but upon closer examination proves to be generic, buzzword-laden, and lacking in genuine analytical depth. The models are exceptionally good at mimicking the form and language of strategic consulting — synthesizing information, producing clear arguments, and offering polished recommendations — but the substance often collapses under scrutiny.

This matters because adoption is racing ahead of evaluation. Leaders and consultants are increasingly turning to ChatGPT and similar tools as "silent partners in the boardroom," trusting them to summarize complex information and offer strategic recommendations in seconds. The speed and fluency of these outputs create an illusion of competence that can be dangerously misleading in high-stakes contexts.

The phenomenon of trendslop reflects fundamental limitations in how LLMs work. These models are pattern-matchers trained on vast corpora of text; they excel at reproducing the shape of strategic discourse without necessarily engaging in the kind of novel reasoning, counterfactual thinking, and context-specific analysis that genuine strategy requires. The result is advice that sounds right but may be dangerously generic — the strategic equivalent of a horoscope: plausible enough to feel personalized, but vague enough to apply to almost any situation.

The implications for organizations are significant. As LLMs become embedded in executive workflows, there's a risk that strategic decision-making becomes shallower rather than sharper — that the convenience of AI-generated analysis crowds out the difficult, context-rich thinking that real strategy demands. The researchers argue for the development of structured evaluation frameworks that can assess LLM outputs on dimensions beyond surface plausibility, including novelty, specificity, actionability, and alignment with organizational context.

The article serves as a timely corrective to the hype around AI in strategic roles. While LLMs have legitimate uses in strategy — synthesizing research, generating options, stress-testing assumptions — treating them as substitutes for human strategic judgment is premature and potentially dangerous. The polish of trendslop is not the same as the depth of genuine strategic insight.
