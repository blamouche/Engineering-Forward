# Jury Dismisses All Claims in Elon Musk's Lawsuit Against OpenAI CEO Sam Altman
**Source**: https://www.npr.org/2026/05/18/nx-s1-5822366/musk-altman-openai-jury-verdict-claims-dismissed
**Date**: May 18, 2026
**Author**: John Ruwitch
**Keywords**: Elon Musk, Sam Altman, OpenAI, lawsuit, jury verdict, statute of limitations, nonprofit, artificial intelligence, Microsoft

## Elevator pitch
A California jury unanimously dismissed Elon Musk's lawsuit against OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, ruling that Musk waited too long — beyond the statute of limitations — to file his 2024 case alleging breach of OpenAI's nonprofit mission.

## Takeaways
- The nine-member advisory jury deliberated less than two hours before ruling Musk filed beyond the statute of limitations; Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers agreed and dismissed the case
- The jury sidestepped the core question of whether Altman and co-founder Greg Brockman committed a "breach of charitable trust" by shifting OpenAI toward a for-profit model
- Musk's claims against Microsoft — which invested $13 billion in OpenAI between 2019 and 2023 — were also dismissed
- OpenAI's lead lawyer called the suit "a hypocritical attempt to sabotage a competitor"; Musk's attorney promised appeal: "This one is not over"
- The verdict interrupted a remedies hearing; if Musk had won, OpenAI and Microsoft could have faced up to $150 billion in damages and Altman's removal

## Synthesis
The three-week trial in Oakland, California ended not with a ruling on the merits of Musk's claims but on a procedural technicality: the jury found Musk filed too late. The nine-member advisory jury took less than two hours to reach its unanimous decision, and Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California agreed, tossing the case.

At the heart of the dispute was OpenAI's transformation from a nonprofit founded in 2015 — with a mission to create advanced AI for "the benefit of humanity" — into a company with a for-profit arm that attracted billions in investment. Musk, who provided $38 million in early funding and left the board in 2018 after a dispute over control, argued that Altman and co-founder Greg Brockman had "stolen a charity" by shifting employees, intellectual property, and resources into the for-profit entity while abandoning commitments to open sourcing and safety.

OpenAI's defense was twofold: first, that Musk himself had supported the creation of a for-profit subsidiary to attract investment; second, that his lawsuit was motivated by competitive interests — he launched xAI in 2023, a year and a half before suing. OpenAI's lead lawyer William Savitt characterized the case as "a hypocritical attempt to sabotage a competitor and to overcome a long history of very bad predictions about what OpenAI has been and will become."

The statute-of-limitations ruling meant the jury never addressed whether OpenAI actually breached a charitable trust — the question at the center of Musk's case. The jury apparently believed Musk was aware of the actions he later deemed breaches more than three years before filing suit in 2024. Musk immediately signaled appeal on social media, arguing the court "never actually ruled on the merits of the case, just on a calendar technicality."

The stakes were enormous. If Musk had prevailed, potential remedies included forcing OpenAI and Microsoft to "disgorge" up to $150 billion into OpenAI's nonprofit foundation, the removal of Altman and Brockman from their posts, and the dismantling of OpenAI's for-profit entity. The trial laid bare the fundamental tension at the heart of OpenAI's structure: a nonprofit mission housed within what has become one of the most valuable for-profit AI companies in the world.
