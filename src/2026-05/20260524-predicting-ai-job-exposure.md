# Predicting AI job exposure
**Source**: https://www.ben-evans.com/benedictevans/2026/5/24/ai-job-exposure
**Date**: May 24, 2026
**Author**: Benedict Evans
**Keywords**: AI, job automation, Jevons paradox, labor economics, technology disruption

## Elevator pitch
Attempts to quantify which jobs AI will disrupt are fundamentally unreliable — historical back-testing against computing, the internet, and smartphones shows that automation often increases employment in exposed fields, reshapes job definitions rather than eliminating them, and creates unpredictable second-order disruptions that no occupational taxonomy can capture.

## Takeaways
- Computing spent a century automating accounting, yet the number of accountants kept rising — the Jevons paradox (cheaper work → more work) and changing job definitions explain why
- Job titles are misleading: "accountants" do different things than in 1970, while adjacent roles like "billing machine operator" appear and vanish without the category itself changing
- The internet didn't change what journalists or A&R scouts did, but it destroyed the business models that paid their salaries — AI will similarly attack business models, not just tasks
- O*NET-style job descriptions are too crude to capture real job complexity, much like expert systems failed to describe cat recognition algorithmically
- Uber was an unpredictable smartphone disruption that no occupational exposure model would have caught — we should expect similar blind spots with AI
- "Directionally correct" predictions about which jobs are "exposed" may be as misleading as an internet-era prediction about physical distribution's demise, which meant totally different things for newspapers, music, TV, and film

## Synthesis
Benedict Evans mounts a thorough critique of the cottage industry of AI job-exposure prediction models — the viral charts, radar plots, and O*NET-based scoring systems that have proliferated since 2023. His argument is not that AI won't change jobs, but that our ability to predict *which* jobs, *how*, and with *what magnitude* is close to zero.

The first line of attack is back-testing against previous technology waves. Computing was the ultimate accounting automation tool — from punch cards to ERPs to spreadsheets — yet the number of accountants has grown steadily for a century. Evans identifies three mechanisms: the Jevons paradox (cheaper analysis means you do vastly more of it), regulatory changes (new rules create new accounting needs), and job redefinition (today's accountants do fundamentally different work under the same title). Census data reveals adjacent categories like "billing, posting and calculating machine operator" that appeared for a decade and disappeared again, absorbed into software with no trace in the occupational taxonomy.

The second problem is that AI will attack business models, not just job tasks. The internet didn't change what a good journalist did — it destroyed the classified-ad monopoly that funded journalism. AI will similarly decouple jobs from the business structures that support them. Someone might have a role with very low AI exposure, but their company's competitive moat might be a building full of people doing very automatable work. Evans calls this the "newspaper test" — would your model have predicted that the internet would gut newspapers?

Third, some of the most transformative effects are entirely unpredictable from occupational data. Uber emerged from smartphones and location data, but no one in 2005 would have put taxi drivers on a "smartphone exposure" list. The medallion mortgage market — hundred-million-dollar loans backed by taxi licenses — was wiped out by something no one saw coming. Evans suggests we are equally blind to AI's Uber-equivalents.

The deeper problem is epistemological: jobs are too complex and subtle to be described in the kind of discrete task lists that exposure models rely on. This is the same reason expert systems failed at image recognition — you can't write down the steps to recognize a cat, and you can't write down what an associate partner at a law firm actually does. Aaron Levie's "Gell-Mann Amnesia" variant captures the dynamic: you know your own field is too complex for AI to fully address, but you look at other fields and think they're toast.

Evans' conclusion is characteristically restrained: "directionally correct" predictions are often wrong in the ways that matter most. You don't know if the exceptions are bigger than the rule. Half the jobs you've analyzed might be unaffected, and entirely different pools of jobs might be transformed in ways you missed entirely. At this early stage, any specific prediction about a particular field will only be correct by luck.
