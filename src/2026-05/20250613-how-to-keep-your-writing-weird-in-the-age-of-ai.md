# How to Keep Your Writing Weird in the Age of AI
**Source**: https://every.to/working-overtime/how-to-keep-your-writing-weird-in-the-age-of-ai
**Date**: 2025-06-13
**Author**: Katie Parrott
**Keywords**: AI writing, textual criticism, lectio difficilior potior, AI smoothing, writing voice, editorial AI, creative process

## Elevator pitch
AI acts as a "timid scribe" that smooths away the distinctive edges of writing, and the key to preserving your voice is learning when to embrace friction over polish.

## Takeaways
- AI defaults to statistical averages, turning "altar-call energy" into "enthusiasm" and "collecting screenshots like a doomsday prepper" into "documenting examples"
- The principle of lectio difficilior potior ("the harder reading is stronger") from textual criticism perfectly describes what AI does to writing
- Building a style guide and custom instructions for AI can help it produce first drafts closer to your voice, but you remain the final arbiter of texture
- Different writing contexts call for different levels of smoothness: brand content needs frictionless clarity, personal newsletters thrive on weird edges
- Polish is an act of taste — in a world where anyone can produce professional-sounding text, the ability to create work with distinctive edges becomes more valuable

## Synthesis
Katie Parrott discovered a 500-year-old principle of textual criticism — lectio difficilior potior, "the harder reading is stronger" — that perfectly captures what happens when AI edits human writing. Medieval scribes copying ancient texts would smooth away difficulties they didn't understand, and AI does exactly the same thing: taking specific, textured prose and rendering it into professional-sounding generic text that slides by without friction.

The examples are striking. LinkedIn's AI turned "Most Every subscribers don't know what they're paying for" into "Most Every subscribers may not be fully aware of what they're paying for" — adding hedges, softening the blow, and erasing the very bluntness that might make a reader pause and think. Claude Opus 4 itself wanted to strip "deep in a rabbit hole" and "8 p.m. on a Tuesday" from Parrott's opening sentence in the name of editorial efficiency.

Parrott's practical approach involves giving AI extensive context — writing samples, beliefs about what makes writing work, known crutches to watch for — and using that to shape a style guide. The AI becomes brilliant at pulling out ideas, creating structure, remembering context, and generating options, but it consistently smooths away cultural references, emotional texture, productive tension, and the weird connections that make ideas memorable.

The framework she proposes is a continuum of texture: roughness on one end, smoothness on the other. Brand content and how-tos need frictionless clarity; personal newsletters thrive on weird tangents; editorial columns sit in the middle. The critical skill is knowing when smoothness serves your purpose and when roughness is exactly what the piece needs. As AI further saturates content production, the pieces that resist statistical prediction — the ones with edges — become more valuable, not less.
