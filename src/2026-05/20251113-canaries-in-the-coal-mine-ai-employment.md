# Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence
**Source**: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence
**Date**: 2025-11-13
**Author**: Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar, Ruyu Chen
**Keywords**: AI employment effects, generative AI, labor market, early-career workers, automation vs augmentation, Stanford Digital Economy Lab

## Elevator pitch
A landmark study using high-frequency payroll data finds that early-career workers (22-25) in AI-exposed occupations have experienced a 16% relative employment decline since widespread generative AI adoption, while experienced workers in the same fields remain stable.

## Takeaways
- Early-career workers (ages 22-25) in the most AI-exposed occupations experienced a 16% relative decline in employment, even after controlling for firm-level shocks
- Employment for experienced workers in the same AI-exposed occupations has remained stable or grown, suggesting AI disproportionately affects entry-level roles
- Adjustments occur primarily through employment (fewer hires) rather than compensation (wage cuts), indicating firms are reducing demand for junior workers rather than paying them less
- Employment declines are concentrated in occupations where AI automates rather than augments human labor — the automation/augmentation distinction has measurable labor market consequences
- The findings are robust to alternative explanations including excluding tech firms and remote-work-amenable occupations

## Synthesis
Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen bring empirical rigor to a question that has been debated mostly through anecdotes: is generative AI actually affecting employment? Using high-frequency administrative data from the largest US payroll software provider, they establish six clear facts about the labor market since the adoption of generative AI. The headline finding — a 16% relative decline for early-career workers in AI-exposed occupations — is striking both for its magnitude and its specificity. The effect is concentrated at the entry level, suggesting that AI is primarily substituting for tasks that junior workers would have performed.

The study's design makes it particularly credible. By controlling for firm-level shocks, the authors rule out the possibility that struggling companies are both cutting junior staff and adopting AI. The robustness checks — excluding tech firms and remote-work-compatible occupations — address the most obvious alternative explanations. The finding that employment adjusts through hiring rather than wages is consistent with the "automation of entry-level tasks" narrative: if AI can handle the work a junior employee would have done, there's simply less demand for that role.

The automation vs. augmentation distinction is analytically powerful. Occupations where AI augments human work (making experienced workers more productive) show stable employment. Those where AI automates (replacing human judgment with model output) show declines. This suggests policy and organizational responses should be differentiated: protecting augmentation-heavy roles while creating new pathways for workers in automation-heavy fields. The paper provides the first large-scale evidence that the AI revolution's labor market impacts are not theoretical — they've begun, and they're hitting the youngest workers hardest.
