# They Said It Would Cost $54 Million. We Said "No Thanks."
**Source**: https://nateglubish.substack.com/p/they-said-it-would-cost-54-million
**Date**: April 6, 2026
**Author**: Nate Glubish (Alberta Minister of Technology and Innovation)
**Keywords**: government-IT, AI-development, public-sector-innovation, cost-reduction, Claude, Copilot, Gemini, legacy-modernization

## Elevator pitch
Alberta's government used a small team of public servants with AI development tools to replace two failing legacy systems for $2.64M instead of the $54M vendors quoted — a 95% cost reduction delivered faster.

## Takeaways
- Three prior attempts since 2016 to replace two legacy government systems (asset tracking and construction project management) had all failed; vendor RFP responses quoted $54M for just one system over 4 years
- The PRISM Initiative, launched June 2025, deployed both systems in under a year with $858K spent so far — 643 public servants now use them daily
- AI's key role was accelerant, not replacement: Gemini processed 50+ hours of legacy system screen recordings into structured build specifications in minutes, at roughly one cent per image
- A cultural shift emerged organically: non-technical government staff started using tools like Loveable to prototype feature ideas, and 1,700+ employees enrolled in the Alberta AI Academy
- The model is explicitly replicable: domain expertise + modern AI tools + willingness to try, not a rare skill set

## Synthesis
This article, written by Alberta's Minister of Technology and Innovation, is a case study in how AI tools are fundamentally reshaping the economics of government IT. It's a story with a clear before-and-after: three failed procurement attempts since 2016, vendor bids of $54M for a single system, versus a small internal team that delivered both systems in under a year for a projected $2.64M total.

The most striking technical detail is how the team handled requirements gathering. Instead of paying consultants millions to watch 50+ hours of legacy system walkthrough videos and produce spec documents over months, they fed the video frames through Google Gemini's vision capabilities. The AI analyzed screen recordings and produced structured requirements, user flows, and data models — preserving every interface element, table structure, and data relationship — in minutes at a cost of roughly one cent per image.

The team's operating model is equally instructive: build fast, ship every two weeks, put working software in front of users immediately, and fix things quickly based on feedback. This created trust that no RFP process could buy — 170 Infrastructure staff voluntarily attend biweekly progress meetings to see what's coming next. When users flag issues, the team typically addresses them within two weeks, compared to the "we'll get back to you in four years" timeline of the traditional approach.

Minister Glubish is careful to credit the human expertise involved — Cohen McLeod and his team are experienced practitioners who understand the business processes they're digitizing. AI didn't replace skill; it amplified it. The piece also reveals an unexpected second-order effect: some non-technical government employees have started using AI prototyping tools on their own to sketch out feature ideas, representing a bottom-up culture shift that the 1,700-person Alberta AI Academy is designed to accelerate.

For anyone involved in enterprise or government software modernization, this article provides a compelling template: small empowered team, AI-amplified development, rapid iteration with real users, and a willingness to kill the traditional procurement playbook when the numbers don't add up.
