# The talent profile Europe's top AI scaleups are chasing
**Source**: https://sifted.eu/articles/the-talent-profile-europes-top-ai-scaleups-are-chasing-oped
**Date**: May 13, 2026
**Author**: Polly Barnes
**Keywords**: AI talent, European scaleups, founder DNA, startup culture, Promethean founders, resilience, risk tolerance, hiring, EQT Ventures, Wayve, Synthesia, Sana, 1X

## Elevator pitch
Europe's most ambitious AI scaleups are deliberately hiring "Promethean Founders" — individuals with founder DNA who've dared to build before, even (or especially) if they failed — because their resilience, comfort with chaos, and conviction under uncertainty are exactly what building in the AI era demands.

## Takeaways
- European AI scaleups like Wayve, Synthesia, Sana, and 1X are actively chasing candidates with "founder DNA" — people who have attempted to build a startup before, regardless of outcome.
- 75% of founders in EQT Ventures' Founder Six dataset score extremely high on resilience and risk tolerance, traits that are rare in the broader population and critical for high-growth AI environments.
- European culture stigmatises startup failure — only 17% of German press coverage portrays entrepreneurship positively vs. 39% in the US — creating a talent pool that's been systematically discouraged and undervalued.
- In Silicon Valley, a failed venture is a credential; in Europe, it's "something to explain away." This cultural gap means Europe's most capable builders are hiding in plain sight.
- The AI moment is forcing a cultural shift: companies that need people who thrive in radical uncertainty are recognising that those who've already "chosen a mission over safety" are exactly who they need.

## Synthesis
Polly Barnes, Operating Partner at EQT Ventures, argues that Europe's top AI scaleups are engaged in a talent hunt for a specific and rare archetype she calls "Promethean Founders." The term draws on the myth of Prometheus — someone who steals fire not for personal gain but because they believe the world should be different. These are individuals defined not by conventional success but by the fact that they dared to build at all.

The article's central thesis is that the European startup ecosystem has systematically undervalued its most potentially valuable talent by stigmatising failure. Drawing on research from Ali Tamaseb's "Super Founders," Barnes notes that many of the world's most successful founders only got there on their second, third, or tenth attempt. Europe has no shortage of people who've made a first attempt, but the culture treats that attempt as a failure rather than a credential. This produces not only fewer serial founders but a broader talent market that discourages capable people from ever discovering what they're capable of — and then fails to recognise them when they do.

The data from EQT's Founder Six framework, built from over 3,000 aspiring and active founders, reveals that 75% of the founder pool scores extremely highly on resilience and risk tolerance. These traits don't guarantee success, but they're extraordinarily rare in the general population, and people who carry both are significantly more likely to start companies and build things from nothing.

The practical test Barnes and the talent leads she's spoken with apply is simple: how does someone behave when things break? The distinction isn't between those who tolerate uncertainty and those who don't — it's between those who tolerate it and those who genuinely thrive in it. A Promethean Founder has already lived in that environment at its most unforgiving and is choosing to do it again.

The cultural contrast with Silicon Valley is stark. In the US, a venture that didn't reach its potential is proof of conviction, resilience, and readiness. In Europe, it's more commonly a liability. McKinsey research cited in the piece shows only 17% of German press coverage portrays entrepreneurship positively, compared to 39% in the United States. This stigmatisation makes European founders more risk-averse and makes it harder both to try and to recover.

Barnes concludes that this is changing. The AI moment demands exactly the Promethean profile: people who have chosen mission over safety, demonstrated comfort with chaos, proven they can build under pressure, and carry the scars to show for it. Europe's best AI companies — Wayve, Synthesia, Sana, 1X, and others — have already figured this out. The rest of the market, she suggests, is catching up. The implication is clear: Europe's competitive advantage in AI may depend not on capital or infrastructure but on its willingness to embrace and empower the builders it has spent decades discouraging.
