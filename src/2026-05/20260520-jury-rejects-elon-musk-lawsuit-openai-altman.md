# Jury rejects Elon Musk's lawsuit against OpenAI and its CEO Sam Altman
**Source**: https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/05/18/jury-rejects-elon-musk-lawsuit-against-openai-its-ceo-sam-altman/
**Date**: May 18, 2026
**Author**: The Washington Post
**Keywords**: Elon Musk, Sam Altman, OpenAI, lawsuit, jury verdict, dismissed, statute of limitations, charitable trust, federal trial, Oakland

## Elevator pitch
A federal jury in Oakland, California, dismissed all of Elon Musk's claims against OpenAI and CEO Sam Altman, ruling the lawsuit was filed too late under the statute of limitations — and Musk attacked the presiding judge online in the aftermath.

## Takeaways
- The jury rejected all of Musk's claims in the federal trial, finding they were filed outside the applicable three-year statute of limitations period.
- Musk reacted to the verdict by attacking the judge in an online post, escalating the combative tone of the dispute beyond the courtroom.
- The case centered on whether OpenAI's 2025 for-profit restructuring violated its original nonprofit charitable mission, which Musk claimed to have helped fund with $38 million in early donations.
- The ruling was procedural rather than substantive — the court never addressed the merits of whether OpenAI's transformation constituted a "breach of charitable trust."
- The verdict removes a significant legal overhang for OpenAI as it prepares for what is expected to be one of the largest tech IPOs in history.
- Musk has indicated plans to appeal the decision to the 9th Circuit U.S. Court of Appeals.

## Synthesis

The Washington Post's coverage of the Musk v. OpenAI jury verdict focuses on the finality of the dismissal and the personal acrimony that has come to define the feud between two of tech's most prominent figures. While the CNBC report emphasizes the financial stakes and competitive dynamics, the Post's framing highlights the judicial process and Musk's combative reaction — specifically his decision to attack the judge in an online post following the adverse ruling.

The verdict itself was sweeping: all of Musk's claims were dismissed. The jury did not split hairs between different alleged breaches or find partial merit in any argument; it was a complete rejection on procedural grounds. The three-year statute of limitations proved to be an insurmountable hurdle for Musk's legal team, which had sought to characterize OpenAI's evolution from research nonprofit to commercial juggernaut as an ongoing harm rather than a discrete event with a fixed start date.

Musk's online attack on the judge adds a layer of personal drama to what was already an extraordinarily tense proceeding. For a plaintiff simultaneously pursuing what may be the largest IPO in history (SpaceX at $1.25 trillion), publicly criticizing the presiding judge is a notable choice — one that could have implications for the appeal if it suggests a lack of respect for the judicial process. The Post's coverage of this detail distinguishes its report from CNBC's more business-focused framing.

The procedural nature of the dismissal leaves the substantive legal question unresolved: can the founders of a nonprofit AI research organization restructure it into a for-profit entity worth hundreds of billions of dollars without running afoul of charitable trust law? That question will either be addressed on appeal to the 9th Circuit or remain an open issue for future litigants. For now, the precedent set is narrow — file your claims on time — rather than broad guidance on AI governance.

The verdict arrives at a moment when the governance of AI companies is under increasing scrutiny from regulators, the public, and the industry itself. OpenAI's trajectory from idealistic nonprofit to $850 billion company has been extraordinary, and the lawsuit forced a public airing of internal communications, early funding decisions, and the personal relationships that shaped the company. Even though the case was dismissed on procedural grounds, the trial record it generated may influence how future AI ventures structure themselves and how early donors negotiate their contributions. The Musk-Altman feud, once a friendship, has now produced a legal record that will be studied for years — even if it didn't deliver the dramatic restructuring Musk sought.
