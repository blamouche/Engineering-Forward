# Researchers Asked LLMs for Strategic Advice. They Got "Trendslop" in Return.
**Source**: https://hbr.org/2026/03/researchers-asked-llms-for-strategic-advice-they-got-trendslop-in-return
**Date**: 2026-03-16
**Author**: Angelo Romasanta, Llewellyn D.W. Thomas, Natalia Levina
**Keywords**: LLMs, strategy, trendslop, AI bias, strategic advice, prompt engineering, management consulting, GPT-5, Claude, decision-making

## Elevator pitch
When researchers tested six leading LLMs on seven core strategic tensions, they found the models consistently recommended the same popular-sounding strategies regardless of company context — a phenomenon they call "trendslop" — revealing that AI-generated strategic advice is a statistical popularity contest dressed as analysis.

## Takeaways
- Across thousands of simulations with GPT-5, Claude, Gemini, Grok, DeepSeek, and Mistral, LLMs uniformly favored differentiation, long-term thinking, augmentation, and collaboration — defensible choices that became meaningless because they were applied without regard to specific business context
- The bias stems from training data: words like "collaboration" and "innovation" appear in overwhelmingly positive contexts, creating a probability landscape where models complete prompts toward popular vocabulary rather than contextually appropriate analysis
- Prompt engineering barely helps: across 15,000 trials, adding company-specific context shifted biased responses by only 11% from baseline, and better prompting reduced bias by less than 2% on key tensions
- When allowed to avoid binary choices, models frequently recommended pursuing contradictory strategies simultaneously (both differentiation and cost leadership) — what strategy scholars call being "stuck in the middle" — dressing up evasion as nuance
- The safest approach is to treat LLM strategic recommendations as a first draft requiring interrogation: use them to generate options, surface risks, and test opposite positions, not as conclusions requiring execution

## Synthesis
The Harvard Business Review article by Romasanta, Thomas, and Levina introduces "trendslop" — a term that captures the systematic tendency of large language models to recommend strategies aligned with current managerial fashion rather than the specific logic of the business asking for advice. This is not a problem of AI being wrong; it's a problem of AI defaulting to the most popular answer in its training data, which makes the advice useless precisely because it sounds right.

The researchers constructed a rigorous experimental design, testing six frontier models — GPT-5, Claude, Gemini, Grok, DeepSeek, and Mistral — across seven fundamental strategic tensions that every business must navigate. These tensions included exploration versus exploitation, centralization versus decentralization, short-term versus long-term performance, competition versus collaboration, radical versus incremental innovation, differentiation versus commoditization, and automation versus augmentation. Each tension represents a genuine trade-off with real consequences on either side.

The results were striking in their consistency. On six of the seven tensions, the models showed a uniform directional bias. Only the exploration-versus-exploitation dimension produced meaningful variation across models. The models reliably picked differentiation over cost leadership, augmentation over automation, long-term over short-term thinking, collaboration over competition. These are defensible positions — differentiation is often good strategy, long-term thinking is often wise. The problem is that the models chose them regardless of company context, which means they weren't doing strategy at all. They were running a statistical popularity contest, and the most-liked ideas in the training data won every time.

The mechanism behind trendslop is architectural rather than incidental. LLMs are trained on internet text that contains deeply embedded valence patterns. "Collaboration," "innovation," and "sustainability" appear in overwhelmingly positive contexts. "Cost leadership," "centralization," and "automation" do not. The model doesn't understand why these words carry their emotional weight; it simply learns the statistical association. Once a buzzword like "differentiation" appears early in a response, subsequent tokens become statistically more likely to follow patterns from the training data that align with that word's positive associations. The output reads like reasoned analysis, but the underlying process is token prediction, not contextual reasoning.

Perhaps the most damning finding concerns prompt engineering. In more than 15,000 trials, the researchers tested every standard technique: reversing option order, adding detailed company descriptions, requesting deeper analysis, specifying the decision-making framework. Adding context shifted biased responses by only 11% from baseline. Better prompting reduced bias by less than 2% on the differentiation and augmentation tensions specifically. Option order alone accounted for 19% of bias variation — suggesting that superficial phrasing choices affect output far more than substantive context does. This directly contradicts the prevailing industry guidance that better prompts produce better strategic reasoning.

The study identified a particularly dangerous failure mode called the "hybrid trap." When models were allowed to avoid binary choices, they frequently recommended pursuing contradictory strategies simultaneously — both differentiation and cost leadership, or both radical and incremental innovation. In strategy scholarship, this condition is called being "stuck in the middle," and it is widely recognized as a path to competitive disadvantage. The model, unconstrained by the real-world impossibility of doing everything at once, generated advice that eliminated trade-offs by pretending both sides could be chosen. This isn't strategic integration; it's evasion dressed up as sophisticated nuance.

The implications extend beyond academic interest. According to McKinsey's 2025 State of AI report, 88% of companies now use AI regularly in at least one business function, and strategy is one of the top three functions where firms report revenue impact. Most of those companies have no systematic method for testing whether AI-generated strategic advice is calibrated to their situation. For brand strategy specifically, trendslop is particularly damaging because differentiation isn't one option among many — it's the entire point. A brand that builds its positioning with AI receives advice optimized for the generic center.

The researchers' practical recommendations are sobering: treat LLM strategic recommendations as a first draft, not a conclusion; use models to generate options and surface risks rather than select answers; test opposite positions to pressure-test reasoning; and keep final strategic judgment firmly in human hands. The businesses most at risk from trendslop are not those that distrust AI but those that use it without a reference point — accepting polished, confident-sounding advice because they have no independent framework against which to test it.
