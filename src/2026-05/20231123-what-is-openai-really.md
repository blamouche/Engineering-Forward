# What is OpenAI, Really?
**Source**: https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/what-is-openai
**Date**: 2023-11-23
**Author**: Gergely Orosz
**Keywords**: OpenAI, Sam Altman, corporate governance, nonprofit, ChatGPT, Microsoft, AI safety, board conflict, startup culture, engineering culture

## Elevator pitch
The November 2023 OpenAI leadership crisis—where CEO Sam Altman was fired, nearly all 778 employees threatened to quit, and Altman was reinstated within five days—exposed the fundamental and unresolved tension between OpenAI's nonprofit governance structure and its for-profit commercial ambitions.

## Takeaways
- OpenAI's board fired CEO Sam Altman on November 17, 2023, triggering a crisis that nearly dissolved the company as 95% of staff signed a letter threatening to join Microsoft
- The board acted without notifying Microsoft, OpenAI's largest investor with $10B committed, and without the board chair Greg Brockman present
- Chief Scientist Ilya Sutskever reportedly orchestrated the coup over concerns about the speed of commercialization and AI safety, particularly after the November 6 Dev Day event
- Within five days, Altman was reinstated as CEO with a restructured board, but the underlying governance tension between nonprofit mission and for-profit execution remained unresolved
- The crisis revealed OpenAI's unusual corporate structure: a nonprofit board controlling a capped-profit entity, creating a governance framework at odds with the company's commercial trajectory

## Synthesis
This article, originally published as a paid subscriber analysis and later un-paywalled at subscriber request, provides a detailed timeline and structural analysis of what the author describes as "the most insane 100 hours" in OpenAI's history. The events of November 17-22, 2023, represent one of the most dramatic corporate governance crises in tech history, and the analysis illuminates structural tensions that remain relevant to understanding AI industry dynamics.

The timeline itself reads like a thriller: Friday noon, Altman is fired by a board meeting he attended; by Friday evening, president Greg Brockman quits; Saturday brings news that Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella is furious and that chief scientist Ilya Sutskever led the coup over AI safety and commercialization speed concerns; Sunday sees a frantic attempt to reinstate Altman, which fails when the board instead appoints Twitch cofounder Emmett Shear as interim CEO; Monday brings Nadella's announcement that Altman and Brockman will join Microsoft, followed by 743 of 778 OpenAI employees signing a letter threatening to follow them; Tuesday night, a deal is struck and Altman returns.

The article's crucial contribution is explaining *why* this happened through the lens of OpenAI's corporate structure. OpenAI began as a nonprofit in 2015 with a board controlling that nonprofit. In 2019, a "capped profit" entity was created—owned and controlled by the nonprofit. This created a board with fiduciary duties to a nonprofit mission at the same time the company was racing to commercialize ChatGPT, which had become the fastest-growing consumer application in history. The board's stated reason—that Altman was "not consistently candid in his communications"—reflected this structural tension more than any specific incident.

The analysis raises deeper questions about what OpenAI actually is: a research lab, a product company, or something in between? The nonprofit governance was designed for a research organization pursuing safe AGI; the company's actual operations had become a fiercely competitive product company shipping consumer AI at unprecedented speed. This fundamental misalignment between governance structure and operational reality created exactly the conditions for the November crisis—and the resolution (Altman's return with a restructured board) addressed the immediate crisis without resolving the underlying structural tension.

For engineers and tech leaders, this case study remains essential reading for understanding how AI companies balance (or fail to balance) commercial pressure, safety concerns, and governance frameworks. The OpenAI crisis of 2023 prefigured many of the governance and trust questions that would intensify as AI deployment accelerated through 2024-2026.
