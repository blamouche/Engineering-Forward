# Exclusive: EQT Wants to Back UK Startups with EU's €5bn Superfund
**Source**: https://sifted.eu/articles/exclusive-eqt-uk-startups-scaleup
**Date**: May 22, 2026
**Author**: Maya Dharampal-Hornby, Freya Pratty, John Thornhill
**Keywords**: EQT, EU ScaleUp Fund, UK startups, venture capital, European Investment Fund, Brexit, cross-border investment

## Elevator pitch
Swedish investment firm EQT, newly appointed to manage the EU's €5bn ScaleUp Fund, wants to invest in UK startups but currently lacks the mandate to do so, highlighting ongoing post-Brexit complexities in European venture capital.

## Takeaways
- EQT was appointed to manage the European Union's €5bn ScaleUp Fund, a significant public-private initiative to boost European tech scaleups.
- EQT's CEO told Sifted the firm would like to invest in UK companies but the fund's current mandate restricts it to EU member states.
- The UK's exclusion stems from Brexit: the UK is not part of the European Investment Fund and doesn't contribute to the EU programs backing the fund.
- UK government representatives have expressed interest in finding a mechanism for UK startups to access the fund, but no structure currently exists.
- This highlights a broader challenge: despite the UK remaining Europe's largest venture capital market, post-Brexit structures limit cross-border public investment.

## Synthesis
The EU's €5bn ScaleUp Fund represents one of the bloc's most ambitious attempts to address Europe's long-standing scaleup funding gap. Appointing EQT — one of Europe's largest and most sophisticated investment firms — to manage the fund signals serious intent to deploy capital at institutional scale. But the exclusive revelation that EQT wants to invest in UK startups and cannot exposes a persistent friction in post-Brexit European venture capital.

The UK remains Europe's most mature startup ecosystem, home to the majority of unicorns and exits. Excluding UK companies from the EU's flagship scaleup initiative creates an artificial border through Europe's innovation landscape. While the UK has its own programs — including British Patient Capital and the National Wealth Fund — these operate at smaller scale and with different mandates.

EQT CEO's acknowledgment that they "would like to back UK startups" paired with the admission that "the fund's mandate doesn't allow it" illustrates the gap between investor intentions and political reality. The situation is not necessarily permanent: UK government officials have expressed interest in exploring a mechanism for participation, potentially through associate membership of relevant EU programs or a bilateral agreement.

For UK startups, the immediate impact is limited — they continue to access European VC through private funds. But the longer-term concern is structural: as the EU deepens capital market integration and builds its own venture ecosystem, UK startups risk being increasingly excluded from institutional European capital flows. The EQT situation is a case study in how politics continues to shape capital allocation years after the Brexit vote.
