# Compound Engineering Gets an Upgrade: From Four Steps to Eight
**Source**: https://every.to/guides/compound-engineering
**Date**: 2026-05-29
**Author**: Kieran Klaassen (Every)
**Keywords**: compound-engineering, ai-native, workflow, ideation, brainstorm, planning, polish, human-in-the-loop, sandwich-model, Every

## Elevator pitch
Kieran Klaassen expands the compound engineering loop from four steps to eight — ideate, brainstorm, plan, work, review, polish, compound — because as AI handles the middle of the cycle, humans now spend most of their attention at the beginning (deciding what to build) and the end (making the result feel right).

## Takeaways
- The original compound engineering loop was: brainstorm → work → review → compound → repeat
- The expanded loop is: ideate → brainstorm → plan → work → review → polish → compound → repeat, with ideate and brainstorm added to the front and polish to the end
- The expansion reflects that the work phase has become "boring in the best way" — if the plan is good and the agent has the right context, it usually does the work right, writing code and running tests autonomously
- The "sandwich" metaphor describes the new human role: AI is the filling in the middle, humans are the bread on either end holding it together — deciding what is worth building at the start, and evaluating whether the experience feels right at the end
- The updated compound engineering plugin ships 43 subagents and 38 slash-command skills
- The pattern applies beyond engineering to knowledge work broadly: the middle of a lot of work will get automated, but humans still need to be present at the beginning and end for the work to be good and to feel like theirs

## Synthesis
Almost a year after coining "compound engineering," Kieran Klaassen has expanded the framework to reflect how the practice has evolved. The original loop — brainstorm, work, review, compound — was mostly about code: getting an AI model to make a plan, do the work, review the results, and incorporate feedback. The work phase has since become routine. If the plan is good and the agent has the right context, it usually executes correctly, writing code, running tests, and fixing obvious issues without human intervention.

The question that remains is where the human fits in. The answer, according to collaborator Trevin Chow, is a sandwich: AI is the filling, humans are the bread on either end. At the beginning, the human decides what is worth building — understanding the user, the product, edge cases, and what feels exciting enough to spend time on. At the end, the human comes back to click around, read the copy, and ask whether the experience feels right. Sometimes everything technically works but the product is still not good, and that is where human judgment makes it better.

The expanded loop adds three steps. Ideate and brainstorm move to the front of the process — before there is even a plan, the human needs to decide what is worth building. Polish moves to the end — after the work technically passes review, the human refines the experience until it genuinely feels right. Compound remains the most important step, because the entire philosophy rests on the principle that every feature should make the next feature easier.

The updated compound engineering plugin now ships 43 subagents and 38 slash-command skills, operationalizing the expanded loop as a system that engineers can run inside their development environment. The guide is framed as engineering-specific, but Klaassen argues the pattern applies to knowledge work much more broadly. The middle of a lot of work — drafting, research, data processing — will get automated. But if you want the work to be good and to feel like yours, you still need to be there at the beginning and the end.
