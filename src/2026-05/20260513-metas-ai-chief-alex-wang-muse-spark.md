# Meta's AI Chief Alex Wang on Muse Spark and the AI Wars
**Source**: https://www.corememory.com/p/metas-ai-chief-alex-wang-muse-spark-ai-wars
**Date**: 2026-05-13
**Author**: Ashlee Vance (Core Memory podcast)

## Elevator pitch
In his first public interview since joining Meta in a $14 billion deal, former Scale AI CEO Alex Wang discusses leading Meta's AI turnaround, the launch of the Muse Spark model, personal tensions with Sam Altman, and Meta's controversial pivot from open-source to closed-source frontier models.

## Takeaways
- Meta acquired Alex Wang from Scale AI in a deal valued at $14 billion in June 2025, with Mark Zuckerberg positioning him as the "rescue plan" for Meta's fading position in the AI race
- Wang's first major deliverable, Muse Spark, launched in April 2026 as a ground-up overhaul—a closed-source model built from scratch in nine months by the new Meta Superintelligence Labs unit
- The podcast reveals Wang's personal "beef" with Sam Altman, extraordinary compensation packages Meta has been handing out to AI talent, and Zuckerberg personally delivering soup to AI recruits
- Muse Spark marks a strategic departure from Meta's traditional open-source Llama approach—it's purpose-built for Meta's products (Instagram, Facebook, Threads) rather than released as open weights
- The Meta AI hierarchy now includes "all-stars" like Nat Friedman, Daniel Gross, and Shengjia Zhao, assembled under Wang's leadership at the newly formed lab

## Synthesis

The Core Memory interview with Alex Wang is the first window into one of the most consequential talent acquisitions in AI history. When Meta paid $14 billion to bring Wang over from Scale AI in June 2025, the terms were extraordinary even by Silicon Valley standards—but so was the problem. Meta, the company that had pioneered open-source AI with Llama, was being lapped by OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google in the frontier model race. Zuckerberg could feel the company "fading," and Wang was the bet-the-company response.

The interview (and Wang's appearance—"a mullet and a powerful whitetail deer camouflage shirt") signals a deliberate departure from the polished corporate communications typical of Big Tech AI launches. Wang is positioning himself as an outsider-insider, someone who built a company from scratch and is now trying to bring that urgency to a trillion-dollar corporation. The mullet-and-camo aesthetic is a message: this isn't going to be a conventional corporate AI effort.

The substance of what Wang has built is captured in Muse Spark, which launched in April 2026 as the first model from Meta Superintelligence Labs. The model represents a ground-up rebuild over nine months—remarkably fast for a frontier model development cycle—and notably abandons Meta's open-source tradition. Unlike Llama, Muse Spark is closed-source and purpose-built for Meta's products. This is a strategic pivot of enormous consequence. Meta's previous AI strategy used open-source as an economic weapon: one-time training cost, zero inference cost for Meta, market pressure on competitor pricing. Muse Spark flips that logic, suggesting Wang convinced Zuckerberg that winning the AI race requires proprietary models that are deeply integrated into Meta's product ecosystem rather than commoditized through open release.

The personal dynamics revealed in the interview add texture to the AI industry's competitive landscape. Wang's "personal beef" with Sam Altman hints at the interpersonal rivalries that now shape multi-billion-dollar corporate strategies. The detail about Zuckerberg personally delivering soup to AI recruits illustrates the intensity of the talent war—when the CEO of a company with $200 billion in ad revenue is doing soup delivery for prospective hires, the competition for AI researchers has reached a level of absurdity that reflects genuine desperation.

The broader Meta AI hierarchy that Wang has assembled is impressive: Nat Friedman (former GitHub CEO), Daniel Gross (former Y Combinator partner and Apple AI lead), and Shengjia Zhao (a researcher known for work on AI alignment and capabilities). This is not a team of Meta lifers adapting to AI; it's a collection of outside talent that would be credible as the founding team of any AI lab, now operating inside one of the world's largest companies.

The interview's timing—Wang's first public appearance after nearly a year of silence—suggests Meta is entering a new phase. The quiet-building period is over; now they need to convince the market that Meta can compete at the frontier. The Core Memory podcast, hosted by Ashlee Vance (author of the Elon Musk biography), is a deliberate choice of venue—not a press release or a tech conference keynote, but a long-form conversation on a platform associated with unfiltered founder interviews. Wang isn't just selling a model; he's selling a narrative about Meta's AI comeback, and he's doing it through the medium that tech culture trusts most: the founder podcast.
