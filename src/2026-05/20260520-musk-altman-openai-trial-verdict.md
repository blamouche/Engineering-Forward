# Musk slams Altman trial verdict as a 'technicality,' vows to appeal
**Source**: https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/18/musk-altman-openai-trial-verdict.html
**Date**: May 18, 2026
**Author**: Jeffrey Kopp and Lora Kolodny (CNBC)
**Keywords**: Elon Musk, Sam Altman, OpenAI, lawsuit, jury verdict, statute of limitations, breach of charitable trust, Microsoft, xAI, SpaceX

## Elevator pitch
A federal jury in Oakland ruled against Elon Musk in his lawsuit against OpenAI and Sam Altman, finding his claims of breach of charitable trust were filed outside the three-year statute of limitations — Musk called it a "calendar technicality" and vowed to appeal.

## Takeaways
- The advisory jury deliberated less than two hours before finding Musk's claims fell outside the statute of limitations; Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers immediately adopted the verdict.
- Musk sought up to $180 billion in "ill-gotten gains" from OpenAI and Microsoft, plus the removal of Altman and President Greg Brockman from leadership.
- Musk testified he gave roughly $38 million to OpenAI on the understanding it would develop AI "for the benefit of humanity," not personal enrichment.
- OpenAI's lawyers portrayed the lawsuit as competitive sabotage, noting Musk started rival xAI (now part of SpaceX) and had previously pushed for OpenAI to fold into Tesla under his control.
- Both companies are racing toward historic IPOs: OpenAI at $850B+ valuation, SpaceX (with xAI) at $1.25 trillion.
- The trial featured testimony from Altman, Brockman, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella, and Musk himself over three weeks.

## Synthesis

The jury verdict in Elon Musk v. OpenAI represents a decisive — though potentially temporary — legal victory for Sam Altman and OpenAI in what has become one of the most closely watched tech industry lawsuits of the decade. The case strikes at fundamental questions about the governance of AI companies, the enforceability of founding promises, and whether charitable nonprofit structures can evolve into for-profit enterprises worth hundreds of billions of dollars.

The jury's reasoning was procedural rather than substantive. After less than two hours of deliberation, the advisory jury found that Musk's claims of "breach of charitable trust" were filed outside California's three-year statute of limitations. Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers adopted the verdict immediately and expressed skepticism about Musk's intent to appeal, stating she was prepared to dismiss it "on the spot" and noting "a substantial amount of evidence to support the jury's finding." This procedural outcome means the court never ruled on the core question: whether Altman and Brockman actually breached fiduciary duties by restructuring OpenAI from a nonprofit into a for-profit entity.

The stakes were enormous. Musk's legal team sought to force OpenAI and Microsoft to disgorge up to $180 billion in "ill-gotten gains," remove Altman and Brockman from leadership, and unwind the company's 2025 restructuring. Musk explicitly stated any recovered money should go to "the OpenAI charity" rather than to him personally — a framing designed to position himself as a defender of the public interest rather than a competitor seeking damages. He testified that his approximately $38 million in early donations were made on the understanding that OpenAI would remain a nonprofit developing AI for humanity's benefit.

OpenAI's defense painted a very different picture. Their lawyers argued Musk's donations carried no restrictions, and that restructuring was a competitive necessity in the expensive race against Google DeepMind. They introduced evidence that Musk himself had proposed a for-profit structure — on the condition he retain control — and had even pushed for OpenAI to be absorbed into Tesla. This narrative framed the lawsuit not as principle-driven philanthropy defense but as competitive retaliation: Musk, having failed to gain control of OpenAI, started his own AI lab (xAI, now merged with SpaceX) and then sued to hobble the competition.

The trial provided a rare courtroom spectacle, with testimony from Altman, Brockman, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella, and Musk himself over three weeks. William Savitt, OpenAI's lead attorney, characterized the verdict as substantive rather than technical: "You brought your claims too late, and you did it because you were sitting on them to use them as a weapon of a competitor who can't compete in the marketplace."

The timing of the verdict is particularly significant given both companies' trajectories toward public markets. OpenAI raised $122 billion at an $850 billion-plus valuation in late March, while SpaceX confidentially filed for an IPO in April at a $1.25 trillion valuation following its merger with xAI. A ruling against OpenAI could have complicated its IPO narrative; the dismissal removes a major legal overhang, though Musk's promised appeal to the 9th Circuit ensures the saga isn't over.

Musk's post-verdict reaction on X — calling it a "calendar technicality" — previews the appeal strategy: argue that the clock on the statute of limitations should have started later, when the alleged harm became apparent rather than when the restructuring occurred. The appeal will test whether procedural time-bar rulings can be overcome when the underlying conduct involves a charity allegedly being "stolen" — a framing with both legal and public relations implications. For now, however, OpenAI and Microsoft have won the first round decisively.
