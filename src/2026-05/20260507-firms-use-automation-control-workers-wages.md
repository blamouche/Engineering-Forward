# Study: Firms often use automation to control certain workers' wages
**Source**: https://news.mit.edu/2026/study-firms-often-use-automation-control-certain-workers-wages-0507
**Date**: May 7, 2026
**Author**: Peter Dizikes, MIT News
**Keywords**: automation, wages, inequality, productivity, Daron Acemoglu, labor economics, wage premium, rent dissipation

## Elevator pitch
An MIT study by Nobel laureate Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo finds that since 1980, US firms have disproportionately used automation to eliminate workers earning a "wage premium"—higher-than-expected pay—rather than to maximize productivity, explaining 52% of income inequality growth while offsetting 60-90% of automation's potential productivity gains.

## Takeaways
- Automation accounts for 52% of US income inequality growth from 1980-2016, with about 10 percentage points coming specifically from firms targeting workers who earned more than comparable peers.
- The largest automation effects hit workers in the 70th-95th percentile of their salary range—not the lowest-paid or highest-paid workers.
- Firms' focus on wage control rather than productivity optimization has "offset 60-90 percent of the productivity gains from automation," helping explain the long-standing productivity paradox.
- The study analyzed 500 detailed demographic groups (by education, gender, age, ethnicity) across 49 industries, creating one of the most granular pictures of automation's labor market effects.
- "You can reduce costs while reducing productivity"—the study reveals a basic but underappreciated distinction where greater profitability and increased productivity are not the same thing.

## Synthesis
This MIT study, published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, makes a compelling and counterintuitive argument about automation's effects on the US economy. The conventional narrative frames automation as an inevitable technological wave that replaces workers broadly in pursuit of efficiency. Acemoglu and Restrepo's data tells a different, more unsettling story: firms have systematically used automation to target specific workers—those earning a "wage premium" above what comparable workers make—to control labor costs, even when doing so reduces overall productivity.

The methodology is notably rigorous. The researchers cross-referenced data from the US Census Bureau, the American Community Survey, and industry sources to analyze 500 demographic groups across five education levels, gender, age, and ethnicity, within 49 industries. This granularity allows them to distinguish between automation that broadly displaces workers and automation that specifically targets higher-earning workers who have managed to secure above-market wages—often non-college-educated workers who had obtained better-than-average salaries through experience, union membership, or favorable local conditions.

The headline numbers are striking. Automation explains 52% of income inequality growth from 1980 to 2016, with 10 percentage points directly attributable to firms eliminating wage premiums. Workers in the 70th-95th salary percentile within their demographic group bear the brunt—not the lowest earners or the highest. This suggests automation's inequality effects operate through a specific mechanism: degrading the earnings of workers who had managed to climb above their expected wage bracket.

The productivity implications are equally significant. The study estimates that inefficiency in automation targeting—choosing wage-reducing automation over genuinely productivity-enhancing technology—has offset 60-90% of automation's potential productivity gains. This directly addresses a puzzle that has vexed economists since Robert Solow's 1987 quip: "You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics." The answer, according to Acemoglu and Restrepo, is that firms are optimizing for profit, not productivity—and the two are not the same.

A concrete example illuminates the mechanism: a call center might adopt automation technology that actually makes operations less efficient but reduces the wage bill enough to increase profits. Firm managers have incentive to make this choice because the cost savings accrue to them, even as the productivity loss affects the broader economy. "If managers can reduce productivity by 1 percent but increase profits, many of them might be happy with that," Acemoglu observes.

The study has important policy implications that go beyond academic economics. First, it suggests that simply encouraging more automation will not necessarily deliver productivity growth—the type of automation matters as much as the amount. Second, it implies that protecting workers' ability to earn wage premiums (through collective bargaining, labor standards, or minimum wage policy) may be essential to ensuring automation is deployed productively rather than punitively. Third, it challenges the technological-determinism narrative that treats automation's effects as inevitable rather than the result of specific firm-level decisions.

Acemoglu's conclusion is both sobering and hopeful: "We could be missing out on potentially even better productivity gains by calibrating the type and extent of automation more carefully, and in a more productivity-enhancing way. It's all a choice, 100 percent." The study doesn't argue for less automation per se, but for automation that genuinely enhances human productivity rather than simply replacing human labor. The distinction has been largely absent from public discourse about AI and the future of work.

The paper adds to Acemoglu and Restrepo's substantial body of work on automation's labor effects, and carries particular weight given Acemoglu's 2024 Nobel Prize. It will likely shape both academic research on technology and inequality, and policy debates about how to govern AI deployment in the workplace.
