# As electric bills rise in the AI boom, states take aim at utilities' profits
**Source**: https://www.latimes.com/world-nation/story/2026-05-17/as-electric-bills-rise-in-ai-boom-states-take-aim-at-utilities-profits
**Date**: May 17, 2026
**Author**: Marc Levy (Associated Press)
**Keywords**: AI energy demand, utility profits, electricity rates, data centers, state regulation, energy policy, affordability, grid modernization, consumer advocacy

## Elevator pitch
The AI boom's voracious energy demands are driving utility profits to record highs while consumer electricity bills soar, prompting bipartisan political backlash in at least six states where governors and regulators are challenging the traditional monopoly utility profit model as fundamentally broken.

## Takeaways
- Utility profits from 110 for-profit utilities rose from $39 billion in 2021 to over $52 billion in 2024, driven by data center expansion
- At least six states — Arizona, Indiana, Maryland, New Jersey, New York, and Pennsylvania — are taking unprecedented action to block rate increases or restructure utility financing
- Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro declared the "20th century utility model is broken" after pressuring PECO to withdraw a 12.5% rate increase
- Consumer advocates estimate about 10% of typical bills represent "excess profit" above what's reasonable
- Utilities warn that squeezing returns threatens grid reliability and could drive investment capital to other states

## Synthesis
The collision between AI's energy appetite and household electricity affordability has ignited a political firestorm across America. AP's Marc Levy documents how the data center construction boom — fueled by AI — has created a two-tier reality: utility companies posting record profits while residential customers face increasingly unaffordable bills.

The numbers tell a stark story. The Energy and Policy Institute found for-profit utility profits jumped from just under $39 billion in 2021 to over $52 billion in 2024 — a period that coincides with the start of the AI data center expansion. Former utility executive turned consumer advocate Mark Ellis estimates about 10% of a typical customer bill represents "excess profit" beyond what Supreme Court precedent would consider reasonable. His proposed solution: force utilities to shop for the lowest-cost investor capital, just as consumers shop for loan rates.

The political response has been unusually bipartisan and aggressive. In Pennsylvania, Governor Josh Shapiro (a Democrat seeking reelection) pressured PECO to withdraw a 12.5% rate increase, then issued an extraordinary letter declaring the "20th century utility model is broken" and that "we can no longer simply prioritize corporate profitability to drive infrastructure development." One Wall Street analyst dubbed it "Quaker State Sticker Shock" as utility parent company stocks lagged. In Indiana, Republican Governor Mike Braun appointed new utility commissioners specifically tasked with blocking rate increases. New Jersey launched what its utility board president called one of the most consequential regulatory reviews in a generation.

The utilities' defense centers on investment necessity. They point out that home electricity bills as a proportion of household income have actually fallen over recent decades, and argue that guaranteed returns are essential to raise capital for grid maintenance, modernization, and reliability. The implicit threat: cut our returns and investors will take their money to other states. Critics dismiss this as fearmongering.

Economist Paul Ferraro of Johns Hopkins offers a nuanced perspective, noting that targeting utility returns is political action rather than economic action — it addresses "deep social disagreements about who should benefit from essential infrastructure" but won't solve the fundamental challenges of modernization, renewable integration, and distributed power. The deeper tension is structural: a regulatory model designed for steady, predictable demand growth is now straining under the explosive, concentrated demands of AI infrastructure. The question of who pays for the AI boom's energy costs — shareholders, ratepayers, or the tech companies themselves — remains unresolved and will likely define utility regulation for years to come.
