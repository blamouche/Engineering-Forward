# How Frontier Firms Are Pulling Ahead — OpenAI B2B Signals
**Source**: https://openai.com/index/introducing-b2b-signals/
**Date**: May 6, 2026
**Author**: OpenAI
**Keywords**: frontier-firms, enterprise-AI, AI-adoption, agentic-workflows, Codex, AI-maturity, tokens-as-proxy

## Elevator pitch
OpenAI's new B2B Signals report shows frontier firms now use 3.5x more intelligence per worker than typical firms — up from 2x a year ago — with the gap driven by depth and agentic workflows, not just more chat messages.

## Takeaways
- Frontier firms (95th percentile of usage) use 3.5x more intelligence per worker; message volume explains only 36% of the gap — richer, more complex interactions account for the rest
- The frontier advantage in agentic tools is dramatic: 16x more Codex messages per worker in frontier firms, with similar patterns in ChatGPT Agent, Apps, Deep Research, and GPTs
- Token generation is used as a proxy for "intelligence demanded" — not measuring business value directly, but tracking how much substantive work employees offload to AI
- Education and learning show the largest task-level frontier advantage, suggesting leading firms use AI to systematically upskill employees
- Multiple adoption paths exist: some industries lead in broad ChatGPT adoption, others in Codex, API intensity, or message intensity — no single leaderboard

## Synthesis
OpenAI's B2B Signals is a strategic product masquerading as research. By publishing aggregated, privacy-preserved metrics on enterprise AI usage patterns, OpenAI simultaneously creates a benchmarking framework that every CIO will want to measure themselves against and establishes the vocabulary ("frontier firms," "intelligence per worker," "depth of use") that will shape enterprise AI discussions for the next year.

The report's core metric — tokens generated per worker — is clever. It's not revenue, not productivity, not cost savings. But it's measurable from OpenAI's side without accessing customer business data, and it correlates with meaningful behavioral differences: workers at frontier firms are providing richer context, asking AI to take on more complex work, and generating more substantive outputs. The contrast drawn — "typical firms use AI to answer questions; frontier firms use it to execute complex work" — sets an aspirational bar.

The agentic workflow data is particularly notable. Codex showing 16x adoption gap between frontier and typical firms validates the thesis that coding agents represent the most advanced deployment pattern available today. But the directional patterns across ChatGPT Agent, Apps, Deep Research, and GPTs suggest this isn't just a developer phenomenon — it's a general shift toward delegation.

The case studies provide concrete texture: Cisco using Codex to reduce build times ~20% and save 1,500+ engineering hours monthly; Travelers Insurance expecting its AI Claim Assistant to handle ~100,000 first notice of loss calls in its first year. These aren't pilots — they're production systems at scale.

The report also serves as a lead-generation funnel — each section directs readers to contact sales or learn more about specific products. But the data is genuinely useful for understanding adoption patterns, and the five recommended practices for moving toward the frontier (measure depth, build production governance, invest in enablement, scale what works, move from chat to delegated work) are actionable beyond OpenAI's ecosystem.
