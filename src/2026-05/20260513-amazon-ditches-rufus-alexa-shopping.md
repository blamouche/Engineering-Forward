# Amazon Ditches Rufus AI Chatbot in Favor of Alexa Shopping Agent
**Source**: https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/13/amazon-ditches-rufus-ai-chatbot-in-favor-of-alexa-shopping-agent.html
**Date**: 2026-05-13
**Author**: Jennifer Pattison Tuohy (The Verge), CNBC staff
**Keywords**: Amazon, Alexa, Rufus, shopping agent, Alexa Plus, e-commerce, AI assistant, agentic commerce, Buy for Me, price alerts, Echo Show

## Elevator pitch
Amazon is retiring its Rufus AI shopping chatbot and replacing it with Alexa for Shopping—an Alexa Plus-powered assistant deeply integrated into the Amazon search bar, website, and Echo devices—capable of price tracking, auto-purchasing, cross-website shopping, and cross-device continuity.

## Takeaways
- Alexa for Shopping replaces Rufus entirely and moves front-and-center into the Amazon search bar, making AI shopping assistance the default rather than an optional feature
- Capabilities include setting price alerts with automatic purchase triggers, tracking a full year of price history, comparing items, auto-reordering, and agentic shopping on external websites via Buy for Me
- Cross-device continuity links Echo smart speakers and Show displays with the website and app—a conversation started on an Echo can inform shopping results on Amazon.com
- The assistant leverages multiple models, pulling from Amazon.com data, web-wide information, and personal customer knowledge to generate "shopping guides" with feature comparisons and AI-generated review summaries
- Amazon is simultaneously suing to keep external AI agents like Perplexity's Comet off its marketplace, revealing the strategic tension between building its own agentic commerce while blocking competitors

## Synthesis

Amazon's decision to kill Rufus and elevate Alexa to the center of its shopping experience is both a product consolidation play and a strategic response to the emerging agentic commerce landscape. Rufus, launched in 2024 as Amazon's experimental AI shopping assistant, was always somewhat buried—accessible through a side panel or specific invocation points. Alexa for Shopping moves AI assistance directly into the search bar, the most fundamental interaction point on Amazon. Every query, from "toilet paper" to "What's a good skincare routine for men," now routes through the AI layer.

The scope of capabilities represents a significant escalation in what a shopping assistant can do. Price alerts with automatic purchase triggers—"Add this sunscreen to my cart if the price drops to $10 and I haven't purchased it in the last 2 months"—turn Alexa from a recommendation engine into an autonomous purchasing agent. The year-long price history tracking addresses one of the most persistent consumer frustrations with online shopping: the opacity of whether a "deal" is actually a deal. Scheduled actions let Alexa proactively hunt for products and deals without user initiation.

The cross-device continuity is the feature that most differentiates Alexa for Shopping from competitors like Google's and OpenAI's shopping bot experiments. An Echo in the kitchen provides a fundamentally different interaction surface than a website, and Amazon is stitching them together. A science project idea discussed with Alexa Plus on a smart speaker becomes context for the shopping experience on Amazon.com. Price alerts set on the website appear on Echo Show displays. This isn't multi-device as a feature checkbox—it's an attempt to make the shopping assistant ambient across the physical and digital spaces where purchase decisions happen.

Daniel Rausch, Amazon's VP of Alexa and Echo, made a pointed critique of competitors: "This type of shopping experience is not a side quest. It's not just scraping a couple of websites and thinking you can pull together some end-to-end shopping journey." The subtext is clear—Google and OpenAI have dabbled in shopping but lack the fulfillment infrastructure, merchant relationships, and logistics depth that make "getting all the way to done" possible. Amazon's bet is that AI shopping isn't about the chatbot interface; it's about the integrated pipeline from intent to doorstep.

The external shopping capability via Buy for Me introduces an interesting tension. Alexa for Shopping can purchase from other websites, theoretically serving the customer's best interest even when it leads off Amazon. But Amazon is simultaneously engaged in legal action to block external AI agents like Perplexity's Comet from operating on its marketplace. The strategy is transparent: build the best agentic shopping experience so customers never need a third-party agent, while legally preventing those agents from accessing Amazon's catalog without permission.

The trust question is the significant risk. Alexa for Shopping's most powerful features—auto-purchasing, personalized recommendations based on purchase history, cross-device context sharing—require customers to hand over substantial personal data and purchase authority. With Pew and Gallup data showing rising AI distrust among Americans, Amazon is asking customers to take a leap of faith at a moment when faith in AI is declining. The company's argument, implicit in Rausch's comments, is that the utility value is high enough to overcome the trust barrier. Whether that calculus holds will determine whether Alexa for Shopping becomes the default commerce interface or another ambitious AI product that customers approach with caution.
