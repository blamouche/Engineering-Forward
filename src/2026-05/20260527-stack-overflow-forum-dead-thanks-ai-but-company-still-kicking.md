# Stack Overflow's forum is dead thanks to AI, but the company's still kicking... thanks to AI
**Source**: https://sherwood.news/tech/stack-overflow-forum-dead-thanks-ai-but-companys-still-kicking-ai/
**Date**: January 7, 2026
**Author**: Claire Yubin Oh
**Keywords**: Stack Overflow, AI, LLM, developer tools, enterprise AI, data licensing, generative AI, Q&A platforms, monetization

## Elevator pitch
Stack Overflow's Q&A forum has collapsed under the weight of AI coding assistants, but the company has ironically found new life — and doubled its revenue to $115M — by selling enterprise AI tools and licensing its archive of developer knowledge to the very AI companies that displaced it.

## Takeaways
- Stack Overflow's question volume has plummeted to ~6,866 per month, roughly matching its 2008 launch levels, killed by ChatGPT, Claude, Cursor, Copilot, and Gemini.
- Despite the forum's decline, annual revenue has roughly doubled to $115 million, and losses narrowed from $84M (FY2023) to $22M.
- The company pivoted from ad-supported forums to enterprise solutions like "Stack Internal," a generative-AI addon now used by 25,000 companies.
- Stack Overflow licenses its massive archive of Q&A data to AI companies, mirroring Reddit's $200M+ data licensing playbook.
- CEO Prashanth Chandrasekar notes that simple questions vanished, but complex questions still get asked on Stack Overflow because LLMs aren't good enough to answer them alone.

## Synthesis
Stack Overflow's story is a parable of creative destruction in the AI age — and a surprising case study in adaptation. When Elon Musk called the platform's decline "death by LLM" in 2023, he captured a real phenomenon: developers no longer need to search through forum threads when AI assistants can generate answers instantly. The numbers are stark. Monthly questions have collapsed from pandemic highs to levels not seen since the site's 2008 launch — just 6,866 in the most recent month tracked.

Yet the company behind the forum is not dying. It has executed one of the more nimble pivots in recent tech history. Stack Overflow's leadership recognized early that while AI was destroying their traffic, it was simultaneously validating the value of their core asset: millions of high-quality, human-curated questions and answers about programming. This archive represents decades of developer knowledge that cannot be replicated from scratch.

The company's strategy unfolded on two fronts. First, enterprise tools: "Stack Internal" provides a generative-AI layer on top of a company's internal knowledge base, powered by the same Q&A expertise that made the public forum valuable. With 25,000 companies now using it, this has become the primary revenue engine. Second, data licensing: Stack Overflow sells access to its content archive to AI companies that need high-quality training data. This mirrors Reddit's successful strategy of monetizing user-generated content through licensing deals — a model that generated Reddit over $200 million in 2024.

The financial turnaround is notable. Annual revenue doubled to $115 million even as engagement cratered. Losses narrowed dramatically through aggressive cost-cutting, including mass layoffs in late 2023 that reduced headcount by 28%. The company has traded ad-driven growth for a leaner, enterprise-focused model.

CEO Prashanth Chandrasekar articulates the nuanced reality behind the headlines. Simple programming questions — the bulk of forum traffic — have indeed migrated to AI chatbots. But complex, nuanced technical problems still bring developers to Stack Overflow because LLMs, while impressive, are "only as good as the data, which is typically human curated." The platform remains one of the best sources of that data.

This creates an ironic feedback loop: AI companies need Stack Overflow's data to improve their models, but those improved models further reduce the need for the public forum. The company is effectively monetizing its own disruption. The article frames Stack Overflow as a "canary in tech's new, circular coal mine" — a platform that both suffers from AI and feeds it, surviving through an uncomfortable symbiosis.

The broader implication is significant for any knowledge platform built on user-generated content. When AI can synthesize that content on demand, the original platform loses its direct utility. But the underlying data retains value — if the platform can negotiate licensing deals before its community and content atrophy completely. Stack Overflow's survival suggests that the companies that built the internet's knowledge repositories may have a second act as data providers to the AI systems replacing them.
