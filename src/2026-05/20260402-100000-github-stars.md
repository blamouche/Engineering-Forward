# 100,000 GitHub Stars
**Source**: https://supabase.com/blog/100000-github-stars
**Date**: 2026-04-02
**Author**: Paul Copplestone (Copple)
**Keywords**: Supabase, GitHub stars, open-source, community, Postgres, milestone, PostgREST, pgvector, Deno

## Elevator pitch
Supabase celebrates reaching 100,000 GitHub stars with eight million developers, reflecting on the open-source community and tools that made it possible while reaffirming a commitment to Postgres as the default database for the internet.

## Takeaways
- Supabase reached 100,000 GitHub stars with eight million developers, a milestone the founders describe as "surreal" given they started with just two people and a Postgres database in 2020.
- The company attributes growth to optimizing for developer experience rather than stars, treating the star count as a side effect of building something developers enjoy using.
- Core product principles remain unchanged: Supabase supports existing open-source tools and communities, describing itself as a "community of communities."
- Key open-source dependencies acknowledged: Postgres, PostgREST (billions of API requests daily), pgvector (default embedding storage), Deno (Edge Functions), imgproxy (image transformations), and Elixir Phoenix (1.5B realtime messages daily).
- Future ambitions include helping Postgres scale to petabytes (via Multigres) and adding pluggable storage (via OrioleDB).

## Synthesis

Paul Copplestone's reflection on Supabase reaching 100,000 GitHub stars is a short but genuine celebration post that reveals a lot about the company's philosophy. The milestone is treated not as a vanity metric but as evidence of community health — "a community that showed up, stuck around, and built things we never anticipated."

**The "community of communities" approach** is the central insight. Supabase didn't build its stack from scratch. It assembled and integrated existing open-source projects: Postgres for the database, PostgREST for the API layer, pgvector for embeddings, Deno for edge functions, imgproxy for image processing, and Elixir Phoenix for realtime. Rather than absorbing these projects, Supabase employs maintainers, sponsors projects, invests in businesses, and develops complementary tools. This is a different model from the typical "platform eats open-source" pattern — it's more like "platform fertilises open-source."

**The timeline is striking.** 2020 to 2026, two founders to eight million developers, zero to 100,000 stars. This trajectory places Supabase among the fastest-growing open-source projects, competing in mindshare with Firebase (Google's proprietary alternative) while maintaining its open-source identity. The fact that the product principles haven't changed since inception suggests the founding vision was well-calibrated from the start.

**Postgres advocacy is explicit.** The post declares an ambition for Postgres to become "the default database for every new project on the internet" — a bold claim in a world where MongoDB, PlanetScale, and various NoSQL solutions have significant mindshare. The mention of Multigres (petabyte scale) and OrioleDB (pluggable storage) signals that Supabase is thinking about the long-term technical challenges that would prevent Postgres from being that default. Petabyte-scale Postgres and pluggable storage engines would address two of the most common reasons teams choose alternative databases.

**What's not said is also notable.** There's no mention of competitors, no revenue figures, no growth metrics beyond stars and developers. The post is purely celebratory and community-focused. This restraint is consistent with Supabase's brand — they tend to let the product and community speak rather than engaging in competitive marketing. For a company that's increasingly positioned as the open-source alternative to Firebase (and by extension, a key piece of the anti-vendor-lock-in movement), this tone reinforces trust.

The brief post serves as a timestamp in Supabase's history — a moment to look back before continuing forward. The closing line ("We are not done") and the forward-looking mentions of Multigres and OrioleDB make clear that 100,000 stars is treated as a waypoint, not a destination.
