# The Dawn of Codex-native Apps
**Source**: https://every.to/context-window/the-dawn-of-codex-native-apps
**Date**: May 5, 2026
**Author**: Katie Parrott
**Keywords**: Codex, agent delegation, collaboration, AI-native apps, allocation economy, inbox zero, compound engineering, agentic automation

## Elevator pitch
Working with AI is bifurcating into two modes — delegation and collaboration — and the meta-skill for knowledge workers is now knowing which tasks to hand off entirely and which to stay close to, pairing with the model in real time.

## Takeaways
- The allocation economy thesis was only half right: some work wants full delegation to agents, while other work demands close collaboration with the AI
- The new meta-skill is the AI version of the serenity prayer: knowing when to delegate, when to collaborate, and having the wisdom to tell the difference
- Codex-native apps are emerging where the agent lives in the same workspace as the human, with the inbox/document split as a pattern for visible, reviewable agent work
- Dan Shipper's inbox-zero workflow uses Codex + Cora + a one-page operating manual: the agent drafts in the background while the human reviews decisions in a shared document
- Jobs like Airtable's "AI Agent Architect, Customer Experience" signal that designing the boundary between agent autonomy and human oversight is becoming a formal role

## Synthesis
Every's latest analysis captures a shift that's becoming visible across the AI tooling landscape: work with AI is splitting into two distinct modes. The first is delegation — handing tasks off to an agent that works independently, whether it's filing bug reports or processing email. The second is collaboration — staying close to the model, pairing on every move in the same window, as with brainstorming, drafting, or complex decision-making. The earlier "allocation economy" thesis, which framed the future of knowledge work as primarily about deciding what to delegate, was only half right. The other half is about the work where you need to stay tethered.

The article offers a concrete example through Dan Shipper's inbox-zero workflow. He uses Codex (OpenAI's agent platform) alongside Cora, an AI email assistant, with a one-page operating manual that specifies VIPs, auto-archive rules, scheduling preferences, and reply style. The agent sweeps the inbox, archives what the manual says to archive, and drafts responses into a shared document. Dan reviews inline — "Spam," "archive," "reply just to Willie" — and the agent executes. The key insight is the pattern: the agent works in the background while the human stays in a document that makes every decision visible and reversible.

This bifurcation also creates new job categories. Airtable's "AI Agent Architect, Customer Experience" role is about designing exactly these boundaries: what knowledge an agent retrieves, which APIs it can use, when it can modify accounts, how failures get measured, and where it hands work back to a person. The article also adapts Musk's five rules of automation for the agentic era, emphasizing that you should delete unnecessary steps, simplify, optimize, and accelerate before you even think about handing a process to an agent — a reminder that throwing agents at broken processes just produces broken automation.
