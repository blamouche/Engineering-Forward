# Researchers Asked LLMs for Strategic Advice. They Got "Trendslop" in Return.
**Source**: https://hbr.org/2026/03/researchers-asked-llms-for-strategic-advice-they-got-trendslop-in-return
**Date**: March 16, 2026
**Author**: Angelo Romasanta, Llewellyn D.W. Thomas, Natalia Levina
**Keywords**: LLMs, strategic advice, trendslop, AI hallucinations, executive decision-making, generative AI, strategy formulation

## Elevator pitch
When researchers tested LLMs as strategic advisors for executives, they found the AI-generated recommendations were "trendslop" — superficially polished but substantively hollow, recycling fashionable buzzwords without the analytical depth needed for real boardroom decisions.

## Takeaways
- LLMs produce strategic advice that sounds authoritative but often lacks genuine analytical rigor and context-specific insight
- The term "trendslop" captures AI-generated content that dresses up conventional wisdom in jargon without adding novel strategic value
- Executives face a growing risk of over-relying on AI-generated recommendations that are confidently wrong or shallow
- The research exposes a gap between the surface fluency of LLMs and the deep, contextual reasoning required for strategy
- Organizations need frameworks for evaluating and stress-testing AI-generated strategic advice before acting on it

## Synthesis
As large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT become increasingly embedded in executive workflows, a critical question emerges: how trustworthy is their strategic advice? Researchers Angelo Romasanta (Esade Business School), Llewellyn D.W. Thomas (University of Sydney), and Natalia Levina (NYU Stern) set out to answer this, and what they found is deeply concerning for leaders who treat LLMs as silent boardroom partners.

The central finding is the concept of "trendslop" — a term the researchers coin to describe the phenomenon where LLMs generate strategic recommendations that are superficially polished, jargon-laden, and seemingly credible, but upon closer examination are substantively hollow. The models excel at producing the *form* of strategic advice — clear arguments, structured reasoning, and confident delivery — but falter on the *substance*. They recycle fashionable management buzzwords, echo conventional wisdom, and fail to deliver the kind of novel, context-specific insight that distinguishes great strategy from mediocre consulting.

This matters because strategy is fundamentally about making choices under uncertainty with incomplete information — exactly the kind of task where context, judgment, and deep domain expertise are paramount. An LLM trained on the internet's collective output may produce advice that sounds like good strategy, but it cannot engage in the kind of first-principles reasoning or counterfactual thinking that distinguishes genuinely innovative strategic moves. The risk is not just that the advice is wrong — it's that it is *confidently* wrong in ways that are difficult to detect without careful scrutiny.

The implications extend beyond individual decisions. As organizations integrate LLMs into strategic planning processes, there is a danger of homogenization — if everyone asks the same AI for advice, everyone gets the same "trendslop" back. This could flatten competitive differentiation and lead to herd behavior at scale. The researchers argue that leaders need structured evaluation frameworks to stress-test AI-generated strategic recommendations, treating them as inputs to human deliberation rather than ready-to-implement answers.

The article also touches on a broader concern about the democratization of advice: LLMs make strategic consulting available to anyone, but availability without quality control is dangerous. The polish of AI prose creates an authority bias that makes it harder for decision-makers to question the underlying reasoning — or lack thereof. The researchers ultimately advocate for a hybrid approach where AI augments human strategic thinking rather than replacing it, with human judgment remaining the ultimate filter for what gets acted upon.
