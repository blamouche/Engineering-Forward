# This 11-Person London Startup Wants to Make AI 100x Cheaper Every Year
**Source**: https://sifted.eu/articles/meryem-arik-doubleword-interview
**Date**: 2026-05-15
**Author**: Maya Dharampal-Hornby (Sifted)
**Keywords**: Doubleword, Meryem Arik, AI inference, cost reduction, GPU optimization, sovereign compute, London startup, MIT, NVIDIA

## Elevator pitch
Doubleword, an 11-person London startup founded by former NVIDIA and TitanML engineer Meryem Arik, is building AI inference infrastructure that claims to be 100x cheaper than competitors — and has already secured £2M in funding and a deployment on France's national supercomputer.

## Takeaways
- Doubleword's pricing claims to be 100x cheaper than competitors like Together AI and Fireworks, with inference at $0.07 per million tokens versus competitors at $7–$14
- The company is already deployed on France's Jean Zay supercomputer through a partnership with GENCI, France's national computing agency
- Arik argues that current AI inference pricing is artificially inflated by GPU scarcity and vendor lock-in, not by underlying cost structures
- The startup targets "sovereign compute" — enabling nations and enterprises to run AI workloads on their own infrastructure rather than depending on US hyperscalers
- Arik describes the current AI market as "a bubble where someone's paying for it right now" and positions Doubleword for the inevitable pricing correction

## Synthesis
Meryem Arik's journey from NVIDIA and TitanML to founding Doubleword is a study in conviction. After watching AI inference costs remain stubbornly high despite advances in hardware efficiency, she became convinced that the market was being artificially propped up by GPU scarcity and vendor lock-in rather than genuine cost structures. Her response: build an inference engine that strips out the margin and passes the savings on.

The numbers Doubleword claims are eye-catching. Inference at $0.07 per million tokens — compared to $7–$14 from competitors like Together AI and Fireworks — represents the kind of order-of-magnitude price drop that could reshape the economics of AI deployment. If these numbers hold at scale, the implications for AI-native startups that currently spend 50–80% of their runway on inference are profound.

Arik's background gives the claims credibility. Her time at NVIDIA provided deep understanding of GPU architectures, while TitanML (an inference optimization startup acquired earlier) gave her hands-on experience with the commercial side of the inference market. She describes the process as one of systematic optimization: identifying every point of inefficiency in the inference pipeline and engineering it away.

The sovereign compute angle is perhaps the most strategically interesting part of Doubleword's positioning. By deploying on France's Jean Zay supercomputer through GENCI, Doubleword is demonstrating that its technology works on national-scale infrastructure — not just in a cloud sandbox. This opens a market that the major hyperscalers have largely ignored: governments and enterprises that want AI capabilities but are unwilling or unable to route their data through US-based cloud providers.

Arik is refreshingly blunt about the market dynamics. She calls the current AI pricing environment "a bubble where someone's paying for it right now" and argues that the correction is inevitable. When GPU supply catches up with demand — as it inevitably will, given the scale of investment flowing into chip fabrication — inference will become a commodity, and companies that have built their margins on scarcity pricing will face an existential reckoning.

The team is small (11 people) and the funding is modest (£2M), but Arik argues this is by design. "If I wanted to build a big team I wouldn't be in London," she says. The constraint forces discipline, and discipline produces efficiency — the same principle that drives the product itself. Whether Doubleword can maintain its pricing advantage as larger players inevitably respond is the question that will define the company's next chapter.
