# What Comes After LinkedIn
**Source**: https://every.to/p/what-comes-after-linkedin
**Date**: 2026-03-16
**Author**: Eleanor Warnock
**Keywords**: portfolios, LinkedIn, personal branding, AI job displacement, skills-based hiring, knowledge work, career development, professional identity

## Elevator pitch
Eleanor Warnock argues that as AI automates rote knowledge work, professionals must prove their judgment through portfolios of work — not resumes or LinkedIn posts — to demonstrate the soft skills that matter most in an AI-augmented workplace.

## Takeaways
- Brand-name credentials (McKinsey, Goldman, Google) are losing signaling power because they don't reveal whether someone was a decision-maker or a "pixel pusher"
- LinkedIn's 1+ billion users share very little content, and much of what is shared is AI-generated drivel that readers gloss over — the platform's format and algorithm make it a poor venue for demonstrating real expertise
- Portfolios — collections of artifacts showing how you think — should become the standard for knowledge workers, similar to how designers and artists have always showcased their work
- Personal branding advice has been around for years but rarely works well because LinkedIn rewards a specific kind of extroversion that many find uncomfortable
- Skills-based hiring is rising as employers shift from screening by credentials to evaluating what candidates can actually do

## Synthesis
Eleanor Warnock's essay addresses a structural problem in professional identity that AI is forcing to a breaking point. For decades, the knowledge worker's career was legible through a series of brand-name signals: a degree from the right school, a stint at McKinsey or Goldman Sachs, a role at Google. These credentials served as heuristic shortcuts for hiring managers — if someone selective chose this person, they must be capable. AI unravels this system from both ends. On one side, it's automating the rote click-work that entry-level and mid-career knowledge workers performed, making the "what" of a job less differentiating. On the other, it makes the invisible skills — judgment, taste, expertise — more valuable precisely because they're harder to automate, but also harder to prove on a traditional resume.

LinkedIn, Warnock argues, is not the solution. Despite over a billion registered users, only a tiny fraction share content, and an increasing proportion of what is shared is obviously AI-written. The platform's technical limitations (no text formatting in posts, an algorithm that surfaces stale content) compound a deeper cultural problem: LinkedIn rewards a specific kind of extroversion — the hot take, the humble-brag, the self-congratulatory announcement — that many people find uncomfortable or inauthentic. Warnock draws on her own reporting about wealth manager Jan Voss, who moved his important thinking to a newsletter because he wanted a place where "posts have staying power."

The alternative Warnock proposes is the portfolio: a body of work that proves value beyond work history. This isn't the "portfolio career" of multiple income streams, but rather a curated collection of artifacts that demonstrate how someone thinks — the same way creative professionals have always showcased design or artistic work. For knowledge workers, this could mean writing on platforms, building interactive tools on personal websites, or creating resources that let people experience expertise without meeting the person. AI, paradoxically, can make this process more accessible by helping with the production work while the human contributes the judgment, taste, and original thinking that constitutes the portfolio's value.

The piece connects to broader trends in hiring, particularly the skills-based hiring movement where employers screen candidates by demonstrated capability rather than institutional pedigree. A thin resume from a prestigious institution is increasingly less valuable than a demonstrable body of work. The implication is clear: in an AI world, what you can show matters more than where you've been.
