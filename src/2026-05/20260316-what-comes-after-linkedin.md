# What Comes After LinkedIn
**Source**: https://every.to/p/what-comes-after-linkedin
**Date**: 2026-03-16 (updated 2026-04-07)
**Author**: Eleanor Warnock
**Keywords**: professional identity, portfolios, LinkedIn, AI and employment, knowledge work, personal branding, career signaling

## Elevator pitch
In an AI world where brand-name credentials lose their signaling power, knowledge workers must prove their judgment through portfolios — curated bodies of work that demonstrate how they think, not just where they've worked.

## Takeaways
- Brand names (McKinsey, Goldman Sachs, Google) are losing their power as heuristics for expertise — in an AI-augmented workplace, what you did at a prestigious firm matters more than the name itself
- Portfolios — collections of artifacts showing how you think, not just your work history — are emerging as the replacement for the traditional resume
- LinkedIn has over 1 billion users but only a tiny fraction share content, and what is shared is increasingly AI-generated and indistinguishable
- Personal branding advice has been around for years but almost nobody does it well — LinkedIn rewards a specific kind of extroversion that many knowledge workers find uncomfortable
- AI enables the portfolio model: interactive tools, writing, and demonstrations of expertise that let people experience your capabilities without meeting you

## Synthesis
Eleanor Warnock's essay identifies a structural shift in how knowledge workers signal competence. For decades, employer brand names served as reliable proxies: if you worked at McKinsey, you could probably build models and slides, and someone selective had chosen you. But in an AI world where junior analytical work is increasingly automated, the "McKinsey" signal doesn't distinguish between a strategic decision-maker and someone who spent two years formatting PowerPoint. The credential loses its information content.

Portfolios — curated bodies of work that demonstrate thinking, not tenure — are the proposed replacement. Warnock draws on her own practice: she writes a newsletter about people using writing to build things, and posts on LinkedIn, all of which constitutes a portfolio that helps people evaluate her beyond her previous employers. The model mirrors creative industries (designers, artists, writers) where the portfolio has always been the primary credential.

The critique of LinkedIn is pointed but accurate: with a billion users and a content feed increasingly filled with AI-generated thought leadership, the platform has become a poor signal of actual expertise. The algorithm surfaces stale content, the format limits expression, and the culture rewards a performative extroversion that many knowledge workers find inauthentic. Warnock's argument is that AI both creates the problem (making credentials less reliable) and enables the solution (making it easier to build interactive portfolios that demonstrate real capability). The essay is a call to action for knowledge workers to invest in their own intellectual artifact collection before the old signaling system fully breaks down.
