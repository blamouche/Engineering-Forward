# Musk Slams Altman Trial Verdict as a 'Technicality,' Vows to Appeal
**Source**: https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/18/musk-altman-openai-trial-verdict.html
**Date**: May 18, 2026
**Author**: Jeffrey Kopp, Lora Kolodny
**Keywords**: Elon Musk, Sam Altman, OpenAI, lawsuit, nonprofit, breach of charitable trust, statute of limitations, Microsoft, xAI, IPO

## Elevator pitch
A federal jury ruled that Elon Musk waited too long to sue OpenAI and Sam Altman over claims they betrayed the company's nonprofit mission, dismissing the case on statute of limitations grounds — a verdict Musk called a "calendar technicality" and immediately vowed to appeal.

## Takeaways
- Jury deliberated less than two hours before finding Musk's claims fell outside California's three-year statute of limitations
- Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers immediately adopted the advisory verdict, dismissing claims against both OpenAI and Microsoft
- Musk had sought up to $180 billion in "ill-gotten gains," removal of Altman and Brockman, and unwinding of OpenAI's 2025 for-profit restructuring
- OpenAI's lawyer called it "a substantive one" not a technicality, arguing Musk sat on claims to use them as a competitive weapon
- The verdict clears a legal cloud for both companies as they race toward blockbuster IPOs (OpenAI at $850B+ valuation, SpaceX at $1.25T)

## Synthesis
The three-week trial between Elon Musk and Sam Altman concluded with a swift jury verdict — less than two hours of deliberation — dismissing Musk's lawsuit against OpenAI on procedural grounds. The jury found that Musk's claims of "breach of charitable trust" fell outside California's three-year statute of limitations, and District Court Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers immediately adopted the advisory verdict.

The core of Musk's case was that Altman and OpenAI president Greg Brockman "stole a charity" by abandoning OpenAI's founding nonprofit mission in pursuit of personal profit. Musk testified he gave roughly $38 million to OpenAI based on the understanding it would develop AI "for the benefit of humanity." His legal team sought extraordinary remedies: forcing OpenAI and Microsoft to disgorge up to $180 billion in "ill-gotten gains," removing Altman and Brockman from leadership, and unwinding the company's 2025 restructuring that enabled its for-profit arm to flourish.

OpenAI's defense was twofold. Procedurally, they argued Musk waited too long — the alleged breaches occurred years before he filed suit in 2024. Substantively, they portrayed the lawsuit as competitive sabotage: Musk had himself floated a for-profit structure (on the condition he retain control), and started his own competing AI lab, xAI (now part of SpaceX), in 2023. OpenAI's lawyers framed the case as an attempt to "kneecap a rival after he failed to gain control of it."

The verdict has immediate financial implications. Both companies are racing toward what are expected to be record-setting IPOs. OpenAI raised $122 billion at over $850 billion valuation in March. SpaceX, which merged with xAI in February, confidentially filed for an IPO in April at a $1.25 trillion valuation. A Musk victory could have thrown OpenAI's corporate structure into chaos; the dismissal clears a significant legal overhang.

Musk's vow to appeal to the 9th Circuit keeps the case alive, but the judge's skepticism — she noted she was prepared to dismiss "on the spot" — suggests a challenging path forward. The deeper significance lies in what the trial revealed about the tension between AI's nonprofit origins and the commercial pressures of building frontier models. As OpenAI's lawyer put it: "You brought your claims too late, and you did it because you were sitting on them to use them as a weapon of a competitor who can't compete in the marketplace." The jury agreed.
