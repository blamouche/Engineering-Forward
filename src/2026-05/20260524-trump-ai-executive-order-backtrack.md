# David Sacks's 11th-Hour Plea Led to Trump's Backtrack on AI Executive Order
**Source**: https://www.wsj.com/politics/policy/david-sacks-trump-ai-executive-order-6019242d
**Date**: May 24, 2026
**Author**: Amrith Ramkumar, Natalie Andrews, Meghan Bobrowsky
**Keywords**: AI regulation, Trump, executive order, David Sacks, AI safety, China competition, AI policy, doomers

## Elevator pitch
Venture capitalist and Trump adviser David Sacks successfully lobbied President Trump at the last minute to backtrack on an AI executive order, arguing that voluntary government testing requirements could evolve into mandatory regulations that handicap US companies against Chinese competitors.

## Takeaways
- Trump was poised to sign a long-awaited executive order establishing a voluntary process for government testing of advanced AI models for safety risks.
- David Sacks intervened in an 11th-hour call, warning that voluntary testing frameworks inevitably become mandatory regulations.
- Sacks framed the order as a victory for "AI doomers" who advocate strong safety guardrails, arguing it would slow US AI progress in the race with China.
- The backtrack represents a win for business-minded officials who prioritize speed and competitiveness over precautionary safety measures.
- The episode illustrates ongoing tension within the administration between national security-driven AI caution and economic competitiveness imperatives.

## Synthesis
The Wall Street Journal reports that a long-anticipated executive order on AI safety was derailed at the last moment by David Sacks, the venture capitalist serving as a key Trump adviser on technology and AI policy. The order would have established a voluntary framework for government testing of advanced AI models to assess potential dangers before deployment.

Sacks's intervention came in an eleventh-hour phone call with President Trump, where he argued that such voluntary processes have a track record of evolving into mandatory regulations over time. His core argument connected AI safety regulation to the broader US-China technology competition: slowing down American AI companies with testing requirements, even voluntary ones, would cede ground to Chinese competitors operating without similar constraints.

The framing of the debate as "doomers versus accelerators" is significant. Sacks explicitly characterized the executive order as a win for the "AI doomer" faction — those who advocate for strong precautionary guardrails to manage existential or catastrophic risks from advanced AI. By contrast, Sacks and his allies represent a business-first perspective that prioritizes speed of deployment and economic competitiveness.

The episode reveals a persistent fault line in AI governance: the tension between precautionary safety measures and the geopolitical imperative to maintain technological leadership. The outcome — Trump backtracking on the order — suggests that competitiveness arguments currently hold more sway in the administration than safety-first approaches, at least when articulated by well-connected figures like Sacks.

The broader context includes ongoing debates about Anthropic and other AI companies' calls for regulation, which Sacks has publicly criticized. This specific intervention demonstrates how individual advisers with industry ties can shape national AI policy at critical moments, and how the US-China rivalry has become the dominant frame through which AI governance decisions are evaluated.
