# AI Was Supposed to Free My Time. It Consumed It.
**Source**: https://every.to/working-overtime/ai-was-supposed-to-free-my-time-it-consumed-it
**Date**: March 9, 2026
**Author**: Katie Parrott
**Keywords**: AI work intensification, variable-ratio reinforcement, FOBO, digital boundaries, epistemic rabbit holes, AI compulsion, burnout, OpenClaw

## Elevator pitch
New research reveals AI doesn't reduce work — it amplifies it through task expansion, blurred boundaries, and psychological hooks that function like slot machines, keeping knowledge workers compulsively prompting long past the point of productivity.

## Takeaways
- UC Berkeley researchers identified three ways AI intensifies work: task expansion (people absorb others' responsibilities), blurred boundaries (prompting during lunch/evenings feels like chatting), and multitasking (parallel AI threads create cognitive overload)
- AI's variable-quality outputs create a "slot machine effect" — unpredictable rewards trigger dopamine in patterns that mirror casino gambling, making it hard to stop prompting
- The "AI genie phenomenon" describes the irrational feeling that stopping means leaving wishes unfulfilled; "epistemic rabbit holes" trap users in cycles where each answer opens new questions
- 54% of employees fear falling behind in AI use; 85% learn on their own time; this Fear of Becoming Obsolete (FOBO) and dopamine form a self-reinforcing cycle
- The author spent 12 hours with her OpenClaw agent Margot on what was supposed to be a quick afternoon setup — a common experience among AI users
- Countermeasures proposed include "AI practice" with intentional pauses, batched notifications, protected focus windows, and analog activities with natural stopping points

## Synthesis
Katie Parrott's essay is a raw, research-backed account of how AI tools create the opposite of their promise: instead of freeing time, they consume it. Drawing on her own 12-hour marathon with her OpenClaw agent Margot — which was supposed to be a "few hours, tops" — she traces the psychological mechanisms that make AI use compulsive.

The core research comes from UC Berkeley's Aruna Ranganathan and Xingqi Maggie Ye, who spent eight months observing AI adoption at a 200-person tech company. They documented three intensification patterns: task expansion (product managers writing code, researchers doing engineering), blurred boundaries (prompting during lunch because it "feels like chatting"), and multitasking-driven cognitive overload from juggling parallel AI threads. These aren't about obligation — unlike the old "checking work email at 10 p.m." dynamic, AI pulls people back because it's genuinely enjoyable.

The behavioral science layer is even more troubling. UBC researchers identified the "AI genie phenomenon" and "epistemic rabbit holes" — cycles where partially satisfying AI responses trigger variable-ratio reinforcement, the same mechanism that powers slot machine addiction. Each prompt might be the one that cracks the problem, so stopping feels irrational. The unpredictable quality of AI outputs directly triggers dopamine in casino-like patterns.

Parrott pairs this with the social anxiety dimension: EY research showing 54% of workers fear falling behind peers in AI use, 85% learning on their own time. FOBO (Fear of Becoming Obsolete) and dopamine form a self-reinforcing cycle: fear drives you to the laptop, tinkering produces a win, the win expands what you feel capable of, which widens the gap to where you think you need to be.

Her proposed solutions are modest and personal: intentional pauses, analog activities with natural stopping points (board games, dog walks), and talking about it with others. But she's clear-eyed that organizations may not help — when workers voluntarily produce more, it looks like a win from the top floor. The essay ends with three words: "You're doing enough."
