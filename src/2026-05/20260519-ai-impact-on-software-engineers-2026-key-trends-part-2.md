# AI's impact on software engineers in 2026: key trends, Part 2
**Source**: https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/ai-impact-on-software-engineers-part-2
**Date**: May 19, 2026
**Author**: Gergely Orosz and Elin Nilsson
**Keywords**: AI tooling, software engineering, codebase quality, AI adoption, developer productivity, AI survey 2026

## Elevator pitch
Analysis of 900+ survey responses reveals that while AI tools reduce tedious work and expand capabilities, they also introduce codebase quality degradation, unrealistic business expectations, and an emerging maintenance crisis where fewer engineers understand increasingly complex codebases.

## Takeaways
- AI tools reduce time on repetitive work but create unrealistic business expectations and codebase quality issues, with management often prioritizing output over quality.
- Adopting AI at company scale is hard: benefits depend on pre-existing engineering culture (guardrails, documentation, quality codebases); AI amplifies existing practices rather than fixing broken ones.
- Codebase quality is decreasing due to AI-generated slop, insufficient code reviews, and more bugs — while maintenance burden falls on a shrinking pool of engineers who understand the code.
- Less experienced engineers benefit less from AI and rack up higher token bills; they need more mentorship and space to learn, not just AI tools.
- AI tool usage feels addictive — the "slot machine" effect and pricing models encourage constant prompting despite diminishing returns.
- Code ownership is eroding and team collaboration is becoming less important as engineers can self-unblock, leading to tooling chaos and workflow fragmentation.
- Some companies are rolling back AI tools to deal with quality deterioration and costly production incidents.

## Synthesis
Gergely Orosz and Elin Nilsson complete their three-part analysis of the 2026 Pragmatic Engineer AI survey with more than 900 responses, focusing on the tradeoffs, organizational challenges, and systemic effects of AI adoption in software engineering.

The survey reveals a nuanced picture of AI tooling tradeoffs. On the positive side, engineers spend less time on tedious, repetitive work, can maintain flow state by self-unblocking, and AI expands the pool of people who can perform high-value work — a finding Microsoft's recent research corroborates. However, the negatives are substantial: unrealistic business expectations about productivity gains, decreasing codebase quality due to slop generation, and a maintenance crisis where fewer engineers understand increasingly complex codebases. Some respondents report the opposite of flow — constant context switching as AI enables starting too many tasks in parallel.

Organizational adoption at scale proves particularly difficult. The survey's key insight is that AI is an amplifier, not a fixer: teams with strong engineering culture, guardrails, documentation, and quality codebases extract more benefits. Teams lacking these foundations see their problems amplified instead. The individual nature of AI workflows creates further challenges — what makes one developer 10x productive doesn't work for another, leading to tooling chaos when each engineer uses different tools with no coherence. Some large companies have already rolled back AI tools to address quality deterioration and costly production incidents.

The impact on codebase quality is perhaps the most concerning pattern. "AI slop" — duplicated, verbose code with poor abstractions — is flooding codebases. Code reviews are becoming superficial because there are too many to review properly. Management often focuses on tracking AI usage rather than output quality, seeing productivity gains as a baseline rather than a bonus. "Drive-by" contributions from engineers unfamiliar with the codebase compound the problem, while the maintenance burden concentrates on the few engineers who still understand the system.

The human dimension includes AI tool "addiction" — the slot machine effect of "just one more prompt" — and pricing models designed to encourage more prompting. Less experienced engineers benefit less and cost more in tokens, suggesting they need mentorship and learning space that AI tools alone cannot provide. The survey also notes that while fewer developers are negative about AI compared to 2024, there hasn't been a proportional increase in positivity — despite substantially better models and tooling. The concept of code ownership is eroding, and team collaboration becomes less necessary as individuals can self-unblock, raising questions about the long-term health of engineering organizations.
