# Notes on Pope Leo XIV's Encyclical on AI
**Source**: https://simonwillison.net/2026/May/25/encyclical-on-ai/
**Date**: 2026-05-25
**Author**: Simon Willison
**Keywords**: AI ethics, Pope Leo XIV, Magnifica Humanitas, Vatican, LLM interpretability, AI regulation, data commons, Catholic social teaching

## Elevator pitch
Simon Willison annotates Pope Leo XIV's encyclical *Magnifica Humanitas*, highlighting its remarkably clear and accessible treatment of AI ethics — covering LLM interpretability, cultural bias, environmental impact, algorithmic accountability, and data as a common good — and notes his own accidental prediction that the Pope would weigh in on AI.

## Takeaways
- The encyclical frames AI as "cultivated" rather than "built" — models grow within frameworks rather than being fully designed, making their internal processes fundamentally opaque.
- It warns that AI's apparent objectivity masks the cultural assumptions of those who built it, and its simulated empathy can create dangerous illusions of genuine human relationship.
- Environmental costs of AI are explicitly called out: enormous energy/water consumption and infrastructure demands that require sustainable solutions.
- The document argues data should be treated as a common or shared good, not a private commodity — a radical framing within Catholic social teaching.
- Clear human accountability must be traceable through every stage of AI deployment, from design to concrete decisions affecting people's lives.

## Synthesis
Simon Willison's post annotates *Magnifica Humanitas*, the first papal encyclical dedicated to artificial intelligence, issued by Pope Leo XIV on May 15, 2026. Willison, a prominent voice in the AI developer community, approaches the document not as a Catholic insider but as someone deeply engaged with the practical and ethical dimensions of LLM deployment. His framing is personal and conversational — he listened to the encyclical on a walk using the ElevenReader app — which makes the theological document feel accessible to a secular tech audience.

The encyclical's core argument is that AI must serve human dignity, not undermine it. Willison surfaces several passages that resonate with current AI discourse. The description of LLMs as "cultivated" rather than "built" captures the interpretability problem that researchers grapple with: we create training frameworks, but the resulting model internals remain largely unknowable. The document connects this opacity to accountability failures — when decisions affecting employment, credit, or reputation are delegated to systems whose processes cannot be traced, justice becomes impossible.

Willison highlights the encyclical's treatment of three AI risks in personal use: excessive reliance weakening judgment, apparent objectivity hiding cultural bias, and simulated empathy creating illusions of relationship. These map directly to concerns about AI companions, sycophancy in LLM outputs, and the outsourcing of critical thinking. The environmental dimension — enormous energy and water demands — receives surprising attention from a religious document, aligning with Pope Francis's earlier ecological encyclical *Laudato Si'*.

Perhaps the most politically provocative section concerns data ownership. The encyclical explicitly rejects treating data as private property, arguing it should be managed as a shared good. Willison connects this to broader debates about who benefits from AI's data flywheel, noting the document's concern that AI amplifies the power of those who already possess economic resources.

Willison closes with a self-deprecating callback to his January 2026 predictions appearance on Oxide and Friends, where he jokingly suggested the Pope might weigh in on AI economic impacts. The prediction turned out to be uncannily timely — though as Willison notes, Pope Leo XIV's choice of name (honoring Leo XIII, who addressed the first industrial revolution) made this intervention more historically inevitable than prophetic.
