# What is OpenAI, Really?
**Source**: https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/what-is-openai
**Date**: 2023-11-23
**Author**: Gergely Orosz
**Keywords**: OpenAI, Sam Altman, board governance, nonprofit, for-profit, Microsoft, corporate structure, AI safety, ChatGPT

## Elevator pitch
The November 2023 firing-and-return of Sam Altman exposed a fundamental governance tension at OpenAI between its nonprofit mission and for-profit commercial ambitions, nearly destroying the company in five days.

## Takeaways
- OpenAI's board of six (3 employees, 3 outsiders) fired Sam Altman in a coup led by chief scientist Ilya Sutskever over safety and speed concerns
- 743 of 778 employees signed a petition threatening to join Microsoft unless the board resigned and Altman returned
- The crisis revealed OpenAI's exotic corporate structure: a nonprofit board controls a capped for-profit entity
- Microsoft, which invested $10B and was not notified in advance, ultimately gained leverage with Altman briefly joining Microsoft
- The core tension between nonprofit AI safety mission and for-profit commercialization remains unresolved

## Synthesis
This article was originally published as a subscriber-only deepdive on November 23, 2023, and was later un-paywalled by request so the wider tech community could understand the dramatic events. Gergely Orosz provides a detailed timeline and analysis of what he calls "the most insane 100 hours" in modern tech corporate history.

The crisis began on Friday, November 17, when Sam Altman joined a board meeting and was fired effective immediately, with the board stating he "was not consistently candid in his communications." Chair Greg Brockman learned of the firing minutes later via a Google Meet and resigned the same day. The board's composition—three employees (Altman, Brockman, Sutskever) and three outsiders (Adam D'Angelo of Quora, Helen Toner of Georgetown, Tasha McCauley of GeoSim Systems)—suddenly shifted to a 4-2 dynamic against Altman. Crucially, the board acted without the chair and without notifying Microsoft, its $10B investor.

The weekend saw a rapid escalation. Reports emerged that Sutskever had orchestrated the coup over concerns about safety and the speed of commercialization, with the November 6 Dev Day representing "an inflection moment of Altman pushing too far, too fast." When Altman tweeted "i love the openai team so much," hundreds of employees—including interim CEO Mira Murati—responded with heart emojis, signaling mass loyalty. Sunday brought efforts to reinstate Altman, but the board instead appointed Emmett Shear (Twitch cofounder) as a second interim CEO after Nat Friedman and Alex Wang declined. Employees refused to attend the emergency all-hands, responding with "fuck you" emojis.

The situation peaked Monday when Satya Nadella announced Altman and Brockman would join Microsoft to head a new AI division. By Monday night, 743 of 778 employees signed a petition threatening to follow them to Microsoft unless the board resigned and Altman returned—meaning OpenAI faced losing 95% of its staff overnight. By Tuesday night, an agreement was reached: Altman returned as CEO, the board was reconstituted, and operations normalized.

Orosz's analysis centers on OpenAI's "exotic" corporate structure: a 2015 nonprofit whose board controls a 2019 "capped profit" entity. This structure created an inherent tension between the stated nonprofit mission of developing safe AGI for humanity's benefit and the commercial pressure of ChatGPT's explosive success. The piece argues this governance tension was the root cause of the crisis and, fundamentally, remains unresolved—leaving the question "What is OpenAI, really?" as pertinent in 2026 as it was in 2023.
