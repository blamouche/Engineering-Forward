# Exploring Agent-Assisted Qualitative Analysis
**Source**: https://www.sh-reya.com/blog/ai-qual-analysis/
**Date**: May 21, 2026
**Author**: Shreya Shankar
**Keywords**: qualitative analysis, grounded theory, AI agents, human-in-the-loop, research methodology, HCI, Claude

## Elevator pitch
Shreya Shankar runs experiments with AI agents performing grounded theory qualitative analysis on tweet data, finding that agents paraphrase rather than analyze, struggle with context-dependent judgment, and create fatigue-inducing human-in-the-loop interfaces.

## Takeaways
- Six different agentic setups were tested for grounded theory analysis, varying methodology instruction and human involvement levels.
- Agents show a strong tendency to paraphrase rather than analyze: the number of "codes" generated per tweet was highly correlated with tweet length (ρ = 0.81), even when longer tweets simply elaborated on the same complaint.
- Even when given grounded theory instructions, agents prematurely converge on fixed framings and don't understand that evaluation criteria evolve throughout qualitative analysis.
- Human-in-the-loop interfaces generated by agents introduced significant fatigue; the interaction design itself became a barrier to effective collaboration.
- Multi-agent setups (hierarchical and independent coders) produced final reports that diverged significantly despite identical inputs, highlighting the subjective nature of qualitative work.

## Synthesis
Shreya Shankar, a newly minted CS professor, explores whether AI agents can assist with one of the most intellectually demanding research workflows: qualitative analysis using grounded theory. Using 451 tweets replying to Sholto Douglas's question "When do you reach for other models instead of Claude?", she ran six agentic conditions varying how much grounded theory methodology was described and where humans were brought into the loop.

The core finding is sobering: agents don't understand what qualitative analysis actually is. They paraphrase instead of analyzing. The number of open codes an agent generated per tweet was highly correlated with tweet length (ρ = 0.81), meaning agents simply restated content in different words rather than identifying distinct analytical concepts. This is a fundamental failure mode — qualitative analysis requires abstraction and pattern recognition, not summarization.

The problem runs deeper than surface-level misunderstanding. Qualitative analysis is inherently context-dependent: the "right" analysis depends on the researcher's background, audience, and evolving sense of what matters. Evaluation criteria themselves shift throughout the workflow as researchers discover what's important through iterative engagement with the data. Current agentic systems tend to assume stable objectives and converge prematurely on fixed framings, making them poorly suited for the emergent, iterative nature of grounded theory.

The human-in-the-loop experiments revealed interface design as a critical bottleneck. Agents generated browser UIs for feedback collection, but these interfaces introduced significant fatigue. The interaction patterns — editing codes, highlighting phrases, writing feedback on memos — felt burdensome rather than collaborative. The multi-agent setups (hierarchical supervisor-worker and independent coder reconciliation) produced final reports that diverged dramatically despite identical inputs, underscoring how much qualitative analysis depends on subjective judgment that cannot be easily distributed or automated.

Shankar's experiments suggest that effective agent-assisted qualitative analysis requires fundamentally rethinking how agents engage with interpretive work. Rather than attempting to replace the researcher's judgment, future systems might focus on augmenting specific subtasks — surfacing potentially interesting passages, tracking how codes evolve over time, or maintaining audit trails of analytical decisions — while keeping the researcher firmly in control of the interpretive process. The blog post, while explicitly not a formal paper, provides a valuable empirical contribution to understanding where current AI systems fall short on tasks requiring taste, judgment, and iterative sensemaking.
