# Anthropic beats OpenAI on business adoption
**Source**: https://econlab.substack.com/p/anthropic-beats-openai
**Date**: May 13, 2026
**Author**: Ara Kharazian (Ramp Economics Lab)
**Keywords**: Anthropic, OpenAI, AI model providers, business adoption, Ramp AI Index, enterprise AI, Claude, GPT, AI inference platforms, cost optimization, Codex

## Elevator pitch
For the first time, Anthropic passed OpenAI in business adoption at 34.4% to 32.3%, but three headwinds — misaligned incentives, declining Claude quality, and cost-inefficient model updates — suggest the lead may be fragile.

## Takeaways
- Anthropic quadrupled business adoption over the last year while OpenAI grew by only 0.3%, according to Ramp spend data across thousands of businesses
- Overall AI adoption reached 50.6% of businesses, meaning half of companies still run no AI in production
- Anthropic's incentive structure is misaligned with customers: the company profits more when businesses use expensive models, creating pressure to route to costly models even when cheaper ones suffice
- Claude quality complaints (frequent outages, rate limits, declining results) and a model update that 3x token costs for image prompts are eroding goodwill
- The fastest-growing vendors on Ramp's platform are AI inference platforms providing access to cheap, open-source models, suggesting a potential shift toward cost-optimized alternatives

## Synthesis
Ara Kharazian's May 13, 2026 update to the Ramp AI Index delivers a milestone finding: Anthropic has overtaken OpenAI in business adoption for the first time, with 34.4% of businesses using Anthropic compared to OpenAI's 32.3%. The data comes from Ramp's corporate card and invoice-based payments across thousands of American businesses, providing a ground-truth view of actual AI spending rather than survey-based or self-reported adoption metrics.

The headline numbers tell a story of momentum. Over the past year, Anthropic quadrupled its business adoption base while OpenAI grew by only 0.3%. OpenAI's near-flat growth during a period of explosive AI adoption suggests its early-mover advantage may have been fully captured, while Anthropic captured successive waves of new adopters. Overall AI adoption now sits at 50.6% of businesses, up 0.2 percentage points from the previous month — meaning half of American businesses still operate with no AI in production.

Kharazian's analysis is notable for its caution about reading too much into the milestone. He identifies three specific headwinds facing Anthropic that suggest the lead could be temporary. First, incentive misalignment: Anthropic's revenue model rewards driving users toward more expensive models, even when cheaper models are sufficient and faster. Uber's CTO announced the company already blew through its 2026 AI budget, and businesses facing similar cost pressure may route toward cheaper alternatives.

Second, Claude quality degradation: users have experienced frequent outages, rate limits, and declining result quality in recent weeks. Anthropic responded by resetting usage limits for all users in April and signing a deal with SpaceX to resolve compute constraints, but the reputational damage from unreliable service during a growth phase is significant.

Third, counterproductive model updates: Anthropic's latest model change triples token costs for any prompt containing an image. Kharazian questions why the product roadmap would prioritize a change that increases costs more than it drives user value, especially when compute is already constrained and costs are rising.

The competitive landscape is shifting in response. The fastest-growing vendors on Ramp's platform are AI inference platforms giving businesses access to cheap, open-source models. OpenAI's Codex is noted as a competent, cheaper alternative with minimal switching costs. Kharazian flags two indicators to watch: OpenAI's market share trajectory (including Codex subscription growth) and the continued expansion of inference platforms for cheaper models.

The piece is framed within a broader observation about the AI software industry's unprecedented dynamism, where market leadership can change in months and vendor stickiness — typically a powerful moat in enterprise software — has limited force when models are increasingly commoditized and switching costs are low.
