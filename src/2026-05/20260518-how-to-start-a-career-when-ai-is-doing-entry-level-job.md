# How to Start a Career When AI Is Doing Your Entry-level Job
**Source**: https://every.to/working-overtime/how-to-start-a-career-when-ai-is-doing-your-entry-level-job
**Date**: 2026-05-18
**Author**: Katie Parrott
**Keywords**: entry-level jobs, AI job displacement, career advice, Gen Z, skills-based hiring, AI apprenticeship, career coaching, judgment

## Elevator pitch
Katie Parrott offers four pieces of career advice for graduates entering a workforce where AI is rapidly absorbing entry-level tasks: chase problems not professions, protect one craft from AI, build things before anyone asks, and create your own AI career coach.

## Takeaways
- Stanford's Digital Economy Lab found employment for 22-to-25-year-olds in AI-vulnerable roles dropped 13% since late 2022, while older workers in the same roles held steady
- Employers now expect entry-level workers to not just use AI but evaluate tools, review outputs, and improve results — skills that require judgment typically built through experience
- Job titles are becoming unreliable targets; the role you chase today might not exist in 18 months — instead, pick a problem you care about and develop expertise around it
- The 10,000-hour rule applies with new urgency: protect one craft (writing, coding, designing) from AI automation to build the judgment that lets you spot when AI output is wrong
- Building a personal AI career coach using ChatGPT or Claude projects, loaded with your work samples and context, can provide structure, feedback, and accountability at zero cost

## Synthesis
Katie Parrott opens with a personal story that frames the entire essay: her first job out of college was writing templated funding profiles at a crowdfunding startup. The work was rote and the pay was nonexistent, but it taught her how to take a company apart and rebuild it as a narrative — a skill that became the foundation of her career. Today, AI could produce one of those profiles in two minutes. Her 23-year-old self would have said "Thank God." Her 36-year-old self says "Thank God it couldn't."

This tension drives the essay. Entry-level work has always been the apprenticeship layer of the economy — the place where people learn to do things badly enough times to eventually do them well. AI is absorbing that layer before young workers can learn from it. The Stanford data Parrott cites is sobering: a 13% employment drop for 22-to-25-year-olds in AI-vulnerable jobs, while older workers held steady. The paradox is that employers simultaneously demand AI evaluation skills that require precisely the judgment those eliminated entry-level jobs used to build.

Parrott's four pieces of advice are pragmatic and specific. "Chase problems, not professions" reframes career targeting around durable intellectual interests rather than brittle job titles — the problem of getting strangers to care about something will outlast the title "content marketer." "Choose one discipline to protect" is the most counterintuitive: in an era where AI can write your sentences, you must write your own sentences to build the judgment that lets you recognize when AI output is wrong. "Make things before anyone asks you to" connects to Eleanor Warnock's portfolio thesis — creating visible artifacts of capability is more valuable than credentials in a skills-based hiring environment. "Build the career coach you wish you had" provides a complete template for creating an AI coaching project, including a detailed prompt with fill-in-the-blank sections for personal context.

The essay's closing argument is that the things AI rewards — curiosity, willingness to question orthodoxy, idealism about how work could work — are precisely the things young people have in surplus. The people who will thrive are those who use those traits to actively build capability rather than waiting for the market to reorganize around their needs.
