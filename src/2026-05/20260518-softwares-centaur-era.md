# Software's Centaur Era
**Source**: https://twitchard.github.io/posts/2026-05-18-softwares-centaur-era.html
**Date**: May 18, 2026
**Author**: Richard Marmorstein
**Keywords**: ai, software-engineering, coding-agents, centaur, automation, jobs, career

## Elevator pitch
The software industry hasn't even entered its "centaur era" yet — the period between AI surpassing human capability and humans still adding value in collaboration — because coding agents still cannot reliably make independent progress on long-horizon projects, which is the economic core of why engineers are valuable.

## Takeaways
- The "centaur era" concept comes from chess: after Deep Blue beat Kasparov in 1997, human+engine teams outperformed engines alone for decades — only recently have pure engines definitively surpassed centaurs
- Coding agents today are not at "Deep Blue beats Kasparov" stage: left alone without human steering, they quickly go off the rails and break codebases
- The ability to reliably make independent progress on long-horizon projects remains the absolute frontier of what models are beginning to become capable of
- As long as human+agent teams outperform agent-only teams, there will be room for humans in software
- Computer science enrollment is shrinking due to AI fears, despite the centaur era not having begun

## Synthesis
Richard Marmorstein opens with a dystopian 2030 vignette — a former knowledge worker whose only remaining task is resisting the urge to contribute anything, because doing so would "strictly decrease expected return" — before dismantling the premise that got us there. His central argument is elegant in its simplicity: for software jobs to truly disappear, AI must not just surpass humans at coding, but render human contribution entirely superfluous. We are nowhere near that point.

The chess analogy provides the analytical framework. Deep Blue defeated Kasparov in 1997, yet for decades afterward, "centaur" teams of humans wielding chess engines outperformed engines playing alone. The centaur era ended only when engines became so strong that even grandmaster steering weakened them — a threshold crossed very recently. Software engineering, Marmorstein argues, hasn't even reached the equivalent of the Deep Blue moment. Claude can build a vibe-coded C compiler, and Cursor can generate a browser, but neither can be trusted to independently improve a software system over weeks and months — which is the actual economic value that justifies engineering salaries.

The piece makes a crucial distinction between impressive demos and economic reliability. Long-horizon autonomous improvement is "the absolute frontier" of what models are just beginning to approach. Until that frontier is crossed, the centaur era can't begin. And once it begins, it could last decades — as it did in chess — during which human+agent teams will remain the dominant paradigm. The real concern Marmorstein identifies isn't immediate job displacement but self-fulfilling prophecy: computer science enrollment is already shrinking because students believe the jobs won't exist, even though the centaur era hasn't started. The most dangerous AI impact on software careers may be the fear of AI itself.
