# I'm the C.E.O. of Goldman Sachs. The A.I. Job Apocalypse Is Overblown.
**Source**: https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/22/opinion/ai-job-crisis-goldman-sachs.html
**Date**: May 22, 2026
**Author**: David Solomon (CEO, Goldman Sachs)
**Keywords**: AI, employment, job automation, Goldman Sachs, labor market, white-collar, economic adaptation

## Elevator pitch
Goldman Sachs CEO David Solomon argues that fears of AI-driven mass unemployment are overblown — the U.S. economy will adapt, AI will augment rather than replace most jobs, and new roles for managing AI will emerge.

## Takeaways
- Goldman Sachs analysis estimates AI may automate 25% of current work hours over the next decade
- White-collar jobs in accounting, banking, and law will see many tasks automated, while blue-collar impact is harder to predict
- Solomon's three reasons for optimism: AI frees humans for complex tasks, enhances existing professions rather than making them obsolete, and creates new jobs for managing AI
- If AI destroys jobs at unprecedented scale, a joint public-private effort should help workers adapt
- Solomon cites Keynes' 1930 prediction of 15-hour workweeks as a reminder that job apocalypse fears have historically been wrong

## Synthesis
In a New York Times guest essay titled "I'm the C.E.O. of Goldman Sachs. The A.I. Job Apocalypse Is Overblown," Goldman Sachs CEO David Solomon makes the case that the panic around AI-driven mass unemployment is misplaced. The essay comes at a moment of heightened anxiety about AI's labor market impact, and Solomon's position — as the leader of a firm whose own economists have been studying AI's effects on jobs — gives the argument particular weight.

Solomon grounds his argument in Goldman's own research, which estimates AI may automate approximately 25% of current work hours over the next ten years. He acknowledges this disruption is real and will "entail new challenges," but frames it as manageable rather than catastrophic. The impact will be uneven: white-collar professions like accounting, banking, and law "will likely see many of their tasks automated," while the effect on blue-collar jobs is harder to model.

The CEO offers three structural reasons for optimism. First, AI will free human workers to focus on more complex, higher-value tasks rather than eliminating their roles entirely. Second, AI will enhance the standards of existing professions — making lawyers better lawyers, accountants better accountants — rather than making those professions obsolete. Third, AI adoption will create entirely new categories of jobs focused on managing, overseeing, and directing the AI systems that companies deploy.

Solomon's historical framing is deliberate. He invokes John Maynard Keynes' famous 1930 prediction that by 2030 people would work only 15 hours a week — a forecast that proved wildly wrong but illustrates how technological unemployment fears have consistently underestimated humanity's capacity to create new forms of work. "His vision of a leisure-filled future remains unfulfilled," Solomon writes, "but it is a good reminder that fears of a job apocalypse may very well overlook A.I.'s potential to spur an economic and productivity revival."

The essay doesn't ignore the counterarguments. MIT economist Daron Acemoglu has warned of "excessive automation" that could trigger work shortages, lower workforce participation, and more "menial, meaningless jobs." McKinsey found that 51% of organizations reported in 2025 that generative AI was reducing their need for entry-level workers. Solomon's response is pragmatic: if AI does destroy jobs at unprecedented scale, there should be a "joint effort" between public and private sectors to help workers and institutions adapt — but he clearly believes that scenario is unlikely.
