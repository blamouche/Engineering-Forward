# Mining Your Life for Context
**Source**: https://every.to/context-window/mining-your-life-for-context
**Date**: 2026-05-13
**Author**: Laura Entis
**Keywords**: personal context mining, Claude Code as second brain, AI productivity, content workflows, Noah Brier, Chronicle, knowledge management

## Elevator pitch
A practical guide to mining your scattered thoughts — Slack threads, voice memos, meeting transcripts — into coherent drafts by connecting AI agents to every source where your thinking lives, before you ever face the blank page.

## Takeaways
- By the time you sit down to write, most of your ideas already exist across Slack, Notion, meeting transcripts, and voice memos — the workflow is about surfacing what's already been said
- Noah Brier has been using Claude Code as a "second brain" for months, connecting it to thousands of personal notes for research, thinking, and writing partnership
- Codex's Chronicle feature revealed Every's head of growth opens iMessage 671 times per day — leading to an AI-generated productivity regimen to reduce app-switching
- A three-step workflow: capture everywhere by default, connect every source your agent can read, then name the deliverable and constrain the source to your own past thinking
- Professional writers are increasingly using "aggressively casual" language (intentional typos, colloquialisms, run-on sentences) to distinguish human writing from AI-generated content

## Synthesis
Laura Entis's piece captures a workflow that's emerging as a best practice among AI-native knowledge workers: treat every communication channel as a source for future drafts, then use agents to surface and synthesize before you write. The insight is counterintuitive — by the time you face the blank page, you've already said what you need to say, just in fragments scattered across platforms. The agent's role isn't to generate new ideas but to find and organize the ones you've already expressed.

Noah Brier's use of Claude Code as a "second brain" is the power-user version of this pattern. By connecting Claude Code to thousands of personal notes, he's built a thinking partner that can reference his own intellectual history. His framework of "pace layers" for AI engineering — different parts of an organization change at different speeds — emerged from this practice. The data point about iMessage interruptions (671 times per day) and the Chronicle-generated productivity regimen shows how AI can also expose bad habits: the tool that monitors you can also coach you.

The writerly response to AI content saturation — intentionally weird, colloquial, error-prone prose — is a fascinating cultural signal. As LLM-generated text becomes the default, the most valuable writing becomes what a model would never produce. This isn't just a stylistic trend; it's an economic one. If AI writing is free and abundant, human writing's value proposition shifts from polish to personality, from correctness to distinctiveness. The practical workflow section — capture by default, connect all sources, name the deliverable — is actionable for anyone who works with ideas.
