- The three-step workflow: capture by default (record everything), connect every source your agent can read, then name the deliverable and constrain the source to only your own past statements

## Synthesis
This May 13 edition of Context Window tackles the upstream problem of knowledge work: all the thinking you've already done but can't easily access when you sit down to produce something new. Laura Entis frames the challenge and solution with precision — by the time you face the blank page, you've probably expressed everything you need across a dozen different platforms. The insight isn't just about efficiency; it's about avoiding the dilution that happens when AI generates content from generic patterns rather than your own prior thinking.

The centerpiece workflow is deceptively simple but operationally powerful. Step one: capture by default, using tools like Monologue to record meetings, calls, conversations, and even early-morning ramblings. The specific tool matters less than the habit of recording thinking wherever it happens, not just at a desk. Step two: connect everything your agent can read — Slack, Notion, Google Drive, Monologue Notes, meeting transcripts — into one searchable repository. For sources without native connectors, export files into an agent-accessible folder. Step three: specify the deliverable and explicitly constrain the agent to pull only from what you've already said, preventing the AI from blending your ideas with its own generated concepts.

The profile of Noah Brier deepens the theme. An early adopter who connected Claude Code to thousands of personal notes, Brier treats the coding tool as a thinking partner for research and writing. His newer framework — "pace layers" of AI engineering, adapted from Stewart Brand — addresses the organizational question: how do you keep an entire company aligned on goals when AI makes code free to produce but doesn't make product vision any clearer?

Austin Tedesco's Chronicle experiment provides a quantitative gut check. Codex's screen-context memory analyzed his computer activity and surfaced that iMessage was active 671 times daily. The productivity regimen it produced aims to slash interactions across Slack, email, and Chrome, redirecting focus into the Codex app where drafting and reviewing can happen in a single environment. Tedesco's enthusiasm — "I'm excited by the idea of keeping Codex open and staying focused" — reflects a broader shift toward AI-native workspaces that reduce context-switching.

The discuss section highlights a cultural reaction: as LLMs flatten writing into a predictable median, professional writers are deliberately leaning into the colloquial, the idiosyncratic, and the intentionally imperfect to signal humanity. Copywriter Sarah Suzuki Harvard's strategy of using "aggressively casual language" captures the emerging arms race between machine-generated polish and curated human weirdness — a dynamic that will only intensify as AI-generated content consumes more of the internet.
