# Europe's hot AI startups set to poach more talent from Big Tech
**Source**: https://sifted.eu/articles/europe-ai-startup-hiring-big-tech
**Date**: May 12, 2026
**Author**: Anne Sraders
**Keywords**: AI talent war, Big Tech brain drain, European startups, Cusp AI, Apple, John Giannandrea, AMI Labs, Multiverse, Lovable, hiring, venture capital, deeptech

## Elevator pitch
European AI startups are increasingly poaching top-tier talent from Big Tech giants like Apple, Meta, and Google — a trend that's poised to accelerate as hot startups offer faster innovation cycles, equity upside, and more autonomy.

## Takeaways
- Apple's former AI chief John Giannandrea joined UK-based materials discovery startup Cusp AI to help build its US operations, marking a symbolic turning point in the European AI talent narrative.
- Other European AI startups like AMI Labs (founded by Meta's former chief AI scientist Yann LeCun), Multiverse, and Lovable have also lured senior talent away from Big Tech.
- Startups offer what Big Tech increasingly can't: speed, meaningful equity stakes, and the chance to build something from scratch — which appeals especially to senior AI researchers.
- This talent migration mirrors a broader shift seen with DeepMind and OpenAI alumni spinning out to found or join smaller ventures across Europe.
- The trend could force Big Tech companies to rethink retention strategies — higher comp, more creative autonomy — while potentially decentralizing AI innovation away from Silicon Valley.

## Synthesis

Anne Sraders' Sifted piece captures a pivotal moment in Europe's AI ecosystem: the reversal of a decades-long brain drain that saw the continent's best technical talent funnel into US Big Tech. The catalyst is John Giannandrea, Apple's former head of AI and Siri, who in April 2026 announced he would join Cusp AI, a Cambridge-based startup using AI to discover new materials. Giannandrea's move isn't just any hire — it's the former leader of Apple Intelligence, the person who oversaw the company's entire AI strategy, choosing a European startup over the gravitational pull of Silicon Valley incumbents.

The article situates this hire within a broader trend. AMI Labs, the startup co-founded by Meta's legendary AI scientist Yann LeCun, has already assembled a 100-person team largely poached from Big Tech. Multiverse, the UK-based edtech unicorn, has brought on senior talent from Meta and Google. Lovable, the Swedish AI coding platform, has done the same. These aren't isolated anecdotes — they represent a structural shift in where top AI talent wants to work.

Sraders reports that recruiters on the ground confirm the acceleration. One US-based recruiter quoted in the piece notes that five years ago, they wouldn't have pitched European startups to US-based AI candidates; now, those same candidates are actively receptive. The reasons are straightforward: startups offer equity upside that can be life-changing (Cusp AI alone is reportedly valued above $1 billion), a faster pace of work unburdened by Big Tech bureaucracy, and the chance to build foundational technology from a blank slate. For senior researchers who've spent years navigating internal politics at Apple or Google, that proposition is increasingly compelling.

The economics support it too. Cusp AI has raised over $200 million from investors including NEA, Temasek, Lightspeed, and Nvidia's venture arm. The startup is using that capital not just for R&D but aggressively for talent acquisition — including opening a Bay Area office specifically to recruit US-based AI researchers. Other European startups mentioned in the piece are similarly well-capitalized, with compensation packages that can compete with FAANG levels, especially when factoring in equity.

The article doesn't shy away from the implications for Big Tech. Giannandrea's departure from Apple is particularly sensitive: his tenure there was already viewed as troubled, with Apple falling behind in generative AI and eventually relying on Google's Gemini to power new Siri features. Losing him — and watching him decamp to a European materials-science AI startup — is both a symbolic and practical blow. For Meta, the departure of LeCun to found AMI Labs represents a similar loss of institutional AI credibility. Google DeepMind, despite its European roots, has also seen alumni leave to join or found startups across the continent.

Sraders includes a note of caution: the trend may partially reflect a cyclical hiring market where well-funded startups can temporarily outbid incumbents. But the consensus among sources is that something more fundamental is shifting. Europe's AI ecosystem has matured to the point where it can credibly compete with Silicon Valley for the world's best technical talent — not on every dimension, but specifically in areas like deeptech, materials science, and AI-for-science where European research institutions provide natural advantages.

The piece ultimately frames this as a talent war with structural consequences. If Europe can retain and attract this caliber of AI leadership, it could reshape the geography of AI innovation over the next decade — moving from a model where Europe trains talent that America captures, to one where Europe builds companies that matter globally. The Giannandrea hire, Sraders suggests, might be remembered as the moment that shift became undeniable.
