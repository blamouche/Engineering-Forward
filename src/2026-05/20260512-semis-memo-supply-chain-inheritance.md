# Semis Memo: Supply Chain Inheritance
**Source**: https://www.citriniresearch.com/p/semis-memo-supply-chain-inheritance
**Date**: May 12, 2026
**Author**: Citrini (Zephyr and Jukan, semis analysts)
**Keywords**: semiconductors, analog, power semis, MLCC, capacitors, AI infrastructure, supply chain, EVs, neoclouds, CPUs, inference, Korea, materials bottlenecks

## Elevator pitch
Citrini Research's Semis Memo argues that AI data center buildout is "inheriting" the electric vehicle supply chain, with analog and power semiconductor manufacturers choosing to raise prices rather than add capacity — creating a compelling investment setup as AI demand overwhelms non-AI headwinds.

## Takeaways
- AI data center demand is now the primary driver for analog and power semiconductors (MOSFETs, diodes, capacitors, inductors), which were previously dragged down by EV/automotive weakness and Chinese competition
- Manufacturers like Texas Instruments and NXP are choosing to raise ASPs rather than expand capacity, a pattern Citrini calls "Post-Traumatic Supply Disorder" — they've been burned too many times by supply gluts
- Nvidia itself credited "the electric vehicle and solar industries" as the foundation for its 800V DC rack architecture, explicitly linking the EV supply chain to AI infrastructure
- The MLCC (Multilayer Ceramic Capacitors) shortage is already driving performance in names like Murata, Vishay, and Samsung Electro-Mechanics
- The memo also covers CPUs in the agentic era, an inference shortage at neoclouds, AI materials bottlenecks, and the Korea semiconductor opportunity

## Synthesis
Citrini Research's May 12, 2026 Semis Memo introduces the concept of "Supply Chain Inheritance" — the thesis that AI infrastructure is not building a new supply chain from scratch but rather absorbing and repurposing the industrial base built for the electric vehicle revolution. This insight reframes the investment case for analog and power semiconductors, a sector that has languished under multiple headwinds: the post-COVID supply chain glut, competition from aggressive Chinese manufacturers, and a prolonged slump in automotive and EV demand.

The core argument is deceptively simple. Companies like Texas Instruments and NXP Semiconductors spent years building capacity to serve the expected EV boom, only to see that demand materialize more slowly than anticipated. Now, AI data center construction — with its enormous power requirements — is stepping into that capacity. But unlike previous cycles where manufacturers responded to demand spikes by ramping supply, these companies are opting to raise average selling prices (ASPs) instead. Citrini's capex intensity chart for Texas Instruments shows no surge in capital expenditure, while a companion chart for NXP illustrates rising ASPs over recent quarters.

This restraint is what Citrini terms "Post-Traumatic Supply Disorder" — a reference to their earlier "26 Trades for 2026" framework. Having been burned by the COVID-era glut, the Chinese price war, and the false-start EV cycle, power semi manufacturers are displaying unusual pricing discipline. The result is a supply-constrained environment where AI demand growth flows directly to margins rather than being absorbed by new capacity.

The inheritance thesis gains credibility from an unlikely source: Nvidia. In a May 2025 technical blog on 800V DC rack architecture, Nvidia explicitly credited the underlying power technology to "the electric vehicle and solar industries." This isn't metaphorical — it's a direct technology transfer. The high-voltage power distribution systems, power quality management components (capacitors, inductors, MOSFETs, surge protectors, UPS), and filtering technologies developed for EVs are precisely what AI data centers need at rack scale. The "rack-external" power story — everything outside the server — is where Citrini sees the highest-conviction opportunity.

The memo expands beyond analog/power into several adjacent themes. The CPU analysis examines how the shift to agentic AI workloads changes processor requirements. The neocloud section flags an "Inference Shortage" — demand for inference compute outpacing capacity as models become more agentic and tool-calling multiplies token consumption. Materials bottlenecks cover the upstream constraints on chip fabrication. The Korea section examines the country's semiconductor ecosystem post-recent policy developments.

For investors, the actionable conclusion is that the AI infrastructure trade has moved beyond the obvious first-order beneficiaries (Nvidia GPUs, memory makers like Micron and SK Hynix). The second-order play — the power delivery and quality management infrastructure that makes AI compute physically possible — is where the next wave of outperformance lies, and the supply discipline of this sector makes it particularly attractive as demand scales.
