## Elevator pitch
OpenCode co-founder Dax Raad shares 14 hard-won lessons on building one of the fastest-growing AI developer tools, arguing that taste and engineering judgment matter more than ever — and that AI makes coding easier but doesn't make the hard parts of the job disappear.

## Takeaways
- AI makes coding easier, but the hard parts of the job (thinking about what to build) don't vanish — Dax still spends ~95% of his energy thinking and only ~4-5% doing
- Thinking upfront beats building prototypes and seeing what sticks, especially before product-market fit — AI doesn't help much in the "what to build" phase
- Shipping 10x more features is a recipe for a Frankenstein product; product positioning beats speed of execution — OpenCode won by claiming the open-source category nobody else had
- Most software engineers profit from AI as time gained, not increased output — until incentive structures change, output stays flat while engineers go home earlier
- AI code generation mutes the "guilt" of writing hacks, building up tech debt; but agents also make refactoring cheaper than ever, so teams should do more of it
- Old "enterprise" patterns (domain-driven design, verbose design docs) are coming back because agents are the new junior engineers that need strong guardrails
- The future-proof tech career: solid software engineering plus deep industry expertise — engineers undervalue how easily they can become domain insiders

## Synthesis
In this Pragmatic Engineer podcast episode, Gergely Orosz interviews Dax Raad, co-founder of OpenCode — an open-source AI coding harness that surged from roughly 650,000 monthly active users to nearly 8 million in just a few months. The conversation offers a refreshingly skeptical perspective from someone building one of the most popular AI coding tools, while remaining personally cautious about AI's transformative promises.

A central theme is that AI makes the mechanical parts of coding easier but does not eliminate the hard cognitive work. Dax observes that his thinking-to-doing ratio has barely shifted — from 95/5 to 96/4 — and that the hardest part of the job remains figuring out what to build, not how fast you can build it. This is especially true before product-market fit, where AI provides little leverage because the core problem is direction, not execution speed.

On product strategy, Dax argues that positioning beat speed of execution as the key to OpenCode's growth. He noticed that no coding agent had claimed the open-source category, despite every market-leading dev tool being open source. By focusing on positioning first and accepting a "good enough" harness during the first five months, OpenCode captured market share before optimizing the product. He summarizes: "Get positioning right and the world just keeps handing you wins you didn't expect."

Perhaps the most provocative insight is about organizational dynamics. Dax reports that most engineers "cash out" AI productivity gains as time saved rather than increased output — they do the same work faster and go home earlier. Until compensation and motivation structures change, AI vendors' promises of increased output to CFOs won't materialize. He also describes a growing divide: motivated engineers who care about quality get buried by "slop PRs" from devs who don't, creating tech debt and burnout. AI code generation hides the guilt of hacks, reducing the natural pressure to refactor — but Dax counters that agents also make refactoring across a codebase cheaper than ever, so teams should actively do more of it.

The conversation closes with career advice: engineers should combine solid software engineering with deep industry expertise, something most engineers undervalue. Old-school patterns like domain-driven design are returning because agents, like junior engineers, need strong guardrails to produce quality code.
