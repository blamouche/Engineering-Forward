# The AI market feels incredibly acquisitive. Should that change how I pick a company?
**Source**: https://sifted.eu/articles/ai-market-change-how-i-pick-a-company
**Date**: May 26, 2026
**Author**: Tom Nugent
**Keywords**: AI startups, career strategy, acquisitions, founder quality, M&A, tech talent, acqui-hires, defensibility

## Elevator pitch
Tom Nugent advises AI startup job seekers not to fear an acquisitive market but to reframe the question: instead of asking "will this company get acquired?", ask "what will this experience make me capable of next?"

## Takeaways
- Big Tech has spent tens of billions on AI acqui-hires in two years, driven by talent scarcity and speed, not just eliminating competitors — OpenAI alone has made over a dozen acquisitions since 2024.
- Founder quality matters more in an acquisitive market: the best founders believe normal outcomes don't apply to them and aren't optimizing for optionality, which paradoxically gives their teams the most options.
- The team you join often matters more than the company — exceptional colleagues compound in value far beyond any single company's lifespan and become your best career insurance.
- Evaluate defensibility by whether the company owns the outcome vs. just a tool: companies selling outcomes turn better models into cheaper inputs, not existential threats.
- An acquisition is not a wasted experience — living through the full lifecycle of an early-stage company teaches you things that compound your ability to build something meaningful later.

## Synthesis
In Sifted's "Ask Zoe" column, managing editor Tom Nugent responds to a reader torn between AI startup job offers in an "incredibly acquisitive" market, delivering nuanced career advice that goes beyond the obvious concern about company longevity.

Nugent opens by validating the premise: the acquisition pace is extraordinary. OpenAI has made over a dozen acquisitions since 2024, Google paid $2.4bn to acqui-hire Windsurf's leadership, and Workday acquired Stockholm's Sana Labs for $1.1bn. Even startups are using acqui-hires to win engineering talent. But he immediately reframes the question — the right lens is not "will this company get acquired?" but "what will this experience make me capable of next?"

The article offers four concrete evaluation frameworks. First, founder quality becomes more important, not less. The best founders combine deep conviction with genuine founder-market fit and pursue visions so ambitious that selling early would feel like failure — and acquirers pay the highest premiums for teams who don't want to be acquired. Second, "bet on the room" — the colleagues you join with often matter more than the legal entity, and exceptional people become your best career insurance policy.

Third, evaluate defensibility through the lens of whether the company owns the outcome or just a tool. Drawing on Sequoia partner Julien Bek's framework, Nugent argues that companies selling outcomes turn better AI models into cheaper inputs rather than existential threats. Fourth, consider what the experience teaches you: living through the full lifecycle of an early-stage company — shipping under constraints, hiring without brand recognition, navigating a potential deal — is proof of capability that compounds throughout a career.

The article's central message is that the best startup careers aren't built by eliminating uncertainty but by choosing the right uncertainty and committing fully. In an acquisitive market, the things you learn and the people you build with are the only truly certain outcomes.
