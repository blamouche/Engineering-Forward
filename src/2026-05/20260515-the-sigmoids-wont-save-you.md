# The Sigmoids Won't Save You

**Source:** Astral Codex Ten (Substack)
**Author:** Scott Alexander
**Date:** May 15, 2026
**URL:** https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-sigmoids-wont-save-you

## Summary

Scott Alexander dismantles the common AI-skeptic talking point that "all exponentials eventually become sigmoids" and therefore AI progress will inevitably plateau before reaching dangerous levels. Using examples from birthrate projections, solar power deployment, and AI capability forecasting, he shows that while all trends eventually flatten, this rarely happens at the exact moment forecasters predict. He applies Lindy's Law to argue that, absent specific knowledge, we should expect AI progress to continue roughly as long as it has already — about another 7 years.

## Key Points

- **The sigmoid argument:** "All exponentials eventually become sigmoids" is technically true but used lazily to dismiss AI risk without engaging with specifics
- **Hall of Fame of sigmoid misidentification:**
  - UN birthrate projections (kept predicting flattening, kept declining)
  - Solar power deployment forecasts (IEA/WEO consistently predicted leveling off, deployment kept growing)
  - Wharton team's 2026 AI capability forecast (predicted plateau; the very next model release blew past it)
- **Lindy's Law:** In conditions of true ignorance, expect a process to continue about as long as it's already continued — AI has been improving dramatically since ~2019, so ~7 more years
- **Burden of proof:** If someone claims AI won't reach a scary level, they must either present an explicit model (engaging with existing work like the AI Futures Timeline Model) or explain why Lindy's Law doesn't apply
- **Key insight:** The fact that trends eventually become sigmoids doesn't mean they become sigmoids at the exact moment you're doing your analysis

## Why It Matters

This is a direct rebuttal to one of the most common rhetorical moves in AI discourse. Alexander provides a rigorous framework (Lindy's Law + demand for explicit modeling) for evaluating claims about AI progress plateauing. The Wharton example — where a formal academic model predicted a plateau that was immediately falsified by the next release — is particularly damning for those who dismiss AI capability trends without engaging with the underlying dynamics.
