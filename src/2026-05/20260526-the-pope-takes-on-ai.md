# The Pope Takes On AI
**Source**: https://www.bigtechnology.com/p/the-pope-takes-on-ai
**Date**: May 26, 2026
**Author**: Alex Kantrowitz and Marty Swant
**Keywords**: Pope Leo XIV, Vatican, AI ethics, encyclical, AI regulation, Anthropic, disinformation, algorithmic addiction, AI harms

## Elevator pitch
Pope Leo XIV released a 55-page encyclical on AI, calling out its potential harms — war, disinformation, surveillance, algorithmic addiction — and arguing for slower, more deliberate adoption, as the technology's public reputation continues to deteriorate.

## Takeaways
- Pope Leo XIV released a 55-page encyclical titled "Magnifica Humanitas" that is ambitious, specific, and potentially uncomfortable for AI companies.
- The pope's growing concern tracks AI's evolution from chatbots to autonomous agents capable of independent action — exactly the shift happening in the industry.
- He called for AI to be "disarmed," likening it to a weapon of war, and argued that a pause in adoption isn't opposed to progress.
- The pope explicitly stated AI cannot offer genuine human connection, love, and care — a direct challenge to AI companion and relationship products.
- Some AI labs, including Anthropic, are reportedly already working with the Vatican, suggesting the encyclical is part of an ongoing dialogue rather than a purely adversarial stance.
- The encyclical arrives as AI's public reputation craters, adding another critical voice alongside growing regulatory and public concern.

## Synthesis
Alex Kantrowitz and Marty Swant report on Pope Leo XIV's landmark 55-page encyclical "Magnifica Humanitas," positioning it as one of the most significant institutional critiques of AI to date. The timing is notable: the pope's concerns have escalated in parallel with AI's evolution from simple chatbots to autonomous agents, mirroring the trajectory that is simultaneously exciting the industry and alarming critics.

The encyclical is remarkably specific in its criticism, calling out AI's potential to enable war, spread disinformation, enable mass surveillance, drive algorithmic addiction, and degrade democratic processes. The pope framed the challenge in ecological terms — "a new dimension of our common home" — suggesting AI now constitutes an environment that humanity must learn to inhabit responsibly, not merely a tool to deploy.

A striking philosophical argument in the document contrasts algorithmic and human approaches to error. For an algorithm, the pope wrote, an error is a flaw to be corrected; for a person, an error can catalyze profound change. This distinction cuts to the heart of debates about AI in creative, therapeutic, and educational contexts, where mistakes and struggle are often essential to growth.

The most provocative recommendation is the call for AI to be "disarmed" — treated as a weapon of war subject to international control. While previous efforts to slow AI development have failed (the 2023 "pause" letter being the most prominent example), the Vatican's moral authority and reach give this intervention a different weight. The pope is among the world's most influential and well-liked figures, and his intervention arrives when AI companies are acutely sensitive to their deteriorating public reputation.

The article hints at a more complex picture: some AI labs, notably Anthropic, are reportedly engaging with the Vatican rather than dismissing its concerns. This suggests the encyclical may be less a condemnation from outside the industry and more part of an emerging dialogue between AI developers and traditional moral institutions — a dynamic that could shape governance and public perception in the coming years.

The encyclical's release also coincides with a broader reputational crisis for AI, which Kantrowitz and Swant have been tracking. As the technology moves from novelty to infrastructure, the questions the pope is asking — about power, autonomy, human connection, and the pace of change — are becoming impossible for the industry to ignore.
