# Sundar Pichai on AI, the future of search, and what's happening to the web
**Source**: https://www.theverge.com/podcast/936445/sundar-pichai-ai-search-google-zero-youtube-web
**Date**: May 26, 2026
**Author**: Nilay Patel
**Keywords**: Google, Sundar Pichai, AI, search, Google Zero, Gemini, YouTube, AGI, Google DeepMind, organization restructure, Decoder podcast, Google I/O 2026

## Elevator pitch
Sundar Pichai details how he restructured Google to compete in the AI era — merging Brain and DeepMind, unifying search leadership, and preparing for a world where Google Search itself could render traditional web traffic obsolete.

## Takeaways
- Pichai restructured Google around three core businesses (Search, YouTube, Cloud) powered by a unified AI infrastructure (Google DeepMind), enabling faster, more consistent product development across 13 billion-user products.
- The concept of "Google Zero" — where Google answers queries directly without sending traffic to websites — has evolved from a theory Pichai once dismissed to a reality major publishers like Condé Nast now plan for.
- Google sees the convergence of its intelligent search box with the Gemini Spark agent platform as the future: searches that trigger tasks, not just deliver results.
- YouTube is next in line for disruption, with AI-powered video summarization and indexing that drops users into relevant moments — setting up potential conflicts with creators similar to those with web publishers.
- Pichai endorses Demis Hassabis's "foothills of the singularity" framing and shares his thoughts on the accelerating timeline toward AGI.

## Synthesis
In this wide-ranging Decoder interview recorded just after Google I/O 2026, Sundar Pichai offers a rare behind-the-scenes look at how he has reshaped Google — and by extension the internet — for the AI era. This is the fifth annual post-I/O conversation between Pichai and Nilay Patel, and it covers everything from organizational restructuring to the existential questions facing the open web.

Pichai describes Google's current structure as organized around three main businesses — Search, YouTube, and Google Cloud — underpinned by massive platforms (Android, Chrome) and powered by a unified AI layer through Google DeepMind. This consolidation, he explains, was a deliberate response to the "ChatGPT moment." Recognizing that the competitive landscape had fundamentally shifted, Pichai merged Google Brain and DeepMind into a single AI research organization — a move he compares to combining Stanford and MIT into one university. He also centralized AI infrastructure under Amin Vahdat and appointed Koray Kavukcuoglu as chief AI architect. For Search specifically, he consolidated leadership under Elizabeth Reid and Nick Fox, and instituted weekly AI product reviews where he personally oversees everything AI-related shipping to users.

On decision-making, Pichai's framework is pragmatic: most decisions aren't consequential, and the velocity gained from simply making them outweighs the risk of error. Only a handful — like merging DeepMind — warrant deep deliberation. He dismisses the extreme organizational experiments happening at Meta and Block, emphasizing that at Google's scale, strong human leaders remain essential even as AI agents increasingly augment engineering teams.

The most consequential part of the conversation centers on the future of the web. Patel, who coined the term "Google Zero" years ago, presses Pichai on the accelerating trend of Google answering queries directly on its results page rather than sending traffic to external sites. What Pichai once batted away is now an industry-wide reckoning: Condé Nast's CEO has publicly told publishers to plan for zero search traffic. Pichai acknowledges this tension but frames the integration of Search with Gemini Spark — where searches become task-initiating agents — as the logical next step. This raises profound questions about whether the open web as we know it can survive a search engine that increasingly becomes a destination rather than a gateway.

YouTube faces a parallel disruption. Google is training models on YouTube content and redesigning search to summarize videos and drop viewers into relevant segments. When Patel asks if Pichai is prepared for the creator backlash that will likely mirror publisher frustrations, the implication is clear: the platforms that built their value on user-generated content are now positioned to extract that value without necessarily sending traffic back to creators.

The interview closes on an ambitious note, with Pichai aligning himself with Demis Hassabis's view that we are "in the foothills of the singularity." While Pichai stops short of providing a specific AGI timeline, his confidence in the trajectory is unmistakable. For a CEO who has presided over perhaps the most consequential restructuring in Google's history, the message is clear: the AI era is not a passing wave — it is the new foundation upon which Google, and the internet itself, is being rebuilt.
