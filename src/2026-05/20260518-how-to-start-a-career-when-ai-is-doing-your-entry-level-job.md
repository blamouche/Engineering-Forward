# How to Start a Career When AI Is Doing Your Entry-level Job
**Source**: https://every.to/working-overtime/how-to-start-a-career-when-ai-is-doing-your-entry-level-job
**Date**: 2026-05-18
**Author**: Katie Parrott
**Keywords**: entry-level jobs, AI job displacement, career advice, AI skills, skills-based hiring, career coaching, Gen Z, Stanford Digital Economy Lab

## Elevator pitch
With AI eliminating many entry-level tasks, new graduates need to chase problems instead of job titles, protect one craft from automation, build things before being asked, and use AI as a career coach.

## Takeaways
- Stanford research shows employment for 22-to-25-year-olds in AI-vulnerable jobs has dropped 13% since late 2022, while older workers in the same roles held steady
- Employers are shifting to skills-based hiring and want workers who can evaluate AI outputs and improve them, not just type prompts
- The "18-month career" phenomenon means job titles are increasingly unstable targets; focusing on persistent problems is more sustainable
- Building an AI career coach in ChatGPT or Claude is a practical project that teaches AI literacy while providing real career guidance
- AI rewards traits young people have in surplus: curiosity, willingness to question established practices, and idealism about how work could be different

## Synthesis
Katie Parrott reflects on her first job out of college — writing templatized funding profiles for startups — a job AI could now do in two minutes. That job taught her how to take companies apart and reassemble them as stories, how to organize information for disinterested audiences, and how to tell good work from bad. Today's graduates face a paradox: the entry-level jobs that taught those skills are being automated away precisely when employers increasingly demand the judgment those jobs were supposed to build.

The data backs up the anxiety. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab found employment for the youngest workers in AI-vulnerable roles has dropped 13% since late 2022. Meanwhile, demand for AI skills in entry-level jobs has nearly tripled, but employers want more than prompt-typing ability — they want workers who can evaluate tools, review outputs, and figure out how to improve them.

Parrott offers four pieces of practical advice. First, chase problems, not professions — the role of "content marketer" may vanish, but the problem of getting strangers to pay attention to something won't. Second, choose one discipline to protect from AI at all costs — write your own sentences, code your own programs, because you need the hours of doing things badly to eventually do them well. Third, make things before anyone asks you to, since skills-based hiring cares more about what you can demonstrate than where you've been. Fourth, build an AI career coach — a project that doubles as a portfolio piece and a tool for navigating the uncertainty.

The underlying message is that waiting for the market to reorganize around what's fair won't work. The senior people who should be training the next generation aren't going to wake up tomorrow and remember they were once trained too. The people who will thrive are those who get curious about what's possible and start building now.
