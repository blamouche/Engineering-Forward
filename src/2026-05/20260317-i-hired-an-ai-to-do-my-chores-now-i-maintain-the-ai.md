# I Hired an AI to Do My Chores. Now I Maintain the AI.

**Date**: March 17, 2026 (Updated May 15, 2026)
**Author**: Jack Cheng
**Keywords**: AI agents, digital maintenance, OpenClaw, personal AI assistants, upkeep, Stewart Brand, automation paradox, COBOL

## Elevator pitch
Setting up an OpenClaw AI agent to handle digital chores revealed a paradox: instead of the AI maintaining his digital life, the author ended up maintaining the AI — and discovered that the struggle with broken systems teaches us things automation cannot.

## Takeaways
- The author set up OpenClaw hoping it would handle iCloud storage alerts, password changes, and medical billing bureaucracy automatically
- Since 2023, he'd been hosting "Digital Mending Circles" — group sessions for tackling maintenance tasks that feel surprisingly daunting despite their triviality
- Drawing on Stewart Brand's philosophy of maintenance ("absolutely necessary and optional"), the piece argues maintenance is virtuous but culturally undervalued
- AI tools like Claude Cowork and Sparkle can handle many digital chores swiftly, but the author finds himself maintaining the AI rather than the other way around
- The piece explores COBOL Cowboys and civic technologists watching Claude Code attempt to modernize government benefits systems
- The core insight: struggling with broken systems ourselves teaches us things about those systems that delegating to AI cannot

## Synthesis
Jack Cheng's essay is a meditation on maintenance in the age of AI agents, framed through his personal experiment with OpenClaw. He set up the agent hoping it would handle the "petty bureaucracy of modern life" — iCloud storage caps, passwords leaked to the dark web across 1,000+ accounts, a confusing medical bill. Instead, he found himself maintaining the AI agent rather than the AI maintaining his digital life.

The essay draws heavily on Stewart Brand's "Maintenance: Of Everything," which argues maintenance is simultaneously necessary and optional — we can always put it off, but deferring too long invites disaster. Cheng traces why societies undervalue maintenance: we prize invention over care, discount what isn't immediately gratifying, and associate maintenance work with low-status labor. These cultural attitudes create a tension with AI agents, which are marketed as liberation from maintenance drudgery.

The deeper argument is that maintenance isn't just a chore to be eliminated — it's a form of knowledge. Cheng's "Digital Mending Circles," running since 2023, reflect the idea that tending to digital mess together teaches us about the systems we inhabit. When we delegate maintenance to AI, we risk losing that understanding. The piece references COBOL Cowboys (aging programmers who maintain critical government systems) and civic technologists watching Claude Code attempt to modernize benefits systems — a vivid illustration that delegating maintenance to AI doesn't eliminate the need to understand the systems being maintained.

The paradox Cheng lands on: AI agents promise to free us from maintenance, but maintaining the agents themselves becomes a new category of maintenance work. For tinkerers, this meta-maintenance might even be satisfying — part of the appeal. For everyone else, it's just another chore. The essay suggests that the real value of digital maintenance lies not in completing the tasks but in the literacy and understanding gained through struggling with systems.
