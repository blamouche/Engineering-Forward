# The 18-month Career Is Here
**Source**: https://every.to/working-overtime/the-18-month-career-is-here
**Date**: 2025-07-29
**Author**: Katie Parrott
**Keywords**: prompt engineering, bridge jobs, AI careers, career obsolescence, AI economy, portfolio careers, job displacement, skills evolution

## Elevator pitch
AI specialties like prompt engineering now rise and fall within 18 months, and the sustainable career strategy is to focus on persistent problems and develop taste rather than chase transient technical skills.

## Takeaways
- Prompt engineering went from $200K jobs to "already obsolete" in under 18 months — the full lifecycle of a bridge job compressed from decades to months
- Bridge jobs appear when new technology needs human translators, are essential at first, then get absorbed once the interface improves
- AI doesn't just replace individual tools; it makes entire skill sets evolve in real time, with tools you build becoming irrelevant within a quarter
- The sustainable approach: follow friction (automate what's tedious), build taste (develop judgment for quality outputs), and bring your baggage (domain knowledge doesn't expire as fast as technical skills)
- Skills declared "dead" can resurface as more valuable than the replacements everyone assumed would supersede them

## Synthesis
The prompt engineering career arc tells the story of AI's acceleration of everything. In 2023, the Wall Street Journal wrote about prompt engineering roles paying $200,000. By spring 2025, the same publication declared the job "already obsolete." The full lifecycle of a bridge job — roles that appear when new technology needs human translators before the interface gets easier — once took decades for typists, years for webmasters. With AI, it compressed to 18 months.

Katie Parrott, now working as an AI operations lead at Every, lives this reality daily. She built a custom AI proofreader for the company, only to watch CEO Dan Shipper add the company style guide as a skill in Dia, the AI-powered browser, making her tool potentially redundant overnight. Every morning, she checks what models improved overnight, what workflows coworkers built, and which of her systems have already been made irrelevant.

The pattern extends beyond tools to entire skill sets. The traditional career model assumes roles remain stable long enough to build expertise. AI breaks that assumption. Michael Taylor, who literally wrote the book on prompt engineering, bets he has about five years before the role disappears entirely — yet he also notes that prompt optimization is proving more important than fine-tuning for delivering results, suggesting declared-dead skills can resurface more valuable than their supposed replacements.

Parrott's three principles for navigating this landscape are practical and tested. Follow the friction — automate what feels tedious because if it's tedious for you, it's tedious for others. Build taste, not just tools — anyone can learn to use Claude, but fewer develop the judgment to know when output is good or how to improve it. Bring your baggage — domain knowledge from "non-technical" backgrounds doesn't expire as fast as technical skills. The bridge you're standing on may be burning, but that vantage point lets you spot where the next one should go.
