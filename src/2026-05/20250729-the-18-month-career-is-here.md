# The 18-month Career Is Here
**Source**: https://every.to/working-overtime/the-18-month-career-is-here
**Date**: 2025-07-29
**Author**: Katie Parrott
**Keywords**: prompt engineering, bridge jobs, career obsolescence, AI economy, portfolio careers, skills evolution, job displacement, career strategy

## Elevator pitch
Katie Parrott examines how AI specialties rise and fall within 18 months — using prompt engineering as the case study — and argues that the sustainable career strategy is to focus on persistent problems, build taste rather than tools, and bring domain expertise that outlasts any specific technical skill.

## Takeaways
- Prompt engineering went from $200K salaries to "already obsolete" in roughly 18 months — but the skill itself may be more valuable now than when it commanded dedicated job titles
- "Bridge jobs" are roles that emerge when new technology needs human translators; they're essential at first but get absorbed once interfaces mature (typists, webmasters, prompt engineers)
- Michael Taylor, who wrote the essential book on prompt engineering, gives his own role about five years before it disappears — and is building his work around problems that persist regardless of tooling
- Three principles for navigating rapid obsolescence: follow the friction (automate what's tedious), build taste (judgment outlasts tool proficiency), and bring your baggage (domain expertise from "non-technical" backgrounds is a durable advantage)
- The traditional career ladder model assumes stable roles and skills; the new model requires drawing the map as you go through open territory

## Synthesis
Katie Parrott's essay captures the whiplash of careers in the AI economy through the perfect case study: prompt engineering. In early 2023, the Wall Street Journal was writing about $200,000 prompt engineering jobs. By spring 2024, the same publication declared the role "already obsolete." The entire arc — emergence, hype, absorption into the general skill stack, declared obsolescence — compressed into 18 months, a speed that would have been unthinkable for previous technology transitions.

Parrott uses Michael Taylor as her central character, and it's a good choice. Taylor literally wrote the book on prompt engineering, writes a column about it for Every, and says it's "most of the work" at his AI startup. He also thinks he has about five years before the job disappears. This is not fatalism; it's strategic clarity. Taylor understands his role as a bridge job — the kind of position that emerges when a new technology needs human translators and gets absorbed once the interface matures. Typists had decades. Webmasters had years. Prompt engineers got 18 months. The acceleration is the story.

The bridge job concept is the essay's most useful analytical tool. These roles are essential during technology transitions — someone has to translate between what the system can do and what humans need it to do. But their very existence signals an immaturity in the interface. Once the technology becomes intuitive enough, the translation layer collapses into the general skillset. The pattern has repeated throughout technological history, but AI compresses the timeline so dramatically that building identity around specific technical skills becomes actively risky.

Parrott's three principles for navigating this environment are drawn from her own experience. "Follow the friction" comes from her work building an AI proofreader for Every — the tedious, repetitive work you notice is probably tedious for others too, and solving it creates real value. "Build taste, not just tools" is a recurring theme across Every's writing: anyone can learn to prompt, but fewer can judge when the output is good or how to improve it. "Bring your baggage" is the most personally resonant: Taylor brought marketing instincts to AI, Parrott brought editorial standards to operations. Domain expertise from so-called "non-technical" backgrounds doesn't expire as fast as specific tool proficiency — and may be exactly what makes someone valuable in an AI-augmented role.

The closing insight is that constant change creates its own advantage: the person on a burning bridge has the best vantage point for spotting where the next one should go. If you can see the gaps before others, you get to help design what fills them.
