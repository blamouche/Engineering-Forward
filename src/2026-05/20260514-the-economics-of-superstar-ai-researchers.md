# The economics of superstar AI researchers
**Source**: https://epochai.substack.com/p/the-economics-of-superstar-ai-researchers
**Date**: May 13, 2026
**Author**: Anson Ho (Epoch AI)
**Keywords**: superstar effect, AI researcher compensation, economics of talent, AI race dynamics, Sherwin Rosen, meta $100M salaries, researcher quality, intelligence explosion, nonrivalry

## Elevator pitch
The enormous pay gap between top AI researchers and the rest isn't just about being 10x better — it's driven by the "superstar effect" where small quality differences compound across billion-user markets, amplified by winner-take-all race dynamics.

## Takeaways
- Top AI researchers earn 10-100x more than median researchers, with Meta allegedly offering $100M compensation packages to poach from OpenAI
- The superstar effect requires two conditions: one person's work can reach a huge market (AI models serve billions), and quantity of labor can't easily substitute for quality (compute constraints limit experiments)
- Even a moderately superior researcher (2x quality) could justify enormous pay because their contribution scales to every user simultaneously
- Race dynamics amplify the effect: labs competing for a potential multi-trillion-dollar outcome adopt "all in or nothing" approaches, paying heavily for any edge
- The superstar premium is likely to increase as AI adoption grows and research shifts toward managing armies of AI agents, where experienced research managers see compounding productivity gains

## Synthesis
Anson Ho's May 13, 2026 essay for Epoch AI applies the economic concept of the "superstar effect" to explain the staggering compensation disparity among AI researchers. The piece challenges the simplistic explanation that top researchers are simply vastly superior, arguing instead that even small quality differences produce enormous pay gaps when scaled across global markets and amplified by competitive race dynamics.

Ho opens with striking compensation data: top researchers at frontier labs earning over ten times the median million-dollar salary, and potentially over a hundred times what an average AI postdoc earns. Meta's alleged $100 million compensation packages to poach researchers from OpenAI anchor the upper bound. The question isn't whether these researchers are talented — they clearly are — but whether the pay multiples reflect proportional quality differences or something structural.

The answer lies in economist Sherwin Rosen's "superstar effect," which kicks in when two conditions hold. First, one person's work can reach a huge market: AI models serve nearly a billion ChatGPT users, so a single researcher's contribution scales to every user simultaneously. Second, quantity of labor doesn't easily substitute for quality: frontier labs are compute-constrained and can only run limited large-scale experiments, so two "merely very good" researchers can't replicate one researcher with deep intuition about which experiments are worth running.

Ho uses accessible analogies: Usain Bolt's gold medal in the 2012 100m sprint earned vastly more reward than the silver medalist who finished 0.12 seconds behind; Taylor Swift earned $60-70M from Spotify while comparably popular artists earned $5-25M. In both cases, tiny quality edges compound across enormous audiences — the same dynamic that makes a "2x researcher" potentially worth far more than 2x the median salary when their work reaches a billion users.

Race dynamics amplify the effect. Frontier labs are competing for a prize potentially worth tens of trillions of dollars annually if they achieve transformative AI. This incentivizes an "all in or nothing" approach where anything improving the odds even slightly is worth enormous sums. Ho draws a parallel to high-frequency trading, where firms spend heavily to execute milliseconds faster despite near-zero social benefit.

The essay wrestles with real complications: top researchers carry trade secrets in their heads from expensive experiments competitors haven't run, many manage teams beyond pure research contributions, and measuring "researcher quality" quantitatively remains nearly impossible. But the core insight is practical: if the 100x pay gap reflects a 100x researcher quality gap, that has implications for modeling an intelligence explosion. If it mostly reflects the superstar dynamic, the quality gap is smaller — and the effect will only intensify as AI adoption grows and research shifts toward managing autonomous AI agents.

Ho concludes that $100M annual compensation may soon seem modest. As billions more people use AI more heavily, and as experienced research managers see their productivity compounded by managing AI armies, the superstar premium could reach even more extreme levels.
