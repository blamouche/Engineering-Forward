# AI's Public Relations Emergency
**Source**: https://www.bigtechnology.com/p/ais-public-relations-emergency
**Date**: May 22, 2026
**Author**: Alex Kantrowitz
**Keywords**: AI public perception, commencement protests, data center opposition, techlash, job displacement, AI messaging, Gallup polling

## Elevator pitch
Alex Kantrowitz warns that AI faces a public relations emergency as college graduates boo AI mentions at commencements, seven in ten Americans oppose data centers in their area, and the industry's own leaders have spent years warning about mass job loss — all while marching toward trillion-dollar IPOs.

## Takeaways
- College graduates across multiple US universities have booed or jeered mentions of AI at commencement ceremonies
- AI leaders have spent years publicly warning about mass job loss, focusing on entry-level roles — while their companies head toward IPOs
- Marc Andreessen told Joe Rogan's audience AI is great because "bots never get frustrated with you" and "never file HR complaints" — messaging unlikely to land with unemployed grads
- Gallup: 70% of Americans oppose AI data centers in their area, with 48% strongly opposed and only 7% strongly in favor
- Unlike the 2018 "techlash" against Amazon/Facebook/Google, AI polls below all major political candidates, lowering the political cost of restrictive action
- A Maine data center moratorium was only stopped by a governor's veto; 2028 presidential races could feature data center bans

## Synthesis
Alex Kantrowitz frames AI's current public perception as an emergency with real political consequences. The evidence is stark: at commencements across the US, graduates have booed AI mentions at the University of Central Florida, jeered at Middle Tennessee State University, and drowned out ex-Google CEO Eric Schmidt at the University of Arizona as he encouraged them to help steer the technology's future.

Kantrowitz argues the industry's own messaging is partly to blame. AI leaders have spent years warning about mass job loss — Anthropic's Dario Amodei, Microsoft AI chief Mustafa Suleyman, and OpenAI's Sam Altman have all publicly predicted white-collar displacement, often focusing on the entry-level roles graduates want. These warnings come from executives whose companies are simultaneously racing toward trillion-dollar IPOs, creating a credibility gap that commencement booing makes audible.

The messaging hasn't helped. Marc Andreessen's recent appearance on Joe Rogan's podcast — where he praised AI because "the bots never get frustrated with you" and "never file HR complaints" — exemplifies the disconnect. Whether or not Andreessen is right about AI creating jobs, this framing is tone-deaf to unemployed graduates on their parents' couches.

The infrastructure consequences are already materializing. Gallup finds 70% of Americans oppose AI data centers in their area, with 48% strongly opposed. In Maine, a data center construction moratorium was stopped only by a governor's veto. Kantrowitz notes a crucial difference from the 2018 "techlash": back then, tech companies polled better than politicians, providing political cover. Today, AI polls below all major candidates, lowering the political cost of restrictive action. The 2028 presidential cycle could feature data center bans and government AI "kill switches" — impractical but politically potent proposals. The stakes are high: AI's expansion depends on public consent for the physical infrastructure it requires.
