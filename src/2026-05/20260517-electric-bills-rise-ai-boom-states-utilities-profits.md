# As electric bills rise in the AI boom, states take aim at utilities' profits
**Source**: https://www.latimes.com/world-nation/story/2026-05-17/as-electric-bills-rise-in-ai-boom-states-take-aim-at-utilities-profits
**Date**: May 17, 2026
**Author**: Marc Levy (Associated Press)
**Keywords**: AI energy demand, utility profits, electricity rates, data centers, state regulation, grid infrastructure, affordability, Arizona, Pennsylvania, Indiana, consumer advocacy

## Elevator pitch
Soaring electricity demand from AI data centers is driving record utility profits and higher consumer bills, sparking a bipartisan political backlash across at least six states as governors, attorneys general, and regulators challenge the traditional monopoly utility profit model.

## Takeaways
1. AI data center energy demand is fueling a moneymaking construction boom for utilities, with for-profit utility profits rising from $39B (2021) to over $52B (2024).
2. At least six states — Arizona, Indiana, Maryland, New Jersey, New York, and Pennsylvania — are taking unprecedented steps to block rate increases and challenge utility profit guarantees.
3. A former utility executive turned consumer advocate estimates ~10% of the typical customer bill represents "excess profit" above what's reasonable under Supreme Court precedent.
4. Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro declared the "20th century utility model is broken," pressuring PECO to withdraw a 12.5% rate increase, while Arizona's AG is challenging two 14% increases.
5. Wall Street is taking notice — utility share prices lagged after state challenges, and "affordability" has become the #1 issue on utility earnings calls, even as utilities warn that squeezing returns threatens grid reliability.

## Synthesis
Marc Levy's Associated Press report, published in the Los Angeles Times, documents a growing political firestorm at the intersection of the AI boom and consumer electricity prices. As AI data centers drive unprecedented demand for power, for-profit utilities are posting record profits — rising from just under $39 billion in 2021 to over $52 billion in 2024 across 110 investor-owned utilities, according to the Energy and Policy Institute. Meanwhile, households are seeing their bills climb, creating a potent political issue in a midterm election year.

The backlash is remarkably bipartisan. In Pennsylvania, Democratic Governor Josh Shapiro pressured PECO (an Exelon subsidiary) to withdraw a 12.5% rate increase that would have added roughly $20 per month for the average residential customer. Shapiro then issued a letter to utility executives declaring the "20th century utility model is broken" and arguing that "we can no longer simply prioritize corporate profitability to drive infrastructure development." One Wall Street analyst dubbed it "Quaker State Sticker Shock" as utility stocks lagged. Meanwhile, in Indiana, Republican Governor Mike Braun appointed a new slate of utility commissioners specifically tasked with facing down rate increases, with their first test being AES Indiana's request for a 10.1% increase.

Arizona Attorney General Kris Mayes, a Democrat seeking reelection, is challenging two proposed 14% rate increases before the state's regulatory board. "It's becoming unbearable for the people in Arizona," Mayes said. "And I think we have to fight back." In New Jersey, the Board of Public Utilities launched what its president called one of the most consequential regulatory reviews in a generation to question how utilities should earn revenue in a modern energy system.

The core tension is structural. Utilities have long operated as regulated monopolies with guaranteed returns — a model designed to ensure stable infrastructure investment. But the AI boom's voracious energy appetite is exposing cracks in that compact. Consumer advocates argue that about 10% of the typical bill represents "excess profit," and some are calling for utilities to shop for the lowest-cost investor capital rather than receiving regulator-set returns. Utilities respond that squeezing returns will drive capital to other states and threaten grid reliability.

The political dimension compounds the economic one. With affordability emerging as a leading midterm theme, the issue has traction beyond traditional consumer advocacy circles. Utility executives are now emphasizing cost-cutting on earnings calls, and "affordability" tops the list of investor concerns. Yet the underlying demand from AI isn't going away — California regulators notably rejected consumer demands to curb profits in December 2025, and the battle lines are only hardening. The outcome of these state-level fights may determine whether the costs of the AI boom's energy appetite are borne by utility shareholders, ratepayers, or some negotiated middle ground.
