# I Hired ChatGPT as My Career Coach
**Source**: https://every.to/working-overtime/i-hired-chatgpt-as-my-career-coach
**Date**: 2024-10-07
**Author**: Katie Parrott
**Keywords**: ChatGPT, career coaching, AI coaching, career development, AI as therapist, self-reflection, prompt engineering, personal development

## Elevator pitch
Katie Parrott used ChatGPT as a free career coach when she couldn't afford a human one, discovering it was a powerful mirror for structured self-reflection but ultimately couldn't replace the deeply human work of deciding and owning your career path.

## Takeaways
- ChatGPT met expectations for originality and empathy but needed improvement on accountability — it was a "yes bot" that required explicit instructions to challenge assumptions
- The AI excelled at structure when given specific frameworks (like positioning statements) rather than vague open-ended questions
- Better prompting forced clearer thinking — the act of structuring queries for AI trained the user as much as the model
- The real value wasn't in prescriptive answers but in providing a space to externalize thinking instead of running anxious internal loops
- Clarity didn't arrive gift-wrapped from the AI; it had to be dug out question by question, prompt by prompt

## Synthesis
When Katie Parrott's therapist suggested hiring a career coach, she faced a familiar problem: $100-150 per hour wasn't in the budget after losing her job. So she did what any resourceful AI skeptic might do — she opened ChatGPT, fed it custom instructions to act as a career coach with expertise in content marketing, tech, and startups, and began what became a month-long experiment in AI-guided career development.

She evaluated CareerGPT across five dimensions. Originality was adequate — the intake survey questions ("What kind of work energizes you?") weren't groundbreaking, but they prompted genuine self-reflection she hadn't been doing on her own. Accountability was the weakest area: ChatGPT couldn't send push notifications, didn't care about missed sessions, and defaulted to enthusiastic support for every pivot. Only when she explicitly told it to stop being a "yes bot" did it begin challenging her.

Structure was where the AI excelled, but only when she met it halfway. Vague open-ended questions generated noise; specific frameworks like positioning statements produced useful output. The surprising meta-lesson was that better prompting forced her to think more clearly about what she actually wanted. Empathy was surprisingly adequate — ChatGPT could deliver effective pep talks and reference her personal history in ways that felt tailored. Clarity was the hardest dimension: the AI didn't provide a perfect life path but served as a mirror that helped her see her own thinking more clearly.

The experiment's real outcome was practical: over the month, Parrott updated her résumé, revived her newsletter, reconnected with colleagues, and eventually landed her current job. But the core insight is that AI career coaching works best not as a source of answers but as infrastructure for the work of self-examination that career development always requires.
