# China, AI, and the Battle for Mythos: How Anthropic's Most Powerful Model Became a Geopolitical Flashpoint
**Source**: https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/12/us/politics/china-ai-anthropic-openai-mythos-chatgpt.html
**Date**: May 12, 2026
**Author**: The New York Times
**Keywords**: Anthropic, Mythos, China, US-China AI competition, Project Glasswing, cybersecurity, zero-day, Singapore, Carnegie Endowment, Trump administration, AI regulation

## Elevator pitch
A Chinese think tank's informal request for access to Anthropic's Claude Mythos model at a Singapore meeting triggered White House alarm, crystallizing the intensifying US-China AI arms race around a model whose zero-day discovery capabilities are classified as "digital weapon-grade" technology.

## Takeaways
- At a closed-door Carnegie Endowment meeting in Singapore (April 2026), a Chinese think tank representative informally asked Anthropic to grant access to Mythos — the request was refused on the spot but triggered high alert within the US National Security Council.
- Mythos, released under Project Glasswing in April 2026, autonomously discovered thousands of zero-day vulnerabilities across all major OS and browsers during testing, with some flaws dating back 27 years; access is limited to ~40 US/UK institutions.
- China is explicitly excluded as an "adversarial nation," yet much of its critical infrastructure (banks, energy, government) runs on the same software where Mythos identified vulnerabilities — leaving China without access to defense capabilities it arguably needs.
- The incident coincides with internal Trump administration debates on pre-release AI model security assessments (akin to FDA drug review) and Trump's scheduled visit to China this week.
- China's cybersecurity market is projected to grow from 1.58 billion yuan in 2025 to 59.35 billion yuan by 2030 (37x growth), but domestic models remain "far from Mythos" in capability.

## Synthesis
The New York Times reported on a seemingly small interaction with outsized geopolitical implications: during a break at a Carnegie Endowment meeting in Singapore, a Chinese think tank representative approached Anthropic officials and requested access to Claude Mythos, the company's most capable AI model. The refusal was immediate, but the incident's journey to the White House National Security Council transformed it into a case study of the US-China AI competition's current dynamics.

Mythos is genuinely exceptional. Released in April 2026 under the Project Glasswing cybersecurity initiative, it autonomously discovered thousands of zero-day vulnerabilities across every major operating system and browser, including flaws that had persisted for up to 27 years. Its SWE-bench validation score of 93.9% dramatically exceeds Opus 4.6's 80.8%. Access is confined to approximately 40 US and UK institutions — Amazon, Apple, Microsoft, CrowdStrike, Cisco, Nvidia, JPMorgan Chase, and the Linux Foundation among them — under a framework that explicitly excludes China.

The practical dilemma is sharp: Chinese banks, energy companies, and government agencies run the same underlying software where Mythos found vulnerabilities, but China has no seat at the defense table. The Chinese cybersecurity industry's reaction has been mixed — official restraint combined with market exuberance (stocks of Qi An Xin, Sangfor, and 360 Security rose for days after Mythos's release) and some domestic skepticism that Anthropic is using security concerns as a marketing gimmick.

The timing is politically charged. Trump administration officials are debating an executive order for pre-release AI model security assessments, with the NSA pushing for intelligence agency review while Commerce wants to retain authority. Trump's visit to China this week puts AI on the bilateral agenda, though Atlantic Council analysts caution that past US-China AI safety dialogues saw China primarily collecting information rather than engaging substantively. The incident crystallizes a core tension: the US treats frontier AI models as strategic assets to be protected from adversaries, while global software interdependence means exclusion creates asymmetric vulnerability.
