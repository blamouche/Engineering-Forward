## Elevator pitch
Opus 4.8 tops both Every's Senior Engineer Benchmark (63/100, edging GPT-5.5's 62) and their writing tests (79.6, the highest score any model has achieved), making it Anthropic's most complete model yet — but output quality is heavily effort-dependent and the Claude app still lags behind OpenAI's Codex harness.

## Takeaways
- Opus 4.8 scored 63 on Every's Senior Engineer Benchmark versus 62 for GPT-5.5 and 33.5 for Opus 4.7, and 79.6 on writing tests — the highest score any model has hit, with fewer AI tells than any non-Claude model
- It produced the best one-shot PowerPoint presentation on Every's enterprise consulting benchmark: a crafted, well-designed deck that effectively told a story, something most models still cannot do
- Output quality is heavily dependent on effort level: Opus 4.8 at extra-high is a competitive senior engineer, while at high it's merely adequate; at high it delivers clean expressive prose, while at medium it succumbs to AI's worst writing tendencies
- The model is more honest than its predecessor: more likely to flag uncertainties about its work and less likely to make unsupported claims, with a new mid-conversation system message feature for updating instructions in long-running conversations
- The catch: the Claude Desktop app still lags behind OpenAI's Codex, which remains a far better harness — making GPT-5.5 in Codex the daily driver for some Every team members despite Opus 4.8's superior raw model quality
- Dan Shipper and Katie Parrott argue the model could have been called Opus 5 without anyone blinking, and that calling it 4.8 undersells the jump from 4.7

## Synthesis
Every's vibe check on Claude Opus 4.8 finds it to be Anthropic's most complete model yet, topping both their coding and writing benchmarks. On the Senior Engineer Benchmark — a first-principles rewrite of a production codebase — Opus 4.8 scored 63/100 at extra-high effort, narrowly beating GPT-5.5's 62 and dramatically surpassing Opus 4.7's 33.5. On writing tests it scored 79.6, the highest any model has achieved, with fewer AI tells than any non-Claude model. It also produced the best one-shot PowerPoint on their enterprise consulting benchmark: a deck that was crafted, well-designed, and told a coherent story.

The model represents a recovery for Anthropic. Opus 4.7 was hard to use and hard to love, and even Every's most devoted Claude users found themselves reaching for GPT models. Opus 4.8 reverses that. Dan Shipper and Katie Parrott argue it is very hard to make a model that is both an incredible software engineer and a near-human writer with depth and emotional intelligence, but that is what this model feels like. They contend Anthropic could have called it Opus 5 and no one would have blinked — calling it 4.8 undersells the jump.

A critical caveat is the effort-level dependency. Opus 4.8 at extra-high is a competitive senior engineer; at high it is merely adequate. At high it delivers mostly clean, expressive prose; at medium it succumbs to AI's worst writing tendencies. This means users must explicitly set effort levels for different task types, and the difference between a good and a disappointing result often comes down to this single parameter rather than prompt engineering. The model is also more honest than its predecessor — more likely to flag uncertainties and less likely to make unsupported claims — and supports mid-conversation system messages, allowing users to append updated instructions in long-running conversations without restating the full system prompt.

The second catch is the app. Despite the model's quality, the Claude Desktop app still trails OpenAI's Codex as a harness. Some Every team members continue to use GPT-5.5 in Codex as their daily driver because the harness matters as much as the model. This creates an interesting split: Opus 4.8 is the better model, but Codex is the better workspace. The practical implication is that model quality alone no longer determines daily driver choice — the surrounding application, tooling, and workflow integration are now equally decisive factors.
