# After the Personal Agent
**Source**: https://every.to/context-window/after-the-personal-agent
**Date**: 2026-05-17
**Author**: Every Staff
**Keywords**: AI agents, personal agents, tacit knowledge, Opus 4.7, context mining, long-horizon agents, workplace AI

## Elevator pitch
Every's newsletter roundup argues the era of personal AI agents is giving way to shared, team-based agents that are more reliable, while also highlighting the growing value of tacit knowledge extraction and the reality check on long-horizon agent benchmarks.

## Takeaways
- Personal AI agents shipped widely at Every (via Plus One/OpenClaw) but proved unreliable and maintenance-heavy, prompting a pivot to shared team agents
- Tacit knowledge — the insights not on the internet — is becoming more valuable as AI commoditizes searchable information
- METR's benchmarks show Anthropic's Mythos reaching 16+ hour autonomous task horizons at 50% reliability, but only ~3 hours at 80% — both narratives are technically true
- Opus 4.7 is winning back users from Codex, with some team members making it their default for synchronous work
- A practical workflow mines scattered thinking from Slack, Notion, voice memos, and transcripts before drafting to avoid blank-page paralysis

## Synthesis
The May 17 edition of Every's Context Window newsletter serves as a state-of-the-union for workplace AI circa mid-2026, weaving together product retrospectives, benchmark analysis, and emerging best practices. The headline theme — "After the Personal Agent" — captures a significant pivot at Every itself. After launching Plus One, a hosted version of the open-source OpenClaw agent harness, to every employee, the team found that the one-agent-per-person model created more frustration than productivity. Agents constantly broke, claimed they lacked access to tools they were in fact connected to, and required constant individual maintenance. The takeaway: shared, role-defined team agents with centralized upkeep outperform a fleet of personalized but unreliable individual assistants. This is being rebuilt as Plus One 2.0.

The newsletter also surfaces a counterintuitive insight from Eleanor Warnock: as AI makes everything searchable, the most valuable knowledge becomes what isn't easily searched — tacit knowledge, the kind extracted through skilled interviewing and human judgment. Warnock's "Socrates as a Service" framing positions the human questioner as more essential than ever, not less.

On the benchmarking front, Katie Parrott dissects METR's latest data on long-horizon agent reliability. The viral chart showing Anthropic's Mythos model breaking past 16-hour autonomous task completion scales tells one story; the 80% reliability chart showing ~3-hour effective horizons tells another. Both are true, and Parrott's analysis helps readers understand which version to trust depending on their use case. The practical takeaway: measure your own longest trusted agent run and extend it deliberately with goal-based commands now shipping in both Codex and Claude Code.

Laura Entis contributes a practical workflow for "mining your life for context" — connecting AI agents to the distributed traces of your thinking across Slack, Notion, voice memos, and meeting transcripts to assemble drafts from material you've already produced, rather than starting from scratch. This pairs with a profile of Noah Brier's use of Claude Code as a "second brain" connected to thousands of personal notes.

Finally, the team notes that Opus 4.7 is recapturing users who had migrated to Codex, with Cora GM Kieran Klaassen making it his default for synchronous work. The broader pattern: the agent landscape is rapidly consolidating around shared infrastructure (Claude Managed Agents, upcoming OpenAI equivalents) while the frontier of value shifts toward skills, workflows, and judgment layered on top.
