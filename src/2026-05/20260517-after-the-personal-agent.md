# After the Personal Agent
**Source**: https://every.to/context-window/after-the-personal-agent
**Date**: 2026-05-17
**Author**: Every Staff (Context Window)
**Keywords**: personal AI agents, agent reliability, tacit knowledge, Opus 4.7, Plus One, agent maintenance, shared agents

## Elevator pitch
Every's Context Window newsletter explores why personal AI agents for every employee failed at scale, and why the future belongs to shared, reliable agent coworkers rather than one-per-person bot armies.

## Takeaways
- Every's experiment giving every employee a personal AI agent (Plus One) failed: agents were unreliable, constantly broke, and required too much upkeep — the problem was the "personal agent" model itself
- The rebuild (Plus One 2.0) shifts to shared, reliable coworkers — specialized agents that serve teams rather than individuals, reducing maintenance overhead
- Eleanor Warnock argues that tacit knowledge — the gold dust that isn't online — is becoming more valuable as AI makes searchable information ubiquitous; seven techniques for extracting it from people
- Several Every team members are switching back to Opus 4.7 after weeks of Codex dominance, especially for synchronous work
- The broader insight: personal agents created a maintenance tax that didn't scale — shared infrastructure agents with clear ownership and reliability SLAs are the emerging pattern

## Synthesis
The "After the Personal Agent" issue of Context Window is a postmortem that doubles as a thesis correction. Every's internal experiment — giving every employee an AI agent through their Plus One harness — produced a clear result: personal agents don't work at scale, at least not yet. The problem wasn't the technology stack (OpenClaw); it was the model of one agent per person. Agents needed constant maintenance, broke unpredictably, and created overhead that erased their productivity gains. This is an important data point for the industry, which has been racing toward personal AI assistants as the obvious deployment model.

The shift to Plus One 2.0 — shared, reliable "coworker" agents that serve teams — suggests a more pragmatic path. Instead of 50 fragile personal agents, you maintain 5 robust shared agents with clear ownership and reliability expectations. This mirrors the evolution of internal tools: you don't give every employee their own Jenkins server; you maintain shared CI infrastructure. The same logic applies to agents.

Warnock's piece on tacit knowledge extraction is a complementary thread: as AI makes codified information trivially accessible, the value shifts to what isn't written down. Her seven techniques for surfacing implicit knowledge from interviews and conversations position human judgment and interpersonal skill as the durable differentiator in an AI-saturated world. The Opus 4.7 note — that team members are voluntarily switching back after weeks of Codex — is a reminder that model preference is contextual and personal, not a one-dimensional leaderboard race.
