# The Unsustainable Subsidy: AI Model Pricing Trends
**Source**: https://tomtunguz.com/ai-model-inflation/
**Date**: 2026-05-20
**Author**: Tomasz Tunguz (Theory Ventures)
**Keywords**: AI pricing, Google Gemini, OpenAI GPT, Anthropic Claude, model economics, capex, subsidies

## Elevator pitch
VC Tomasz Tunguz analyzes the pricing trajectories of Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic's flagship models, revealing that Google's AI prices triple each year while OpenAI's were subsidized before rising — and with all three vendors under record capex pressure, the era of artificially cheap frontier AI may be ending as margins become the priority.

## Takeaways
- Google Gemini 3.1 Pro pricing has tripled year-over-year to $2/$12 per million input/output tokens, though it remains the cheapest at less than half of competitors
- OpenAI GPT-5.5 is priced at $5/$30 per million tokens — after a period of apparent subsidy when prices were lower
- Anthropic Claude Opus 4.7 has maintained stable/luxe pricing at $5/$25, with some decreases for most powerful models
- All three vendors are under record capex spending pressure, shifting strategy from growth/share to margins
- The pricing divergence reflects different strategic phases: cuts when cash is plentiful and market share matters, increases when margins matter

## Synthesis
Tomasz Tunguz's brief but data-rich analysis captures a pivotal moment in AI economics. Using pricing data for the three major frontier model providers, he identifies a clear pattern: AI model pricing is inflating — and fast. Google's prices have tripled annually, OpenAI raised prices after a period of apparent subsidy, and even Anthropic, which maintained stable luxury pricing, is operating in an environment where the era of cheap frontier AI appears to be ending.

The raw numbers tell the story: Google Gemini 3.1 Pro at $2/$12 per million tokens (input/output), Anthropic Opus 4.7 at $5/$25, and OpenAI GPT-5.5 at $5/$30. Google remains the cost leader, but even its prices are climbing rapidly. The chart data suggests OpenAI had been subsidizing its flagship model for a period — keeping prices artificially low to win market share — before raising them as the financial reality of record capex spending set in.

The strategic implications are significant. If frontier AI pricing is structurally increasing rather than decreasing (as the commodity narrative would predict), it validates the premium-pricing thesis that underlies OpenAI and Anthropic's IPO valuations — at least in the short term. It also conflicts with the narrative from the CNBC piece (processed separately) about cheap Chinese alternatives undercutting pricing. The resolution may be that both trends coexist: frontier models get more expensive as they get more capable, while cheaper alternatives capture the volume market.

Tunguz's framing of "the unsustainable subsidy" is apt. The AI industry has been running on venture capital and strategic investment that kept prices artificially low. As public market discipline approaches (IPOs) and capex demands grow (SpaceX $45B deal, new data centers), the industry may be entering a period of price discovery where the true cost of frontier AI becomes visible for the first time.
