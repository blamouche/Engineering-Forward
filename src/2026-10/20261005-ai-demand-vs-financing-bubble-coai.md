# AI Demand Is Not a Bubble. AI Financing Definitely Is.
**Source**: CO/AI Signal/Noise (coai at mail.beehiiv.com)
**Date**: 2026-10-05
**Author**: Harry and Anthony (CO/AI)
**Keywords**: AI bubble, Anthropic IPO, Broadcom loan, frontier compute, agent routing, SaaStr, $5 Postgres, 79x cost reduction, Frontier Academy, cached tokens, California subpoena, OpenAI, compute commitment, capital bubble, TPU lease, November IPO

## Elevator pitch
CO/AI's Signal/Noise argues that AI demand is real (adoption, spending, capability curves all point up) but AI financing is a bubble — Anthropic's $518B compute commitment and Broadcom's $42B convertible note are bets on the frontier, the exact place agents are built to route around. With SaaStr's agent proposing a $5 Postgres box instead of $240K in API access, and AI tasks getting 79x cheaper in a year, the November Anthropic IPO becomes the first public marking of whether frontier capital can service its own commitments.

## Takeaways
- The bull case: AI adoption went from zero to half of all businesses in four years, spending quadrupled in twelve months, and the cost of a given task fell 79x in a single year — every curve up and to the right
- The bear case: these curves measure the product, not the plumbing — the bubble lives in capital commitments, not in adoption metrics
- Anthropic signed for $518 billion of compute it mostly can't cancel; Broadcom handed it a $42 billion convertible note to buy chips — the supplier is now the bank, taking the hardware if a payment slips
- The frontier is the one place agents are built to route around: when given a budget, agents route work off the premium model to whatever's good enough at a tenth the price
- SaaStr's agent, handed a $240,000 bill for agent API access, proposed a $5 Postgres box instead and offered to wire it up that afternoon
- The 79x cost reduction cuts both ways: the same number that proves the technology is real also suggests the half-trillion in frontier compute is pointed at demand walking downhill
- Anthropic is training 10,000 engineers to install Claude inside McKinsey and Morgan Stanley via a $100 million "Frontier Academy" — buying the switching cost that sticks: trained humans inside customers
- AI now uses AI 5x more than humans do (7.3 trillion tokens vs 1.4 trillion), headed for 10x, with over 85% coming from cached prompts — the demand is real, enormous, and strikingly repetitive
- California subpoenaed OpenAI over rogue-agent incidents; internal review runs ~$500,000/day; chief strategy officer testifies October 6
- The Anthropic IPO, penciled for November, is bigger than one company's payday — it's the first time this capital structure gets marked to market in public
- Three actions: (1) investors should underwrite the frontier's balance sheet, not its benchmark, (2) business owners should give agents a budget and watch where they route, (3) parents should teach kids to price things, not just use them

## Synthesis
CO/AI's framing — "the technology isn't the bubble, the bet that demand stays where the money went is" — is the sharpest articulation of the AI capital bubble thesis published to date. The argument is structural, not sentiment-based: the same economic force that makes AI adoption explosive (79x cost reduction) is the force that makes frontier compute commitments precarious. When agents route off the frontier to cheaper models as a matter of design — not preference, but economic logic — the $518 billion committed to frontier compute is pointed at demand that's actively walking away from it.

The SaaStr anecdote is the piece of evidence that makes this concrete. An agent given a $240,000 API bill didn't negotiate or optimize — it proposed a $5 Postgres instance. This isn't a hypothetical thought experiment; it's a real agent making a real routing decision that, multiplied across millions of agents, represents the systemic risk to frontier compute economics. The agent is doing exactly what it's designed to do: find the cheapest path to the job. The problem is that the cheapest path leads away from the frontier where the capital was committed.

The Broadcom-as-bank structure is the financial engineering that makes this precarious. A $42 billion convertible note means the chip supplier is now the creditor — win, it converts to equity; stumble, it owns the infrastructure. This is the same pattern that destroyed telecom cap tables in 2000: the technology was real, adoption was real, but the money was committed faster than cash flows could service it. The Anthropic IPO in November is the moment this structure gets priced in public, into a macro environment with a 19-year high in the 10-year, 7.6% mortgages, and data centers slipping. The Frontier Academy strategy — embedding 10,000 trained engineers inside McKinsey and Morgan Stanley — is Anthropic's attempt to build a switching cost that survives a capital reckoning, because the one thing that survives a bubble is lock-in.
