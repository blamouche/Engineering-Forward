# How to Get Better at AI by Asking AI
**Source**: Every (hello@every.to)
**Date**: 2026-10-02
**Author**: Arielle Shipper
**Keywords**: AI adoption, agent orchestration, Codex, subagents, eight levels of AI adoption, context packets, MCP, computer use, specialist agents, Katie Parrott

## Elevator pitch
An operations lead at Every describes how she leveled up from one-off chats to delegating whole projects to teams of specialist AI agents — by asking the AI itself to assess her work patterns and teach her the next level.

## Takeaways
- The "Eight Levels of AI Adoption" framework maps progression from basic chatbot use to full agent orchestration; the author placed at Level 5.5, with orchestration as the next step
- The key insight: ask your AI agent to assess how you've been working together across sessions, then turn that assessment into concrete tactical recommendations grounded in your actual past projects
- Moving from single-agent to multi-agent workflows means using one orchestrator thread as the "manager" that gives each specialist agent a context packet — the minimum background, source material, constraints, and success criteria needed to do its part without inheriting the entire project
- For high-stakes work, add skeptic and verifier agents whose only job is to challenge assumptions and ensure accuracy (e.g., checking for overlap in vesting schedules in case paperwork was incorrect)
- The author set up a recurring weekly task where Codex scans her sessions and sends a graded report on Friday afternoons, scoring her work against the eight levels and noting week-over-week changes

## Synthesis
Arielle Shipper, head of operations at Every, was prompted to change her AI usage by a tweet from Katie Parrott, who had asked Codex to assess her level on Mike Taylor and Laura Entis's "Eight Levels of AI Adoption" guide. The framework maps a progression from basic chatbot use (Level 1) to full agent orchestration (Level 6+), with each level delegating more work and trust to AI.

Shipper's approach was meta: she used AI as her own coach. First, she asked Codex to look across past sessions and level her — it placed her at 5.5. Then she asked for specific recommendations to reach Level 6 or 7, grounded in her actual work history (e.g., a Stripe API project where she could have divided the work among a data puller, a verifier, a workbook builder, and a tax-logic specialist). When she didn't understand the recommendations (schema mapper, context packets), she asked Codex to explain how to execute them practically — not just what to do, but how.

The orchestration model Codex described maps to how you'd run a project with human teammates: one lead (orchestrator) holds the business goal, constraints, deadline, and definition of done. It gives each specialist a context packet — the minimum context needed to do its part — then pulls results back together. Shipper started keeping one chat per project as the manager, handing tasks to separate AI agents and tracking results. She added skeptic and verifier agents for high-stakes data work. She also made the feedback recurring: a weekly Friday report that scores her work, notes changes, and suggests experiments.

The piece is honest about limitations: the setup is still experimental, she sometimes gets baffling answers or projects drift, and when tired or rushed she defaults to long single-thread conversations. But the trendline shows her Codex usage shifting from one-off chats to delegated multi-agent project management — a transition driven entirely by asking the AI to teach her how to use it better.
