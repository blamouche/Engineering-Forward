Every's weekly "Copy Our Homework" issue collects nine reusable AI workflows from the week's Context Window editions — including agent-driven computer errands, four-step deck generation, folder auditing, and token spend optimization — alongside new Vibe Checks of the Jev model by Mike Taylor and GPT-6 Astra's VFX capabilities by the Afterimage studio, plus six thesis statements about the future of human work with AI.

## Takeaways
- **Seven staffers on agent-delegated errands**: Every team members hand off calendar upkeep, Verizon support chats, listing items for sale, troubleshooting slow laptops, and transferring internet service to Codex — demonstrating that agent-driven computer use has moved beyond demos to mundane personal productivity.
- **Four-step deck workflow**: Douglas Brundage designs 2-3 reference slides, hammers out copy with Codex, has it build the full deck in Google Slides, and saves his edits as rules for next time — creating a compounding system where each deck improves the agent's output.
- **Folder organization as agent infrastructure**: Kieran Klaassen's four rules — one folder per job, store what agents know separately from the system that assigns tasks, keep daily/weekly/monthly/yearly memory files, fit the system to how you already work — represent a practical pattern for agent-augmented knowledge work.
- **Token spend auditing**: Randy Counsman's unbounded agent swarm consumed 4.5 billion tokens on a Blender model in 1.5 days; the fix caps the orchestrator at five subagents and gives a judge agent a reference image. A prompt that audits session usage records can separate necessary work from repeated status checks.
- **Jev Vibe Check by Mike Taylor**: A first look at Jev as an entirely new model architecture from an OpenAI researcher who taught chatbots to follow instructions — a model that makes decisions rather than generating text.
- **Astra VFX Vibe Check**: Afterimage studio tested GPT-6 Astra on real VFX workloads — masking, tracking, compositing 3D objects into live video — finding it handles rigid geometry well enough to prototype VFX without a specialist.
- **Six thesis statements**: Predictions include "good design will signal mediocrity" (Douglas Brundage), "technical competence will yield to people skills" (Will England, Walleye Capital), "the risk won't be bad execution — it'll be endless execution" (Diana Palacio), and "AI will make uncertainty cheap to explore without making it cheap to resolve" (Graham Walker, MDCalc).

## Synthesis
Every's "Copy Our Homework" format is itself a workflow innovation. Instead of publishing scattered daily pieces and letting them get lost in the feed, the weekly roundup gathers every prompt, workflow, and tip into a single stealable collection. The format acknowledges a real problem: at the pace AI tooling evolves, even dedicated readers miss things, and the value of a workflow is realized when someone actually uses it.

The agent-delegated errands section is the most immediately actionable. Seven Every staffers describe specific, mundane tasks they've handed off to Codex — calendar upkeep, support chats, selling items, troubleshooting. These aren't hypothetical use cases; they're actual errands completed by people who write about AI for a living. The specificity matters: "I had Codex handle a Verizon support chat" is more useful than "AI can automate customer service" because it reveals the friction points, the guardrails needed, and the type of task where agents add real value vs. where they waste tokens.

The deck workflow from Douglas Brundage is a mini-system design pattern. The key insight is the feedback loop: saved edits become rules for the next iteration, which means the agent's output quality compounds over time rather than resetting with each session. This is how you turn a general-purpose agent into a specialized tool — not by fine-tuning, but by accumulating workflow-specific rules in the agent's context. The same pattern applies beyond decks: any repetitive creative task where the human's edits follow discoverable patterns can be captured this way.

Kieran Klaassen's folder organization rules deserve more attention than they'll get. The separation of "what agents know" from "the system that assigns tasks" is architecturally important — it means agent knowledge is portable, inspectable, and editable independently of the orchestration layer. The daily/weekly/monthly/yearly memory file structure mirrors how human memory actually works: most things are forgotten, but the compressed summaries persist and inform future decisions. And the rule "fit the system to how you already work" is the most important one — agent infrastructure that requires you to change your workflow will be abandoned, while infrastructure that wraps around existing habits will compound.

The token spend story is a cautionary tale with a concrete fix. 4.5 billion tokens on a single Blender model in 1.5 days is not a sign of success; it's a sign of unbounded recursion. The fix — five subagents max, judge agent with reference image — is a pattern that applies broadly: any agent system needs bounds on depth, width, and verification, or it will consume resources without producing better results.

The six thesis statements are the most thought-provoking part of the issue. Graham Walker's "AI will make uncertainty cheap to explore without making it cheap to resolve" captures something essential: AI dramatically lowers the cost of generating hypotheses and exploring options, but the cost of actually resolving uncertainty — through experiments, evidence, or real-world feedback — remains high. This asymmetry means AI can make us feel more certain without making us more correct, which is a risk that compounds as AI tools become more persuasive.
