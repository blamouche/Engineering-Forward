# Compound Writing: How I Turned My Process Into a Plugin
**Source**: https://every.to/emails/compound-writing-how-i-turned-my-process-into-a-plugin
**Date**: 2026-09-02
**Author**: Every (Katie Parrott)
**Keywords**: AI writing, Compound Writing, plugins, skills, Claude, editorial process, AI-assisted writing, style guides, writing workflow

## Elevator pitch
Every staff writer Katie Parrott shares how she packaged her interviewing, outlining, drafting, and revising methods into reusable AI skills bundled as a plugin — turning a personal writing process into something others can install and adapt to their own voice.

## Takeaways
- The "AI writes vs. human writes" framing is wrong — writing is a process of thinking, developing ideas, drafting, editing, and publishing, and AI can help throughout
- Compound Writing is a plugin that bundles skills for interviewing, outlining, drafting, and reviewing — inspired by Kieran Klaassen's Compound Engineering and Dan Shipper's agent-native architectures
- The evolution of AI writing tools: long chats → Projects with style guides → Skills for specific methods → Plugins that bundle skills for sharing
- AI helped the author get past the blank page and draw insights from experience, but the writing got stronger over time as she developed her own voice
- The process captures what the work teaches: a recurring aversion becomes a guardrail; a sentence-level preference becomes an instruction the agent reads next time
- Compound Writing is described as a "writing gym" — skills provide equipment and structure, but the writer decides where to push harder
- The plugin approach means you can choose specific help (getting an idea onto the page, a skeptical read, a paragraph that doesn't sound like you) without adopting someone's entire routine

## Synthesis
Katie Parrott's Compound Writing piece is both a practical guide and a philosophical statement about AI-assisted writing. The core argument is that the common question — "Did a human write this, or did AI?" — is the wrong frame entirely. Writing is a multi-stage process (thinking, developing ideas, drafting, editing, publishing), and AI can assist at every stage without replacing the human's role as the decision-maker and voice behind the work.

The technical evolution she traces is important for understanding where AI writing tools are heading. The early workflow was a long chat where you pasted material, asked for a draft, explained what was wrong, and tried again — with useful discoveries often buried in conversation history. Projects introduced persistent context: style guides, sample essays, and instructions available across chats. Skills then allowed preserving a method for a particular job — an interview skill tells the agent how to draw out thinking without supplying an argument; an editing skill tells it to examine structure before polishing sentences. Plugins bundle these skills so they can be shared and installed by others.

The deeper insight is about compounding self-knowledge. As Parrott used AI in her writing, it helped her see her own habits more clearly: how a frustration at work becomes a framework in her column, or how accumulated details give way to a short sentence that releases pressure. Once those patterns had names, she could use them deliberately, notice when they became formulas, and decide when to break them. The process gets more specific to the writer as they use it — a recurring aversion becomes a guardrail, a sentence preference becomes an agent instruction. This is not AI replacing craft; it's AI making craft explicit and transferable.
