# What Playing With AI Taught Me About My Work
**Source**: Every (hello@every.to)
**Date**: 2026-09-14
**Author**: Katie Parrott (Every)
**Keywords**: AI creativity, play vs work, Compound Writing, Astra, AI workflows, process improvement, distraction

## Elevator pitch
An Every frontier team member's weekend of playing with AI — turning writing skills into imaginary creatures and Every columns into Disney characters — unexpectedly improved her actual writing system, revealing how AI-mediated play can generate useful professional insights through side doors rather than deliberate planning.

## Takeaways
- The author used Astra to turn Compound Writing skills into interactive "Codex pets" — creatures that embodied writing processes like brainstorming (a purple axolotl) and saving lessons (a mossy tortoise with archive drawers)
- Building imaginary worlds for AI characters felt like play but generated real insights about how to teach writing skills
- The pressure to turn play into a deliverable ("a prompt a reader could run") made the work harder and less productive than the original exploration
- Distraction-led AI use produced a more useful result than planned work: turning Every columns into Disney characters led to adding "Reader's want and need" and "Their hero's journey" sections to the Compound Writing system
- The audience guidance now lives in AUDIENCE.md, a file the writer and AI consult during piece development
- Key lesson: useful things can arrive through side doors while planned projects stall — the challenge is recognizing and capturing the useful without making the whole experiment the next assignment

## Synthesis
Katie Parrott writes in Every about the productive tension between playing with AI and getting work done. As a member of Every's frontier team — whose job is to explore what AI makes possible and turn those learnings into something others can use — she describes spending Labor Day weekend and hundreds of dollars in tokens creating imaginary creatures and worlds with Astra, an AI tool.

The first experiment turned Compound Writing skills into characters: the Brainstorm skill became a purple axolotl with a light bulb, the Save skill became a mossy tortoise with wooden archive drawers for a shell. She built interactive worlds for these characters, inspired by Hayao Miyazaki's animated worlds. When she tried to formalize this into a deliverable — a prompt readers could run independently — the project stalled under the weight of requirements.

The breakthrough came from a different distraction entirely. While avoiding an ambitious essay, she asked Astra to turn Every's columns into Disney characters with villains, sidekicks, and signature songs. Working on these characters required thinking about what moves them: what they want, what stands in their way, what they need to learn. Those questions turned out to be exactly the right questions for understanding what a reader needs from an essay. By the next morning, she'd added audience guidance to Compound Writing — sections for "Reader's want and need" and "Their hero's journey" — creating AUDIENCE.md as a shared reference for writer and AI.

The essay's honest insight: the useful thing arrived through a side door, while the planned project sat there. The challenge for anyone working with AI is learning to take something useful from an experiment without making the whole experiment the next assignment.
