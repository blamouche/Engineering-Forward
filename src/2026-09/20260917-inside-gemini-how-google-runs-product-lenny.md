# Inside Gemini: How Google Runs Product for Its Model
**Source**: Lenny's Newsletter (lenny@substack.com) — https://theskip.substack.com/p/inside-gemini-how-google-runs-product
**Date**: 2026-09-17
**Author**: Nikhyl Singhal (The Skip / Lenny's Newsletter)
**Keywords**: Google, Gemini, product management, frontier model, Tulsee Doshi, evals, vibes, prototyping, model personality, shipping, AI labs

## Elevator pitch
A podcast conversation with Tulsee Doshi, who leads product for Google's Gemini models, revealing how product management at a frontier AI lab differs from consumer or enterprise PM — no customer hands you a requirements doc, the team decides what the model should get better at, and the scarce skill is saying precisely what "good" looks like.

## Takeaways
- **Frontier model PM is a new category**: Consumer PM is about reading data and building taste. Enterprise PM starts from a customer's stated problem. Running product for a frontier model takes a mixture of both and bends each: no customer hands the team a requirements document, the team decides what the model should get better at, then describes it precisely enough for researchers to build toward.
- **Customer requests get weighed against where the research is going**: Customers can't see what the researchers are investing in. Simply stack-ranking requests would produce "a boring product." The PM's job is knowing where the research is going, protecting it, and still meeting a growing number of use cases. That requires establishing a seat with researchers — calibrating to what motivates each researcher and delivering value to earn earlier involvement.
- **A model has a personality, and someone has to own it**: Benchmarks don't capture what a model is like to talk to. Tulsee calls this gap "vibes" and treats it as product territory. The instrument is a standing set of prompts — from asking for a joke to running an agentic task — tested against each new model and the competition. Simon Tokumine (PM for NotebookLM) is cited as someone whose model opinions she trusts because he deeply uses every model, not just Google's.
- **The scarce skill is saying what good looks like**: Researchers can hill-climb toward almost anything you point them at. The hard problem is defining good evals, choosing which to invest in, and setting expectations. Tulsee's advice: "Take 20 prompts" — twenty examples where a win and a loss are obvious. That forces a vague ask into something specific enough to align a room. "Technical" on this team means thorough, not computer science — hiring from finance, consulting, and operations as readily as engineering.
- **Prototype the features that don't work yet**: The best PMs she works with prototype things the model can't handle yet — not science fiction, but "glimmers of light" where a small improvement would unlock something. NotebookLM keeps a set of features the model can't support; each new model gets tried against them. When capability catches up, they're first to benefit, and the feedback drives research direction.
- **Shipping runs on conviction, not checklists**: Each release is a fresh zero-to-one — a new model nobody has used. Basic evals first, then real users as fast as possible, with two questions: is anything fundamentally broken, and are there glimmers of light? The tell is behavior change: longer conversations, harder tasks, people coming back.

## Synthesis
In this fourth conversation of the Inside PM series (after Meta, Stripe, and Waymo), Nikhyl Singhal sits down with Tulsee Doshi, who leads product for the Gemini models — the engine powering the Gemini app, Search, and every developer on the API, plus Google's video, image, music, and audio models. Doshi has been at Google eleven years, across Search, YouTube, and a stint leading responsible AI.

The conversation reveals that product management at a frontier AI lab is a fundamentally different job from consumer or enterprise PM. Google's big-tech launch machine — planning cycles, launch reviews, hundreds of teams coordinating — still exists around her, but a model team can't run that way because each release is a new model nobody has used. The team decides what "capable" means: for which customers, for which uses, and where the model should shine. Coding, video, long documents, agents that take actions over hours — those are choices made before the research starts.

The process for handling requests is more concrete than expected. A product team builds a prototype on the model as it exists today — the surest way to see where it falls over. Failures get sorted: some fixable with system instructions or better prompting, some fundamental to the model requiring real research time. Those get turned into evals: a set of examples with a clear definition of what a win and a loss look like. "Nothing can replace a great eval," Doshi says. The scarce skill across the entire operation is saying, precisely, what good looks like — and that's a skill any PM can practice tomorrow, lab or no lab.

Doshi identifies four habits she would carry to any product job: build first (prototype before researching requirements); start with twenty prompts (the fastest way to say what you actually want); keep a few features the model can't handle yet (so you're first when capability arrives); and use every model yourself, yours and competitors' (the scores won't tell you what a model is like to work with). None of this needs a lab — it needs a PM who knows what they want well enough to show it, and who keeps their hands on the models long enough to notice when the answer changes.
