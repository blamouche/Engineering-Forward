# Evals for Everyone: Building Personal Benchmarks for Every Employee
**Source**: https://every.to/evals-for-everyone
**Date**: 2026-09-10
**Author**: Every (Laura Entis)
**Keywords**: AI evals, personal benchmarks, model evaluation, Dan Shipper, Mike Taylor, Fable 5.1, GPT-6 Astra, model comparison, AI testing

## Elevator pitch
Every CEO Dan Shipper and head of evals Mike Taylor make the case for building personal AI benchmarks for every employee — custom evals that test how well a model performs specific parts of your work, graded against your own standards — and share a practical five-step workflow for turning the corrections you already give AI into reusable checks.

## Takeaways
- Public benchmarks like graduate-level science tests don't tell you whether a model knows where you'd put a comma or how many ideas belong on a slide — personal benchmarks fill that gap
- Every is building a personal benchmark for every employee: collecting 5-10 recurring AI tasks, running them across models, and turning subjective feedback into pass/fail checks
- GPT-5.6 Luna outperformed Fable 5.1 and GPT-5.6 Sol on Mike Taylor's benchmark, convincing him a smaller, cheaper model could handle much of his daily work
- The five-step workflow: pick a recurring task, use it as a test, turn feedback into a checklist, verify AI can reliably apply checks, resolve disagreements and test again
- Evals become a compounding loop: grading rules can become skills that improve future outputs, and failures become structured feedback rather than one-off corrections
- The team's daily model usage reveals a fragmented landscape: Astra for fluid writing and computer use, Fable 5.1 for thinking and planning, GPT-5.6 Sol for cost-effective coding tasks

## Synthesis
Every's "Evals for Everyone" article is one of the most practical guides to AI model evaluation published this year. The core insight is that public benchmarks — even sophisticated ones like graduate-level science tests where Astra scores 96% and Fable 5.1 scores 93% — are useless for answering the question that actually matters: does this model do my specific job well? Dan Shipper's answer is to build a personal benchmark for every employee, turning subjective editorial and operational preferences into reproducible, pass/fail checks.

The workflow is elegantly simple. Mike Taylor and Nityesh Agarwal collect 5-10 tasks a person regularly delegates to AI, run them across multiple models, and ask the person to articulate what they like and dislike. Those judgments become checks — some programmatic (does the page load? do calculated scores match source data?), others subjective (does the design avoid the dark-mode aesthetic Mike dislikes?). An AI judge then applies the checks, and the human verifies agreement. When the benchmark reliably matches human judgment, it becomes a permanent tool for comparing models.

The most telling result: GPT-5.6 Luna — a smaller, cheaper model — outperformed Fable and Sol on Mike's benchmark. This is the kind of insight that only a personal benchmark can surface, and it has direct cost implications. The article also includes the team's weekly model usage breakdown, which reads like a real-time map of the frontier: Astra for fluid writing, Fable for deep thinking, Sol for cost-efficient coding, Opus for heavy lifting. The implication for any engineering team is clear: stop benchmarking against public leaderboards and start benchmarking against your own work.
