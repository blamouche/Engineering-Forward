## Elevator pitch
Sifted's deep dive into Europe's most active growth investors reveals that the EU's €5bn Scaleup Europe Fund managed by EQT has become the dominant force in late-stage European tech funding, tackling the chronic shortage of growth capital that has long held back the continent's most promising companies.

## Takeaways
- The EU's €5bn Scaleup Europe Fund managed by EQT is the most active growth investor in European tech this summer
- Europe's chronic shortage of late-stage growth capital has been the main bottleneck preventing homegrown tech companies from scaling
- Samsung's backing of Mistral's €3bn Series D highlights the strategic interest of industrial players in European AI independence
- The Scaleup Europe Fund's size and mandate represent a structural shift in European tech financing, moving beyond fragmented national funds
- France has sought to block the UK's role in the fund, revealing ongoing political tensions in how European tech investment is governed

## Synthesis
Sifted's analysis of Europe's growth investor landscape centers on the €5bn Scaleup Europe Fund managed by EQT, which has dominated European tech headlines throughout the summer. The fund directly addresses what has long been identified as Europe's primary tech bottleneck: the absence of late-stage growth capital that forces successful startups to seek funding from US investors or relocate entirely.

The fund's scale — €5bn — puts it in a different league from the fragmented national venture funds that have characterized European tech investing. Samsung's participation in Mistral's €3bn Series D (which Sifted had scooped early) illustrates how the strategic value of European AI independence extends beyond software into the semiconductor and hardware supply chain. Industrial players are increasingly willing to back European champions with patient capital.

The political dimension is revealing: France's attempt to block the UK's role in the fund shows that European tech investment remains entangled in post-Brexit politics. The question is whether the fund can transcend these tensions to build a genuinely pan-European growth financing ecosystem — or whether national interests will continue to fragment what should be a unified capital market.
