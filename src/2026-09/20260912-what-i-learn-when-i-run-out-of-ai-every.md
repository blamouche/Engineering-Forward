# What I Learn When I Run Out of AI
**Source**: https://every.to/p/what-i-learn-when-i-run-out-of-ai
**Date**: 2026-09-11
**Author**: Jack Cheng (Every)
**Keywords**: AI usage limits, creative process, Claude, Fable 5.1, model efficiency, snacking, computing history, time-sharing, Alan Kay, Sherry Turkle

## Elevator pitch
A reflective essay on how hitting AI model usage limits forces deeper thinking, drawing parallels from computing history to argue that constraints — not unlimited access — may produce better work.

## Takeaways
- Hitting Anthropic's weekly usage limits on Claude subscriptions forced the author to save his biggest questions for "consulting the oracle" sessions, giving ideas time to develop during the wait
- As frontier models like Fable 5.1 became more token-efficient, the same limits stretched to 3-4 days instead of one, leading to "snacking" — choosing easy, low-impact work over difficult, high-impact work (a concept from Will Larson)
- The essay draws a historical parallel: before time-sharing in the 1960s, programmers waited their turn on mainframes, and the wait itself imposed a discipline that immediate access removed
- Alan Kay envisioned the personal computer as an "object to think with" — a dynamic medium for exploring ideas — but it sometimes becomes an "object to think for," generating work without considering its value
- Sherry Turkle observed in 1984 that immediacy creates a compulsion: "It is hard to walk away from a computer program with an undiscovered 'bug'"
- The author proposes building a "smaller version of the journey to Delphi" into daily work: writing down why a question matters before bringing it to the model

## Synthesis
Cheng's essay operates on two levels: a personal reflection on AI usage habits and a broader argument about the relationship between constraints and creative thinking. The core insight is that unlimited access to powerful AI models can degrade the quality of thinking by removing the forced pauses that previously separated idea generation from execution. When the author had to wait for model access, he spent that waiting time refining his questions — a process that improved the eventual output even though it felt like friction.

The historical parallels are well-chosen. Annie J. Easley carrying punch cards to a separate NASA building, Mary Berners-Lee queuing at Ferranti with a sign reading "Think—but not here!" — these examples illustrate that the tension between access and deliberation predates AI by decades. The time-sharing revolution of the 1960s, which made computing interactive, also introduced what UC Berkeley's Susan Graham called "a certain amount of sloppiness." Alan Kay's vision of computing as a "dynamic medium" — an instrument for expressing and altering ideas in real time — was revolutionary but also removed a constraint that had functioned as a quality filter.

The most actionable suggestion is deceptively simple: before bringing a difficult question to a model, write down why it matters and what you think the answer might be. This creates a "smaller version of the journey" — a deliberate pause that forces articulation of intent before the model's response arrives and potentially overrides it. Some ideas, Cheng acknowledges, will still deserve to be built in the morning. Others will lose their appeal once the immediate gratification of AI execution is no longer available to mask their thinness.
