The structure gives Revolut US valuation upside while maintaining European liquidity — it's London's best available deal, not a vote of confidence in the LSE.
- **Wise's LSE-to-Nasdaq migration is the closest precedent**: Wise's recent migration from London to New York shows the direction of travel for European fintech — dual-listing is a stepping stone, and the gravitational pull of US capital markets remains strong.

## Synthesis
Linas's newsletter this week covers two stories that, on the surface, have nothing in common: a fintech IPO and a consumer AI platform launch. But the connective tissue is the infrastructure layer for commerce — who controls the rails when agents, not humans, initiate transactions.

Revolut's dual IPO is the closer of two eras. For three years, Storonsky publicly criticized the LSE as irrational — lower valuations, less liquidity, worse terms for founders. The dual listing is a compromise that gives Revolut access to US capital markets while maintaining a European presence. But the Wise precedent — Wise migrated its primary listing from London to New York — suggests the dual structure is often a transitional state rather than a permanent one. The deeper story is that European fintech's most successful companies are structurally pulled toward US capital markets, and dual-listing is the compromise that manages that tension for now.

The Meta Muse connectors story is the more forward-looking piece. Meta's connector launch is essentially a bid to become the platform layer for agentic commerce — the infrastructure through which agents discover, negotiate, and execute transactions. The Stilla acquisition is strategic because it gives Meta agents on both sides: Muse as the consumer-side agent and Stilla's infrastructure on the merchant side. If a Muse agent wants to buy something, it can use Stilla's rails to complete the transaction without leaving Meta's ecosystem. This is the App Store model applied to agent-driven commerce: Meta controls the discovery, the payment, and the agent layer.

But Linas identifies the critical gap: Meta's ambition far exceeds its actual developer platform. The vision is to be the App Store for agentic commerce, but the developer tools, API surface, and partner ecosystem don't yet match that vision. This gap is where competitors — Anthropic, Google, and open-source alternatives — can still establish themselves. Anthropic's own agentic commerce strategy, covered in the newsletter's bonus section, is the most direct competitor to Meta's approach, with a different architecture (querying systems via MCP rather than hosting data centrally).

The Grok Bot guide rounds out the competitive landscape: xAI's consumer agent is another entrant in the agentic commerce race, though its positioning is more aligned with real-time information access than transaction execution. The overall picture is of a land grab: multiple tech giants are racing to build the infrastructure layer for agent-driven transactions, and the winner will control the rails for a market that CO/AI estimates at $3-5 trillion in AI agent transactions by 2030.

The strategic question for every consumer-facing business is what to do now. If agents become a significant transaction channel, businesses need to expose their inventory, pricing, and fulfillment through agent-readable APIs. The companies that build agent-native commerce interfaces early will have a structural advantage — just as companies that built mobile commerce early captured the smartphone commerce wave.
