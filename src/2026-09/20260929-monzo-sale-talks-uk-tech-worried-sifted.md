# Monzo Sale Talks Have UK Tech Worried
**Source**: Sifted Daily (sifted@sifted.eu)
**Date**: 2026-09-29
**Author**: Damisola Sulaiman
**Keywords**: Monzo, Nubank, UK tech, fintech, banking licence, Cohere, AI sovereignty, AI agents, Stripe, Manna, European spacetech, Nscale, Reactive Technologies

## Elevator pitch
Sifted covers the Monzo-Nubank sale talks (£8-10B) reigniting fears about Britain's tech exit problem, Cohere's chief AI officer warning about local capabilities, AI agents as the next buyer redesigning pricing, Manna's move to Oklahoma, and European spacetech's launch independence.

## Takeaways
- Sky News reported Monzo is in early talks to sell to Brazilian banking giant Nubank for £8-10B, roughly double Monzo's last valuation, sparking debate about Britain's inability to retain its most successful tech companies
- Comments were more nuanced than the usual "Britain is a bad place to build a business" narrative — people were genuinely happy for Monzo's staff while mourning that a British tech success might become a foreign subsidiary instead of a public company
- Revolut repeatedly came up unprompted as the "real" winner of UK neobanking, raising the question of whether a company needs to be its category's outright winner to earn an IPO
- Cohere's chief AI officer warned that the US and China will hold Europe "by the throat" if it doesn't develop local AI capabilities, framing AI sovereignty as a national security issue
- Stripe is already redesigning products and pricing for a world where AI agents make purchases, not just humans — leaders from Anthropic, Lovable, and ElevenLabs shared monetisation strategies for agent-driven transactions
- Manna boss Bobby Healy moved to Oklahoma, saying "the US is open for business, Europe is not," highlighting the regulatory divergence in drone delivery
- UK investors including Entrepreneurs First and Seedcamp called for US-style exemptions for small VC funds, arguing current rules stifle a new generation of investors
- Nscale (London) raised $3.36bn in pre-IPO convertible funding led by Third Point with Nvidia, Apollo, and Citadel for AI data centre and GPU infrastructure
- Isar Aerospace's successful launch gives hope for European spacetech independence from SpaceX

## Synthesis
The Monzo-Nubank sale talks crystallise a recurring anxiety in UK tech: Britain can produce category-defining companies but cannot hold onto them. The £8-10B price tag — roughly double Monzo's last valuation — only works if Nubank can do in Britain what it has never done outside Latin America: turn £25.7B of mostly unlent deposits into loans. The deal would be a stock-heavy acquisition, and the strategic value lies in Monzo's banking licence, not its technology, which is a reversal of how tech acquisitions usually work.

The broader debate surfaced a second theme: Revolut is now the implicit benchmark against which every UK neobank is measured. The fact that commentators brought up Revolut unprompted suggests that the market has already anointed a winner, and the question is whether being second is enough for a public listing. This expectation gap — between being a category winner and being a strong second — may itself be pushing companies toward sales rather than IPOs.

Cohere's intervention on AI sovereignty adds a different dimension. The argument that Europe needs local AI capabilities to avoid being held "by the throat" by the US and China mirrors the fintech sovereignty debate: in both cases, the concern is that strategic infrastructure — whether banking rails or AI models — is controlled by foreign entities. Stripe's push into agent-driven commerce, where AI agents make purchases autonomously, further underscores how the rules of engagement are being rewritten by AI, and how unprepared European regulation is for a world where the buyer might not be human.
