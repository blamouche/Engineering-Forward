# A16z Wants More European Deals
**Source**: Sifted Daily (sifted@sifted.eu)
**Date**: 2026-09-22
**Author**: Anne Sraders (Sifted)
**Keywords**: a16z, Andreessen Horowitz, European tech, venture capital, European startups, home turf advantage, US vs Europe

## Elevator pitch
A San Francisco-based a16z partner argues European startups should stop trying to replicate Silicon Valley and instead lean into what makes Europe unique — regulatory frameworks, deep tech talent, and market-specific moats — as the firm ramps up its European deal flow.

## Takeaways
- **a16z is actively increasing European deal flow**: The firm's SF-based partners are spending more time in Europe, looking for companies that build on European strengths rather than aping US models.
- **"Unapologetically European" as a competitive advantage**: The partner highlights that the best European companies succeed by embracing local market dynamics, regulatory environments, and talent pools rather than trying to be Silicon Valley clones.
- **European tech is not falling behind — it's differentiating**: The us-versus-them framing obscures the real story: European startups are building in areas where Europe has structural advantages (fintech, deep tech, privacy-first, regulation-compliant).
- **Palantir and Goldman alumni launching against the Big Four**: The newsletter also covers a new startup founded by Palantir and Goldman Sachs veterans aiming to disrupt the Big Four consulting firms — a signal of talent flowing from incumbents into European challenger ventures.
- **VC attention is shifting eastward**: With US valuations remaining high and European deal multiples more reasonable, major US funds like a16z see an arbitrage opportunity in Europe's tech ecosystem.

## Synthesis
The interview with the a16z partner crystallizes a shift that's been building throughout 2026. For years, the European tech conversation was framed as a catching-up exercise — Europe needs more capital, bigger funds, deeper talent pools, more risk appetite. The a16z partner inverts that framing entirely: the opportunity for Europe isn't to become a better Silicon Valley, it's to be the best version of itself.

This argument rests on three pillars. First, European regulatory frameworks — GDPR, the AI Act, MiCA — are not just compliance burdens but moats. Companies that build privacy-first, regulation-compliant products from day one have a structural advantage as global regulation converges toward European standards. Second, Europe's deep tech talent in areas like semiconductor design, quantum computing, and materials science comes from a different tradition than US software-first engineering — one that may prove more durable as AI commoditizes software. Third, the European market itself, with its fragmentation across languages, currencies, and regulatory regimes, forces companies to build distribution and localization capabilities that translate directly into global expansion.

The newsletter also covers a startup founded by Palantir and Goldman Sachs alumni taking on the Big Four consulting firms. This is a microcosm of the broader trend: experienced operators from top-tier institutions are choosing to build in Europe rather than the US, and they're targeting incumbents that have been slow to adopt AI. The Big Four's audit and consulting businesses are vulnerable to AI-native challengers who can do at a fraction of the cost what armies of associates used to do manually.

The a16z partner's perspective should be read as both signal and strategy. As signal, it confirms that US mega-funds are actively hunting European deals — which means European founders will increasingly have the choice of taking US term sheets at US-style valuations. As strategy, the "embrace what's unique" framing is also a recruitment pitch: a16z wants European founders to know they won't be forced into a US-shaped box if they take a16z money. Whether that promise holds when portfolio companies face pressure to prioritize US revenue growth remains to be seen.
