# 90 Minutes of Unfiltered Product Advice from Snap and Discord's Product Chief
**Source**: Lenny's Newsletter (lenny@substack.com) — https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/90-minutes-of-unfiltered-product
**Date**: 2026-09-21
**Author**: Lenny Rachitsky (Lenny's Newsletter)
**Keywords**: Peter Sellis, Snap, Discord, product management, team design, Nikita Bier, PM quality, ads business, growth, core product, systems thinking, product leadership

## Elevator pitch
Peter Sellis — the first PM at Snapchat and later Head of Product at Discord — delivers an unfiltered, post-departure interview covering why he designs teams like terrorist organizations, what it was really like to manage Nikita Bier, why the median PM is bad, why Snapchat's ads business never reached its potential, and why growth almost always comes from the core product rather than features bolted on.

## Takeaways
- **Teams designed like terrorist organizations**: Sellis advocates for flat, autonomous, cell-like team structures rather than traditional hierarchical org charts, drawing an explicit analogy to how terrorist cells operate — small groups with independent decision-making authority and clear missions.
- **Managing Nikita Bier**: Sellis offers a candid account of working with the controversial consumer app builder, providing insight into the tension between individual brilliance and organizational fit.
- **The median PM is bad**: Sellis argues that most product managers lack the systems thinking and judgment needed to be effective, a problem he attributes to how the role is defined, hired for, and measured.
- **Snapchat's ads business failure**: The ads business never reached its potential despite the product's massive cultural impact, a gap Sellis attributes to organizational and strategic decisions rather than market conditions.
- **Growth comes from the core product**: Nearly all meaningful growth comes from improving the core product experience, not from launching adjacent features or growth hacks bolted onto the side.
- **Three oxymorons of great PM**: Sellis identifies paradoxical principles that define excellent product management — counterintuitive truths that only make sense in practice.
- **Systems thinking as core competency**: The recommended reading (Thinking in Systems, The Innovator's Dilemma) underscores Sellis's belief that product management is fundamentally about understanding dynamic systems, not executing feature roadmaps.

## Synthesis
This interview is valuable precisely because Sellis has nothing to promote. He's left Discord, has no book, no startup, no PR team filtering his words. That freedom produces the kind of honesty that sitting executives rarely afford — honesty about failed strategies, difficult personalities, and structural problems in the PM profession.

The terrorist organization analogy for team design is provocative but substantive. Terrorist cells operate with small size, autonomous decision-making, clear missions, and redundancy — qualities that traditional product org charts suppress through hierarchy, approval gates, and centralized planning. Sellis isn't advocating for chaos; he's arguing that the coordination cost of traditional structures exceeds their benefits, especially at the speed consumer products require. This aligns with how both Snap (in its early years) and Discord (during its fastest growth period) actually operated — small teams with broad autonomy, shipping against clear metrics.

The Nikita Bier discussion is likely to draw the most attention, but the more durable insight is Sellis's critique of the median PM. The argument isn't that individual PMs are untalented; it's that the role has been defined in a way that systematically produces mediocre outcomes. If PMs are measured on delivery (shipping features) rather than outcomes (moving metrics that matter), they optimize for throughput over impact. The systems thinking reading list reinforces this: most PM frameworks treat products as static artifacts to be specified, while Sellis treats them as dynamic systems with feedback loops, delays, and unintended consequences.

The Snapchat ads business post-mortem is the most strategically important takeaway. Snap built one of the most beloved consumer products in history — a product with extraordinary engagement and cultural resonance — yet failed to monetize that engagement at the level of Meta or Google. Sellis's framing suggests this wasn't a technology problem or a market problem but an organizational one: the structures, incentives, and decisions that produce great consumer products don't automatically produce great ad businesses, and the gap between product excellence and commercial excellence is where most consumer companies fail.

The "growth comes from the core product" principle is the through-line. It's a rejection of the feature-factory model where PMs ship new surfaces hoping one sticks. Instead, it argues that the highest-leverage work is deepening the core experience — making the thing people already love work better, faster, and for more people. This is harder to do, less visible in roadmaps, and rarely rewarded by the incentives most PM organizations have set up. But it's where the actual growth lives.
