# "Load-Bearing": AI Vocabulary Now Dominates 47,000+ GitHub PRs
**Source**: https://louisabraham.github.io/load-bearing/
**Date**: 2026-08-28
**Author**: Louisa Abraham
**Keywords**: AI vocabulary, GitHub PRs, load-bearing, LLM writing style, AI-generated code, linguistic analysis

## Elevator pitch
A linguistic analysis of 47,000+ GitHub PRs found that a word cluster nonexistent in 2025 now accounts for 45% of human-authored PRs — with "load-bearing" as the top term — revealing how deeply AI writing patterns have penetrated software engineering communication.

## Takeaways
- A word cluster that didn't exist in 2025 now appears in 45% of human-authored GitHub PRs
- The top word in this new cluster is "load-bearing," used in a metaphorical sense common in AI-generated text
- The analysis covered 47,000+ GitHub PRs, providing a large statistical sample
- This reveals how AI writing patterns are contaminating/propagating through human communication in software engineering
- The finding suggests AI-generated text has become normalized enough that humans are adopting its vocabulary unconsciously

## Synthesis
The analysis of 47,000+ GitHub PRs for "Claude-ish" vocabulary reveals something more subtle and pervasive than simple AI-generated content detection. A word cluster that simply didn't exist in 2025 — led by the term "load-bearing" used in its metaphorical sense — now appears in 45% of human-authored PRs. This isn't about detecting AI-generated PRs; it's about detecting how AI writing patterns have seeped into human writing.

"Load-bearing" is a perfect example. In pre-AI English, "load-bearing" was primarily an engineering term referring to structural elements that carry weight. AI models, particularly Claude, popularized its metaphorical use: "load-bearing assumption," "load-bearing paragraph," "load-bearing question." This usage pattern was distinctive enough to AI-generated text that it became a tell — and now it's appearing in human-authored PRs at scale.

The 45% figure is striking. It doesn't mean 45% of PRs are AI-generated; it means 45% of PRs written by humans now use vocabulary patterns that originated in AI text. This is linguistic contamination in the most literal sense: humans who read AI-generated text (code reviews, documentation, summaries) are absorbing and reproducing its vocabulary patterns in their own writing.

The implications extend beyond linguistics. If AI writing patterns are this pervasive in human communication, then the boundary between "human-written" and "AI-written" text is blurring not because AI is getting better at imitating humans, but because humans are getting better at imitating AI. The vocabulary of software engineering is being reshaped by the language models that participate in it, and this analysis provides the quantitative evidence.
