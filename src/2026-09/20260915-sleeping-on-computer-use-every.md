# You're Probably Sleeping On Computer Use
**Source**: Every (hello@every.to)
**Date**: 2026-09-15
**Author**: Laura Entis (Every)
**Keywords**: computer use, GPT-6 Astra, Codex, AI agents, automation, Google Slides, Claude Code, productivity

## Elevator pitch
GPT-6 Astra's computer use capability — clicking, typing, and scrolling through real applications — has transformed it from a "wouldn't use it daily" model to Mike Taylor's daily driver in under two weeks. Every's team is now outsourcing a remarkable range of tedious tasks to AI agents: filling school forms, maintaining calendars, checking book links, editing videos, listing items on marketplaces, and building slide decks. The piece argues computer use is the underrated capability that turns "I should probably do that" into "I'll ask the agent to do that."

## Takeaways
- Mike Taylor upgraded Astra from "yellow" (okay, wouldn't use daily) to "starting to believe Astra is AGI" in less than two weeks, driven entirely by computer use
- Astra handled six presentations for a prompt engineering course, adding screenshots and making hundreds of tiny edits in Google Slides with minimal intervention
- Taylor gave Astra a tedious assignment: checking every link in PDF proofs of his new book — Astra opened each destination, verified it matched, and compiled a spreadsheet of problems
- Every staff share how they use computer use: Randy Counsman has Codex submit follow-up maintenance requests while cooking; Brian Dell has Codex maintain his family calendar ("the highest-leverage thing I've done in my adult life")
- Becky Isjwara uses Codex to browse iPhone photos, identify sellable items, and list them on marketplaces via iPhone Mirroring
- Tyler Nishida uses computer use to manage app builds and assemble characters in Blender, finding it more useful than the apps' own agent connectors
- Douglas Brundage (head of marketing) has a workflow: design 2-3 reference slides, give Codex the copy, and let it build the full deck in Google Slides
- The piece also features six "Thesis Statements" — predictions about the future of human work with AI from builders and thinkers

## Synthesis
Laura Entis captures a genuine inflection point in how AI agents interact with software. The shift is not about a new model being smarter at reasoning — it's about a model being competent at operating a computer. Mike Taylor's about-face on Astra is the clearest signal: within two weeks of his measured Vibe Check, he was calling it AGI, not because it got better at writing but because it could navigate Google Slides, edit videos, and verify URLs in PDF proofs.

The "Inside Every" section is the most practical part of the piece. Each staffer has found a different way to exploit computer use, and the pattern is consistent: the tasks being outsourced are not intellectually hard, they're just annoying enough that humans routinely postpone them. Filling school forms, maintaining calendars, clearing WhatsApp storage, listing items for sale — these are the bureaucratic friction of modern life that computer use can eliminate. Brian Dell's assessment that calendar management via Codex is "the highest-leverage thing I've done in my adult life" is both hyperbolic and instructive.

The "Steal This Workflow" section on slide deck creation is the most actionable. Douglas Brundage's four-step process — design reference slides, determine copy, let AI do the heavy lifting, then feed edits back as rules — is a template any team could adopt. The mistakes (cramming 30 examples on one slide) are easy to fix, and the iterative rule-building means the agent gets better over time.

The broader implication is that computer use is the capability that makes AI agents feel like assistants rather than tools. When an agent can operate the same applications you use, the barrier to delegation drops dramatically. The piece's closing thought: "Computer use turns 'I should probably do that' into 'I'll ask the agent to do that.'"
