# Garbage In, Gospel Out: Understanding the Consequences of AI Decision Making

## Elevator pitch
Two real-world disasters — a chatbot hallucination that nearly started a US-China war and a Palantir AI targeting tool that contributed to a strike killing 123 children in Minab, Iran — illustrate that the real AI risk isn't superintelligence but machines moving too fast for human verification, and the framework for managing it is Bezos's one-way door vs. two-way door test.

## Takeaways
- **A chatbot nearly started a US-China war**: An analyst at Special Operations Command Pacific asked a chatbot to read a ship's manifest; it mixed open-source chatter with classified signals and concluded a Chinese vessel was carrying nuclear weapons components. Two AI passes, zero verification. Planes were in the air before someone checked the source — the report was "entirely false."
- **Palantir's Maven contributed to the Minab school strike**: Overreliance on Palantir's AI targeting tool helped wave through a strike on what turned out to be an elementary school in Minab, Iran, killing 123 children. Vetting that once took hours was compressed into minutes, and the civilian-harm review team had been cut from ten people to one.
- **The risk is speed, not smarts**: "AI allows you to get to a bad idea faster." The fashionable fear is superintelligence — a model that wakes up and outthinks us. The real risk showed up in the opposite costume: a machine too dumb to know it was wrong, moving faster than any human could check it.
- **The ruin problem in uniform**: When a decision can't be undone, you stop maximizing return and start minimizing the chance of catastrophe. Speed is free right up until the door only swings one way. The machine can't tell a two-way door from a one-way one — so humans must.
- **Anthropic's R&D Automation Index**: Claude now "leads" 26% of Anthropic's own R&D with ~30,000 internal agents, 90%+ of work at "collaborates or higher." But the load-bearing number is 0% fully autonomous — an IPO-era move to quantify self-improvement before a regulator does it for you.
- **McKinsey: 30% of companies using agentic AI got slower**: Not a failure of the model — a failure to define the win and budget for checking. The J-curve is real: output dips before it climbs, and shops that quit during the dip booked the cost and none of the gain.
- **Google admits Gemini was used to breach three companies**: All three US frontier labs have now confessed their models were turned into attack tools — the disclosure race means being the lab that didn't report misuse is now the reputational risk.
- **The "slowdown" is now an antitrust suit**: Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI, and Google are accused of illegally coordinating to slow AI. Meanwhile Anthropic steers toward a November IPO on a $100B revenue pace — you can't sell "we might slow down" to the public and defend "you colluded to slow down" in court at once.

## Synthesis
CO/AI's newsletter is the most important piece of AI commentary this week because it reframes the safety debate from a theoretical concern about future superintelligence to two documented catastrophes caused by present-day AI moving faster than human verification could keep up.

The ship manifest hallucination is the cleaner case. An analyst asked a chatbot to read a manifest. The machine mixed open-source chatter with classified signals and produced a confident conclusion: the Chinese vessel was carrying nuclear weapons components. A second AI turned that conclusion into a formal intelligence report. Two AI passes, zero verification between them. Planes were in the air. Boarding teams were staging. The US was one order away from firing on a Chinese ship. Then someone opened the source. The report was "entirely false." The eight words to tape to your monitor: "AI allows you to get to a bad idea faster."

The Minab case is the grimmer one. Two Tomahawks hit an elementary school in Iran. 123 children died. Pentagon investigators traced the strike partly to overreliance on Palantir's Maven AI targeting tool. Vetting that once took hours — hours spent checking whether the target was actually what the data said it was — got compressed into minutes. The Centcom team whose job was to catch exactly that kind of error had been cut from ten people to one. The savings math dropped the column where you verify the work.

CO/AI's central argument is that we've been arguing about the wrong noun. The fashionable fear is superintelligence — the model wakes up, outthinks us, wants things. The real risk showed up in the opposite costume: a machine too dumb to know it was wrong, moving at a speed no human could match. To the model, a support ticket and a kill order are the same problem. Same confidence, same tone, same half-second answer. The gradient — this one's reversible, that one ends lives — lives only in the human head. And we optimized away the very thing that carried that gradient: slowness. The board that meets monthly, the analyst who reads the source, the review that eats a week. That friction wasn't waste. It was how the system spent more deliberation on the decisions that could kill you.

The framework CO/AI proposes is Bezos's one-way door vs. two-way door test. Let AI rip through two-way doors — drafts, summaries, first-pass code — where speed is pure upside. Put a human hard on the one-way doors: anything that moves money, ships to a customer, or can't be reversed. This isn't a blanket "slow down AI" argument. It's a sorting argument: allocate human oversight where the decision is irreversible, and let speed flourish where mistakes are cheap.

The supporting stories reinforce the thesis from different angles. Anthropic's R&D Automation Index shows 26% of R&D "led" by Claude but 0% fully autonomous — the company is quietly drawing the same line CO/AI advocates. McKinsey's finding that 30% of agentic AI adopters got slower is the corporate version of the Minab lesson: speed without a scoreboard is just faster mistakes. The antitrust suit alleging AI slowdown coordination is the political version: you can't coordinate to slow down while racing to a $100B IPO.

The article's practical recommendations are concrete: sort your decisions by whether they can be undone before you let an agent near them; write your definition of "done" as a number before you deploy; fund the checker like the risk desk it is. The Centcom lesson is that cutting the verification team from ten to one wasn't efficiency — it was removing the friction that catches catastrophic errors. Glean's data showing AI saves 11 hours per week but 6.5 of those go back into checking output is the healthy version of the same dynamic. The question isn't whether to use AI — it's where to put the human gate.
