## Elevator pitch
CO/AI's analysis of the US reindustrialization push reveals a critical bottleneck: while AI intelligence is becoming cheap and downloadable, every robot joint needs high-torque motors with neodymium magnets doped with heavy rare earths — and China refines 94% of the world's rare-earth magnets, giving it a stranglehold on the robotics supply chain.

## Takeaways
- XPENG's IRON humanoid robot walked off the world's first automotive-grade humanoid assembly line in Guangzhou — 76 joints, 2,250 TOPS in its chest
- Every robot joint needs a high-torque motor, and every motor needs a neodymium-iron-boron magnet doped with dysprosium or terbium to survive heat
- China refines 94% of the world's rare-earth magnets, up from about 50% in 2005 — the grip tightens the further you get from the raw material
- The US and Australia have the rocks, but lack the refining capacity: the bottleneck is processing, not mining
- AI intelligence is the cheap part now — open models are distilled copies at a tenth of the cost — but the physical supply chain for robots remains concentrated in China
- Musk's promise to build ten billion robots is constrained not by AI capability but by magnet supply and manufacturing infrastructure

## Synthesis
CO/AI's Signal/Noise analysis reframes the robotics revolution around a material constraint that most AI commentary ignores: magnets. The story starts with XPENG's IRON robot walking off an automotive-grade assembly line in Guangzhou — a manufacturing milestone — but the real insight is the supply chain ladder behind it.

China's dominance in rare-earth magnets (94% of global refining) is not a mining problem; it's a processing problem. The US and Australia have the raw materials, but decades of offshoring have hollowed out the refining capacity. The ladder tightens at each stage: China mines 60% of magnet rare earths, refines 91%, and produces 94% of finished magnets. This means that even if the US builds robot assembly plants, the critical motor components depend on Chinese supply.

The implication for AI-driven reindustrialization is profound. The intelligence layer — the AI models controlling robots — is becoming commoditized through open-source distillation. But the physical layer — the motors, the magnets, the precision manufacturing — remains geographically concentrated. Musk's vision of ten billion robots is not limited by AI capability but by a supply chain that runs through a single country. Reindustrialization is not just about building factories; it's about rebuilding the entire mineral processing and manufacturing stack.
