## Elevator pitch
CO/AI's Signal/Noise analysis dissects the week's biggest AI safety story: Anthropic's alignment lead Evan Hubinger putting better-than-10% odds on AI killing every human this decade, a senior researcher resigning in protest, and CEO Dario Amodei's telling silence — arguing this pre-IPO candor window is closing fast and the silence of those with the most to lose is the real signal.

## Takeaways
- Anthropic alignment lead Evan Hubinger put better-than-10% odds on AI extinction this decade, backing resigning researcher Jacob Coxon's warning that both big labs are "gambling with our lives"
- CEO Dario Amodei — who wrote "Machines of Loving Grace" and spent years asking to be regulated — went silent during the week his own safety chief went on record with an extinction number
- Jim Cramer flipped from bullish on the Anthropic IPO to comparing the 10% odds to a surgery fatality rate that makes you cancel the operation
- CO/AI argues the expensive silence is the informative one: Amodei not speaking tells you more than Coxon speaking, because the prisoner's dilemma means no one who could slow down can afford to
- OpenAI used ~10,000 agents over 88 hours to solve the Navier-Stokes problem, then declined the $1M Millennium Prize — the flex is the pace, not the prize
- Meta shipped Muse for 3.6 billion users, saying "secure" 11 times in the press release — counting the word tells you where the real doubt is
- OECD study across 760,000 students in 91 countries: students who skip AI outperform those who lean on it, because "cognitive struggle" is the point of learning

## Synthesis
CO/AI's Signal/Noise delivers a sharp analytical lens on what may be the most consequential week in AI safety discourse. The core thesis is that the market should read Dario Amodei's silence — not Jacob Coxon's resignation or Evan Hubinger's 10% number — as the real signal. In markets, the expensive silence is the informative one. The two men with all the information and everything to lose have gone quiet or gone vague, while the junior researcher who screamed has little stock to protect.

The article frames this through the Kubrick Doomsday Machine metaphor: the terror was never that a villain would choose to fire it, but that the device took the human hand off the switch by design. Build, or be beaten — nobody who could pull the lever can afford to. CO/AI argues this is pre-IPO candor: the day these companies go public, the 10% becomes a risk-factor paragraph written by a lawyer, and safety leads stop giving interviews.

The practical advice is actionable and unsentimental: stress-test your 2027 plans for a hidden "pause bet" (flag every line that only pays off if AI slows down), harvest the candor before the S-1s land, and put kill switches in your own stack rather than waiting for Washington. The supplementary stories — OpenAI cracking Navier-Stokes with 10,000 agents, DeepMind mapping all 9 billion human DNA mutations, the OECD finding students do better without AI — round out a picture of acceleration that no regulation appears able to brake.
