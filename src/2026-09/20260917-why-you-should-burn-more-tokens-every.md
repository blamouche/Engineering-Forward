## Elevator pitch
Every CEO Dan Shipper consumes more than three times as many tokens as the next-highest user at the company, and that gap is a feature, not a bug: low token spend should be a smoke signal that teams are thinking too small, and expensive experimentation — even when it fails — is worth it if it reveals new capabilities or limitations.

## Takeaways
- **Low token spend is a smoke signal**: Dan Shipper consumes 3x more tokens than the next-highest user at Every. Rather than celebrating low AI bills, he sees them as a sign teams may be thinking too small. The goal isn't tokenmaxxing for sport — it's giving people room to experiment with radical new strategies that could deliver productivity gains justifying the cost.
- **A 4.5 billion token failure is still a good investment**: Head of video Randy Counsman burned 4.5 billion OpenAI tokens trying to make a 3D model of his face with Astra. The result was "very janky." But the failure revealed how his agent setup created a compute-hungry "unruly swarm" — an orchestrator deploying unlimited subagents that passed growing context back and forth even for simple status checks. He now has a benchmark and a leaner setup.
- **Three questions for every expensive run**: Head of operations Arielle Shipper asks: What did it cost? What did it buy us? And what did we learn? She monitors the company's ChatGPT auto-refill at $500, gets Slack notifications on every top-up, and messages the people at the top of the usage leaderboard when charges spike.
- **Don't send Fable to do a Sonnet's job**: Spiral general manager Marcus Moretti's strategy is to stay within Claude Code Max weekly limits by selectively routing work: basic tasks to Sonnet, tightly scoped changes to Opus, and Fable 5.1 only for larger features where it specifies what needs to be built and how. For complex work, Fable delegates implementation to cheaper models.
- **Infrastructure for spending transparency**: Every is building a skill that lets employees create and share AI spending P&L statements, while head of evals Mike Taylor is creating personal benchmarks for every employee to show which models — including less popular, cheaper ones — can handle recurring tasks. The goal: make spending and model choices more transparent without discouraging ambitious experiments.

## Synthesis
Every's Context Window newsletter makes the counterintuitive case that companies should worry less about how much they spend on AI tokens and more about whether their teams are experimenting enough. CEO Dan Shipper's token consumption — more than 3x the next-highest user — isn't framed as excess but as evidence that the rest of the company might be keeping AI bills low by thinking too small.

The centerpiece is a cautionary tale turned learning opportunity. Head of video Randy Counsman burned 4.5 billion OpenAI tokens (over a day and a half) trying to create a 3D model of his face using Astra, Blender, and a multi-agent orchestrator setup. The result was "very janky." An AI audit of his Codex sessions showed how his setup created a compute-hungry "unruly swarm" — he told an orchestrator to deploy agents "as needed" without limiting their number, and added an implementer agent that passed growing context between subagents even for simple status checks. He has since rebuilt: dropped the implementer, limited the orchestrator to five subagents, added pause points for human feedback, and generated a reference image for a judge agent to check against rather than following an open-ended "keep improving" instruction.

The article also covers the practical infrastructure Every is building around token spend. Head of operations Arielle Shipper evaluates expensive runs case by case using three questions: what did it cost, what did it buy, and what did we learn. The company keeps the ChatGPT auto-refill at $500 and monitors top-up frequency as an early-warning system. On the tooling side, Every is developing a skill for employees to create and share AI spending P&L statements, and head of evals Mike Taylor is building personal benchmarks for every employee to compare which models — including cheaper, less popular ones — can handle recurring tasks.

The model routing perspective from Spiral's Marcus Moretti rounds out the piece. His approach is to stay within Claude Code Max weekly limits by matching model to task: Sonnet for basic analytics checks, Opus for tightly scoped product changes, and Fable 5.1 reserved for larger features where it specifies what to build and how, then delegates implementation to cheaper models. The article includes a practical prompt for auditing token waste: review session history, break down usage by activity and model, look for repeated work and unnecessary status checks, and recommend the three most useful changes to instructions, model choices, or agent setup.
