# Nubank's £10B Monzo Bid and the First AI Agent Selloff on Wall Street

**Author**: Linas Sekeriatis

## Elevator pitch
Linas breaks down Nubank's £8-10B bid for Monzo (why stock carries most of the offer, why Monzo lends out less than 10p per pound held, and why Nubank's 22.9% margins won't survive UK rules) and the first AI agent selloff on Wall Street — Goldman Sachs' "consumer inertia" basket that lost 7% in six sessions as the market repriced stocks by agent access.

## Takeaways
- Nubank (Nu Holdings) is in early talks to buy UK digital bank Monzo for £8-10B, roughly double Monzo's last valuation — the price only works if Nubank can turn Monzo's £25.7B of mostly unlent deposits into loans, which it has never done outside Latin America
- Stock will have to carry most of the offer because Nubank's currency is its equity — Nu Holdings has been one of the best-performing fintech stocks, making a stock-heavy deal attractive to Monzo's backers
- Monzo lends out less than 10p of every pound it holds, meaning its deposit base is vastly underutilised — Nubank's 22.9% margins come from aggressive lending in Brazil, but UK lending regulations (capital requirements, responsible lending rules) will compress those margins significantly
- Nubank already runs a stablecoin account in more than 35 countries without a banking licence in any of them — licence-free expansion cannot replace this deal because the real asset is Monzo's UK banking licence and its deposit base
- Goldman Sachs built a "consumer inertia" basket of stocks that benefit from customers not switching — it lost 7% in six sessions as AI agents threaten to automate comparison, switching, and negotiation
- Schwab, Allstate, and Planet Fitness sold off while Shopify and Meta surged — the market is now applying a new valuation test: does this company benefit from agent access or is it threatened by it?
- The market's new valuation framework for agent access means consumer-facing stocks are being repriced based on whether AI agents will help them acquire customers or help customers leave them
- Bonus deep dives include Grok Bot as the first real AI agent OS, the Claude Code Projects Playbook (making Claude your chief of staff), and how to use Jev (TypeSafe's decision model)

## Synthesis
The Nubank-Monzo deal and the AI agent selloff on Wall Street are two stories about the same phenomenon: the erosion of friction as a business moat. Nubank's entire expansion strategy — entering 35 countries without a banking licence via stablecoins — was built on removing the regulatory friction that traditionally gates financial services. The Monzo acquisition is the opposite move: buying a banking licence because the friction of UK deposit-taking requires it. The irony is that Nubank is paying double Monzo's valuation for the regulatory friction it spent years avoiding, which means the licence is now worth more than the technology.

The Goldman Sachs "consumer inertia" basket crystallises a new investment thesis: companies whose profits depend on customers not switching are structurally short AI agents. If an agent can compare insurance policies, brokerages, or gym memberships in seconds, the inertia that sustained Allstate, Schwab, and Planet Fitness evaporates. The 7% selloff in six sessions is the market pricing in a future where the cost of comparison drops to near zero — the same dynamic that Amazon brought to retail, but applied to every consumer-facing service simultaneously. The flip side — Shopify and Meta surging — reflects the thesis that agent-driven commerce benefits platforms that facilitate transactions rather than those that gate them.

The convergence of these two stories suggests that the fintech and AI narratives are now inseparable. Nubank's stablecoin expansion was an early instance of removing human friction from financial services; MPP and agent-driven payments are the next step. The market is beginning to price this convergence: companies are being valued not just on their current earnings but on whether AI agents will strengthen or weaken their customer relationships. The "consumer inertia" basket is the first explicit attempt to quantify this, and its 7% decline suggests the market believes inertia is a dying moat.
