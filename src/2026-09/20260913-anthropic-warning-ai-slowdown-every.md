# What to Make of the Anthropic Warning: AI Slowdown Goes Mainstream
**Source**: Every (hello@every.to)
**Date**: 2026-09-13
**Author**: Kaushik Viswanath (Every)
**Keywords**: Anthropic, Dario Amodei, AI safety, AI risk, Jacob Coxon, responsible scaling, AI slowdown, Navier-Stokes, OpenAI, personal benchmarks, evals, AI skills erosion

## Elevator pitch
After a former Anthropic engineer resigned with a public warning that AI could wipe out humanity (endorsed by Anthropic's own alignment lead at >10% odds), CEO Dario Amodei published an essay arguing AI companies should deliberately slow model improvement — endorsed hours later by OpenAI's Sam Altman and Elon Musk. Every's editorial team discussed what to make of it, finding more book-club skepticism than fire-drill alarm, while separately covering personal benchmarks, skills erosion from agent use, and the Navier-Stokes proof's implications.

## Takeaways
- Jacob Coxon, a former Anthropic engineer, resigned with a public warning that AI could wipe out humanity; Anthropic's alignment science lead endorsed the warning, putting odds above 10%
- Dario Amodei published an essay arguing AI companies should deliberately slow model improvement because advances outrun understanding; Sam Altman endorsed it and committed OpenAI to independent evaluators with employee-like access; Musk agreed
- Every's editorial team was largely skeptical: Mike Taylor compared it to GPT-2 release fears and Google's "sentient chatbot" engineer — labs hire people prone to worry, so loud exits say more about recruiting than models
- Kieran Klaassen framed it as the system working as designed: Anthropic's responsible scaling policy is built to make room for bell-ringers; Kate Lee called it a communications disaster despite being the system working as intended
- Brian Dell challenged the historical argument: if risk is ~10%, a decade of nothing is roughly what you'd expect, and tells little about whether the number is right — the real danger may be mundane (a security hole in mass-produced humanoid robots)
- Kieran Klaassen's "To Read—Or Not to Read the Code?" addresses skills erosion: researchers found extended AI agent use erodes oversight skills; his answer is to keep learning the work agents do for you (four habits, including having the model quiz you after sessions)
- Every is building personal benchmarks for every employee — custom evals testing how well a model performs specific parts of your job, graded against your own standard
- Ashwin Sharma on Navier-Stokes: OpenAI's proposed solution shows AI can make breakthroughs that generations of mathematicians couldn't; skeptics of AGI are reconsidering; he expects no breathing room before the next breakthrough

## Synthesis
Kaushik Viswanath reports on Every's internal editorial discussion following Jacob Coxon's resignation from Anthropic with a public warning about AI extinction risk — endorsed by Anthropic's own alignment science lead at above 10% odds. The piece captures the range of reactions: Mike Taylor's historical skepticism (GPT-2 was once too dangerous to release; labs hire people prone to worry), Kieran Klaassen's systemic framing (the responsible scaling policy is built to make room for bell-ringers, so a public resignation is the system working as designed), and Brian Dell's statistical counter (a decade of nothing is exactly what you'd expect at 10% risk, so it proves nothing either way). Viswanath himself finds rogue agents wreaking havoc plausible but struggles to connect that to human extinction.

The newsletter also covers Kieran Klaassen's "To Read—Or Not to Read the Code?" which addresses a concrete problem: researchers have found that extended use of AI agents erodes the very skills oversight depends on. Klaassen's answer is four habits to keep learning alongside the agents, including having the model quiz you after a long session. Every is also building personal benchmarks (evals) for every employee — custom tests of how well a model performs specific parts of your job, graded against your own standard — with Mike Taylor finding a smaller model could handle much of his daily work.

Ashwin Sharma's Alignment column reflects on the Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize solution: OpenAI's proposed proof shows AI can make breakthroughs that generations of mathematicians couldn't settle, and skeptics of AGI are reconsidering. He expects no breathing room before the next comparable breakthrough, given exponential AI acceleration. Seven Thesis Statements from frontier builders are included, with predictions ranging from "Powered by AI" will mean cheap (ÌníOlúwa Abíódún) to certainty will be the trap (Alex Tryon).
