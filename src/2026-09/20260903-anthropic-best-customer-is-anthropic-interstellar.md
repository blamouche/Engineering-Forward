# Anthropic's Best Customer May Just Be Anthropic — The Frontier Is a Star, Not a Business

## Elevator pitch
Ramp data reveals 80% of OpenAI's and Anthropic's business revenue comes from 1% of customers — all AI companies themselves — meaning the frontier AI market is a reflexive loop, not a sustainable business, and everyone else should buy the cheapest model that clears the bar.

## Takeaways
- 80% of OpenAI's and Anthropic's business revenue comes from 1% of customers, a concentration risk "unseen in any other software category" per Ramp
- The 1% are AI companies themselves: labs training models, startups writing code, quants trading — creating a reflexive loop where spending tokens generates capital to spend more tokens
- Fable 5.1 cost more to train than Fable 5, sells to the same ring of buyers, and Anthropic cut cache prices 75% on launch day to attract non-frontier buyers
- Google shipped its third Flash model in six weeks at the same price; zero Pro models shipped all year — a verdict on who pays for the frontier
- OpenAI cut off Cursor (after SpaceX bought it) while plugging ChatGPT into Epic's medical records and crossing $1B ad run rate — labs are eating their own customers
- Token index hit 97 cents; the frontier's price is coming down because the 1% can't carry the next model alone
- Tunguz's analysis: AI improved his 10th-percentile writing twice as much as his 90th — the ROI for most work is doing what you did badly somewhat better, on the cheapest model that clears the bar

## Synthesis
The core insight from this CO/AI Signal/Noise piece is that the frontier AI market exhibits a dangerous reflexivity pattern. Ramp's data shows 80% of business revenue at OpenAI and Anthropic comes from 1% of customers, and those customers are overwhelmingly other AI companies — labs training the next model, startups writing code, and quants trading. Each turn of the loop costs more: Fable 5.1 cost more to train than Fable 5, sells to the same ring of buyers, and Anthropic's first move on launch day was cutting cache prices 75% to get anyone else in the door. Giovanni Cattani's taxonomy shows the frontier's demand is essentially three jobs — AI research, writing software, and trading — roughly half of all frontier inference revenue, and every one of them reflexive.

The analogy the authors draw is to stellar nucleosynthesis: each stage burns hotter and shorter until it reaches iron, the element that costs more to fuse than it gives back. The frontier is that star, and what it throws off — bounded work like taxes, tickets, reports — is what everyone else builds with. Tunguz's self-analysis of five years of writing confirms this: AI didn't make him faster at good work (his line edits held at 136 per post), but it deleted his bad drafts. The 10th-percentile piece improved twice as much as the 90th. That's the ROI for most of the world, and it goes to the cheapest model that clears the bar.

The strategic recommendations are sharp: don't sign multi-year frontier deals when prices are dropping to find you; point agents at the work you do badly rather than the work you do well; and run the risk factor on your own book — if your revenue depends on customers who are themselves funded by the AI trade, you own the loop whether you meant to or not. The piece also highlights that OpenAI is simultaneously cutting off customers (Cursor) and expanding into new verticals (Epic healthcare records, $1B ad run rate), suggesting the labs' relationship with their own customers is increasingly extractive.
