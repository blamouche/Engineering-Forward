# Proof Indigestion: Terence Tao on AI Generating Proofs Humans Can't Verify
**Source**: https://every.to
**Date**: 2026-08-30
**Author**: Every Staff
**Keywords**: Terence Tao, mathematical proofs, AI proofs, proof indigestion, Lean, verification, mathematics

## Elevator pitch
Terence Tao, one of the world's greatest mathematicians, warns that AI is now generating apparently correct proofs faster than humans can verify them — creating a "proof indigestion" problem where the bottleneck has shifted from finding proofs to understanding them.

## Takeaways
- Terence Tao identifies a new problem: AI generates proofs faster than humans can verify them
- AI produced a proof of Sendov's conjecture (a longstanding problem about polynomial roots) that passed all 90,000 lines but was barely understandable to humans
- Tao's human version ran to about 15,000 lines and was "remarkably elementary" using surprisingly basic tools
- The division of labor between humans and machines in mathematics is changing — humans may become translators of AI proofs
- This parallels the AI-native SDLC problem: when AI produces output too complex for human review, the verification bottleneck becomes the issue

## Synthesis
Terence Tao's concept of "proof indigestion" names a problem that is emerging across multiple domains where AI produces output: the bottleneck has shifted from production to verification. In mathematics, AI is now generating proofs that are apparently correct — they pass formal verification systems — but are too complex for humans to understand.

The specific example is telling. Lech Mazur produced a proof of Sendov's conjecture (a longstanding problem about the roots of polynomials) formalized in Lean. The proof passed all 90,000 lines of verification but was barely understandable to a human. Tao worked through it with AI, pen, and paper, and found the core idea. His version ran to about 15,000 lines and used "surprisingly basic tools" — the problem had resisted experts for many decades, yet the approach was elementary once the core idea was extracted.

This is the "proof indigestion" problem: AI can produce correct results that humans cannot digest. The mathematical proof is correct (formally verified), but no human can follow the reasoning. To make the AI proof useful, a human had to translate it into something other humans could understand, explain, and build upon. That shows how the division of labor between humans and machines may change: humans become translators of AI proofs rather than primary proof authors.

Carl Friedrich Gauss called mathematics "the queen of the sciences" because every other STEM field is built on the language of mathematics. If AI generates materials and drugs that work before anyone understands why — as Tao notes — the implications extend far beyond mathematics. Proof indigestion is the mathematical instance of a broader pattern: AI systems produce correct outputs whose reasoning humans cannot follow, creating a verification gap that grows wider as AI capabilities increase.
