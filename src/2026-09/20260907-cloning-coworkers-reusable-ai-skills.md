## Synthesis
The concept of "cloning your coworkers" sounds dystopian until you parse what it actually means: capturing the professional judgment of your best people as reusable AI skills, so that their expertise persists and scales across the organization. Every is running this experiment on its own team, and the implications for organizational knowledge management are significant.

The technical approach builds on the "skills" paradigm that has emerged in AI-native workflows. A skill is a reusable prompt or workflow that encodes how a specific task should be done. What's new is applying this to capture human judgment rather than just automate tool use. Instead of a skill that "writes a meeting summary," you create a skill that "writes a meeting summary the way Sarah does it" — incorporating Sarah's priorities, her sense of what matters, her editorial judgment.

The value proposition is clear: institutional knowledge is one of the most valuable and most fragile assets in any organization. When a key person leaves, their judgment goes with them. If that judgment is captured as an AI skill, it persists. New team members can leverage it. The skill becomes part of the organization's infrastructure rather than an individual's tacit knowledge.

The questions this raises are equally significant. Who owns a skill derived from a person's professional judgment? What consent is required? Does "cloning" a coworker's expertise create a competitive dynamic where the person trains their replacement? These are not hypothetical concerns — they are the next frontier of workplace AI ethics. The technology is ready; the governance frameworks are not. Organizations experimenting with this approach will need to navigate these questions carefully, or risk the trust erosion that makes any knowledge-capture initiative fail.
