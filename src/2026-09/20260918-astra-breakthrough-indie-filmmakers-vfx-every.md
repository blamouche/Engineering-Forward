# Vibe Check: Is Astra a Breakthrough for Indie Filmmakers?
**Source**: Every (hello@every.to) — https://every.to/p/vibe-check-is-astra-a-breakthrough-for-indie-filmmakers
**Date**: 2026-09-18
**Author**: Cyrus Duff and Josh Lee (Afterimage / Every)
**Keywords**: GPT-6 Astra, VFX, visual effects, indie filmmaking, Afterimage, masking, tracking, Blender, Nuke, 3D modeling, video compositing, Sol, creative studio

## Elevator pitch
Creative studio Afterimage puts GPT-6 Astra through real-world VFX tests on footage from actual commercial shoots — masking, tracking, compositing 3D objects into live video, and creating impossible events in casual iPhone footage. Astra handles rigid geometry and compositing tasks well enough to prototype VFX without a specialist, but struggles with organic forms and scene interactions. The verdict: a genuine breakthrough for small studios that expands what's aesthetically possible, not just what's practical.

## Takeaways
- **Astra outperforms Sol on foundational VFX skills**: In masking tests (separating a 16mm boxer from his background) and tracking tests (following a cooking pan through a shot), Astra produced more coherent outlines and usable masks. Astra chose Robust Video Matting and wrote custom Python to refine edges; Sol relied on GrabCut and left artifacts. Neither was perfect, but Astra's output was workable with follow-up prompts — Sol's was not.
- **Astra can drive professional VFX software autonomously**: Astra assembled a final composite in Nuke (professional compositing software) via its Python scripting API, controlling the software to keep replacement food behind an actor's arms and sleeves. The authors never touched Nuke themselves — Astra carried out all revisions while they directed.
- **3D modeling and compositing into real footage works well for rigid objects**: Astra rendered a 3D building into a Brooklyn street shot from a Reve commercial, matching camera tracking, softness, and texture. A wineglass added to a table showed convincing transparency, reflections, and distortion. A Snoopy float composited into handheld iPhone footage used SAM 3.1 for masking, Runway's Aleph 2.0 for texture, and Blender for the 3D model.
- **Organic forms and scene interactions remain hard**: Eyes grown from fingertips looked "tacked on" rather than merged into skin. Cows added to a drone shot of a field didn't integrate — grass didn't overlap hooves, mouths didn't pull at grass, bodies didn't deform naturally in motion. Repeated revisions didn't fix these issues.
- **The real breakthrough is aesthetic, not just practical**: The most interesting consequence is that Astra unlocks types of images that didn't previously exist — combining casual iPhone footage with visual maximalism. Small studios can now prototype and execute VFX without upfront budgeting or hiring specialists, widening creative possibilities for independent filmmakers.

## Synthesis
Every's Vibe Check column invited Afterimage — a creative studio that works with tech companies like Cursor, Reve, and Turbopuffer — to test GPT-6 Astra on real VFX workloads. The cofounders, Cyrus Duff and Josh Lee, previously led video at the Browser Company of New York. Their question was simple: could Astra handle the visual effects they'd normally make themselves, and if so, would it be a breakthrough for independent filmmakers and small studios?

The article is structured as a series of practical experiments comparing Astra and Sol on the foundational skills of VFX work: masking (separating subjects from backgrounds) and tracking (following objects across frames). In both tests, Astra produced superior results. For masking, Astra used Robust Video Matting and wrote custom Python code to exclude shadows and remove background traces around soft edges, while Sol's GrabCut approach left artifacts. For tracking, Astra followed foreground boundaries more closely through a cooking shot where hands and sleeves crossed the frame. The authors emphasize that neither model was perfect, but Astra's output was usable with follow-up prompts while Sol's was not.

The most striking result is Astra's ability to drive professional VFX software autonomously. When compositing a replacement dish into a cooking pan, Astra assembled the final composite in Nuke — industry-standard compositing software — through its Python scripting API. The authors never touched the software themselves; they directed revisions while Astra executed them. This represents a meaningful shift in the division of labor between human creative direction and machine execution.

Astra's 3D modeling capabilities carried over well to compositing rigid geometric objects into real footage. A building rendered in Blender was composited into a Brooklyn street shot with matched camera tracking and texture. A wineglass added to a table showed convincing transparency, with reflections and distortion through the glass. A Snoopy float appeared outside a window in handheld iPhone footage, combining SAM 3.1 for masking, Runway's Aleph 2.0 for texture, and Blender for the 3D model. An Afterimage gold sphere flew into a scene, landed on a rug, and opened to reveal a recessed wordmark — all controllable through prompts.

The limits are equally clear. Organic forms — eyes growing from fingertips, cows in a field — didn't convince. The eyes looked tacked on rather than merged into skin; the cows didn't interact with grass, didn't deform naturally in motion, and lacked weight in their steps. Repeated revisions didn't resolve these issues, suggesting these are architectural limitations rather than prompt-engineering problems. Scene interactions that require small physical relationships — skin stretching around blinking eyes, grass responding to hooves — remain beyond what Astra can achieve convincingly.

The authors' conclusion is that Astra represents a genuine breakthrough for small studios, but the most interesting consequence is aesthetic rather than practical. In a pre-Astra world, they would never have thought to add VFX to casual iPhone clips. The combination of casual footage with visual maximalism feels genuinely new. The ability to prototype and execute VFX without upfront planning or specialist hiring widens what small studios can offer clients and unlocks types of images that didn't previously exist.
