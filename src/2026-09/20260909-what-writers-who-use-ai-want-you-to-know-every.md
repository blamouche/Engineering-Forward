# What Writers Who Use AI Want You to Know
**Source**: https://every.to/essays/what-writers-who-use-ai-want-you-to-know
**Date**: 2026-09-09
**Author**: Every (Laura Entis)
**Keywords**: AI writing, professional writers, Compound Writing, Kevin Roose, Maggie Appleton, Dan Shipper, writing workflow, AI-assisted writing

## Elevator pitch
Five professional writers — including NYT alum Kevin Roose, designer Maggie Appleton, and Every CEO Dan Shipper — share their distinct, idiosyncratic AI-assisted writing workflows, revealing that writing with AI is a skill in its own right: the same tool that helps one writer think more clearly makes another's prose worse.

## Takeaways
- There is no universal recipe for AI-assisted writing: the same tool that helps one writer think more clearly makes another's prose worse
- Kevin Roose used AI to research, transcribe, and fact-check his 448-page AGI Chronicles in one year instead of three to five, using queryable notebooks as an "outboard brain" for 150+ interviews
- Maggie Appleton uses dictation mode to brain-dump notes, then asks Claude Code to surface connections she has overlooked and fill research gaps
- Dan Shipper's Compound Writing plugin brings AI into the entire writing process, from developing an idea to sentence-level edits, with multiple AI reviewers and feedback selection
- Alexandra Samuel wrote 52,000 words in 10 days using AI as a conversational partner to break writer's block — rough material that gave her momentum for a final manuscript
- Writers still struggle to reliably identify which parts of the process AI makes easier without making their writing worse — the boundary between help and crutch is personal and shifting

## Synthesis
Laura Entis's piece for Every is a nuanced exploration of how professional writers actually use AI — not the polemical "AI and writing" discourse that dominates online, but granular, honest accounts from five working writers with distinct workflows. The article's core insight is that writing with AI is a genuine skill: knowing when and how to use it is as much a craft as writing well.

Kevin Roose's experience is the most quantitatively dramatic: his 448-page AGI Chronicles, based on 150+ interviews, took roughly a year instead of an estimated three to five. His AI pipeline automated transcription, extracted key details, identified potential sources (sometimes exploiting security flaws to find contact information), and served as a queryable "outboard brain." The governance challenge — keeping sensitive source information away from the very companies he was reporting on — shows that AI-assisted journalism introduces new operational security considerations.

Maggie Appleton's approach is more exploratory: using AI to surface intellectual connections she's missed, re-introducing her to relevant work from her own field. Dan Shipper's Compound Writing plugin represents the most systematic integration — AI at every stage from ideation to editing. The throughline is that none of these writers has a universal recipe; the skill is in knowing your own process well enough to identify where AI helps and where it hurts.
