## Elevator pitch
Three of the biggest law firms — Kirkland & Ellis ($500M), Morgan & Morgan ($1B over 10 years), and Latham & Watkins (buying its own Nvidia servers) — are building their own AI in-house rather than renting frontier intelligence. Even Harvey, the best-funded legal-AI vendor, is climbing off the frontier models it was built on. The signal is clear: customers with proprietary data and an honest eval loop are walking off the labs' land, while everyone else rents good-enough thinking by the sip. The market splits clean between sovereigns and tenants.

## Synthesis
CO/AI's Signal/Noise column identifies a structural shift in the AI market that's happening faster than most people realize. The trigger is three simultaneous moves by the biggest names in law: Kirkland's $500M in-house build, Morgan & Morgan's $1B decade-long commitment, and Latham buying its own Nvidia servers to fine-tune open-weight models on sensitive client data. The authors argue that one firm doing this is a headline, but three is a trend — and it's accelerating.

The analysis cuts to the economic logic. The frontier models (Anthropic, OpenAI) have a genuine moat — they're the best intelligence on earth. But the frontier is overkill for 98% of legal work. Contract review, intake memos, and support queues can be handled by a good open-weight model that the firm controls. Pay the frontier premium for the 2% of work that needs it; for the other 98%, you're renting something you could own.

The most provocative claim comes from Alexandr Wang: a well-built agent swarm out-produces 100 senior engineers, and the underlying machine is "just markdown files, cron jobs, goal, metrics, data." The magic isn't the model — it's the eval loop. This aligns with the Gartner finding that 40% of agentic AI projects will fail by 2027, not because of model quality but because nobody could define what success looks like. The companies pulling ahead are the ones with an honest eval loop and the data to prove it.

The market implication is a clean split: firms with proprietary data and an honest eval walk off the labs' land and take the crown jewels. Everybody else rents good-enough thinking by the sip. The labs keep the frontier but watch the highest-value work get built in-house on cheaper models. The Ninth Circuit ruling — that AI agents act on behalf of users, not developers — adds a legal dimension: when your agent signs or sends, you're liable, which makes owning the stack even more attractive.
