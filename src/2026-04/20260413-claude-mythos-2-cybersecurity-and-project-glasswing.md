# Claude Mythos #2: Cybersecurity and Project Glasswing

**Date**: April 13, 2026
**Author**: Zvi Mowshowitz
**Keywords**: Anthropic, Mythos, cybersecurity, Project Glasswing, AI safety

## Elevator pitch
Zvi examines Anthropic’s decision to keep Mythos tightly gated for defensive cybersecurity work, arguing that frontier cyber capability has become dangerous enough that selective release and coordinated patching may be the least bad option.

## Takeaways
- Zvi examines Anthropic’s decision to keep Mythos tightly gated for defensive cybersecurity work, arguing that frontier cyber capability has become dangerous enough that selective release and coordinated patching may be the least bad option.
- Zvi’s essay takes Anthropic’s Mythos and Project Glasswing at face value as evidence that frontier models have crossed into a new cyber regime: one where end-to-end vulnerability discovery and exploitation is strong enough that broad public release becomes hard to defend. The piece is less about benchmark performance than about the governance implications of shipping such a model.
- The article highlights Anthropic’s selective release strategy—credits for critical-software partners, vulnerability hunting, and months of coordinated patching—as a defensive race against the window in which these capabilities exist but systems are not yet hardened. In that framing, limited access is not just a product tactic but a containment tactic.
- Whether one agrees with all of Zvi’s conclusions, the useful takeaway is that frontier model release policy may increasingly fragment by domain. Public general-purpose access, closed high-risk access, and partner-only vertical deployment could become standard tiers once capabilities move faster than existing institutions can absorb.

## Synthesis

Zvi’s essay takes Anthropic’s Mythos and Project Glasswing at face value as evidence that frontier models have crossed into a new cyber regime: one where end-to-end vulnerability discovery and exploitation is strong enough that broad public release becomes hard to defend. The piece is less about benchmark performance than about the governance implications of shipping such a model.

The article highlights Anthropic’s selective release strategy—credits for critical-software partners, vulnerability hunting, and months of coordinated patching—as a defensive race against the window in which these capabilities exist but systems are not yet hardened. In that framing, limited access is not just a product tactic but a containment tactic.

Whether one agrees with all of Zvi’s conclusions, the useful takeaway is that frontier model release policy may increasingly fragment by domain. Public general-purpose access, closed high-risk access, and partner-only vertical deployment could become standard tiers once capabilities move faster than existing institutions can absorb.
