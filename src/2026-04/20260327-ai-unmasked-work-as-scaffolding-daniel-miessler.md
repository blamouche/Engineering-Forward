# AI Unmasked Our Work as Scaffolding
**Source**: https://danielmiessler.com/blog/ai-unmasked-our-work-as-scaffolding
**Date**: March 27, 2026
**Author**: Daniel Miessler
**Keywords**: knowledge work, scaffolding, AI disruption, cybersecurity, consulting, software development, commoditization

## Elevator pitch
AI is revealing that 75-99% of knowledge work consists of scaffolding overhead — process maintenance rather than genuine expertise — and that AI's ability to handle scaffolding will fundamentally restructure professional services.

## Takeaways
- 75-99% of knowledge work across domains is scaffolding: context-building, tooling maintenance, report formatting, process overhead
- In cybersecurity, 99% of testing time is not finding new vulnerabilities but context-building and workflow management
- In software development, most time is peripheral infrastructure work rather than actual coding
- High-end consulting primarily applies templated solutions with only 2-12% situation-specific insights
- AI crushes scaffolding work by packaging context, methodologies, and knowledge bases into reproducible systems

## Synthesis
Miessler's scaffolding thesis is a specific and provocative claim: that the activities people perform in their expert roles consist predominantly of work that is necessary to enable the occasional genuinely difficult task, but that this maintenance work has been systematically conflated with expertise itself.

The cybersecurity example is the sharpest illustration. The impression of security work is that it involves sophisticated vulnerability hunting, creative attack surface analysis, and novel exploit development. The reality for most practitioners is that the vast majority of their time is spent on activities that are prerequisites for that work: building context about the target organization's infrastructure, maintaining and configuring testing tools, managing workflow, and formatting reports. The expert insight — identifying a novel vulnerability chain, constructing an exploit — is a small fraction of total time that is difficult to separate from the scaffolding because they are interleaved.

The consulting observation is the most economically significant. McKinsey-style consulting commands premium rates on the premise of bespoke expert analysis tailored to each client's situation. Miessler argues that the bespoke element is a small fraction of the actual work — the rest is applying well-documented frameworks and templates that have been applied hundreds of times before. If AI can handle the template application, the remaining differentiator is the 2-12% of genuine situation-specific insight, which may not command the same premium when clients can see exactly how much of the work required genuine expertise.

The mechanism by which AI commoditizes scaffolding is important to understand precisely. AI doesn't replace the entire process — it replaces the rote, reproducible components by packaging them (context, methodology, knowledge base) into systems that can be invoked without the scaffolding overhead. This makes the ratio of expert insight to total work time higher, potentially making expert work more satisfying. But it also means that organizations purchasing expert services are no longer paying for the scaffolding portion, which compresses revenue proportional to how much scaffolding the service consisted of.

For professionals, the implication is that genuine differentiation increasingly requires identifying and investing in the non-scaffolding portion of their work — the judgment, insight, and novel thinking that AI cannot yet replicate — rather than efficiency in scaffolding management.
