# Help! My boss is addicted to ChatGPT and Claude

**Source**: https://sifted.eu/articles/help-my-boss-is-addicted-to-ai
**Date**: April 29, 2026
**Author**: Éanna Kelly
**Keywords**: AI, management, workplace, communication, startup culture

## Elevator pitch
Senior leaders are increasingly using chatbots to communicate with employees, and many employees experience the result as inauthentic, lazy or undermining.

## Takeaways
- The article documents growing frustration with managers who rely on ChatGPT or Claude for routine internal communication.
- Employees often read AI-written messages as a signal that the sender did not care enough to respond personally.
- AI becomes more problematic when managers use it to critique or second-guess employees’ work instead of making their own judgment calls.
- Some founders are adjusting by mixing AI assistance with more personal language and context.
- The broader issue is not tool usage itself, but whether AI removes accountability, authenticity and trust from workplace relationships.

## Synthesis
This Sifted article examines an understated but important workplace effect of generative AI: the social cost of leaders outsourcing their voice to chatbots. The examples are mundane on purpose, covering things like sick-leave replies, team announcements and feedback loops. That is exactly why the piece lands. AI in management does not need to fail spectacularly to damage trust. It can simply make everyday communication feel impersonal, evasive or performative.

The strongest point in the article is that employees are usually quick to detect the pattern. Once a message feels templated or overly polished, the recipient often interprets it as a sign of low effort rather than efficiency. In that sense, AI-assisted communication changes the meaning of the message. What might once have been seen as a basic act of consideration starts to look like an automated transaction.

The article also highlights a more serious use case: managers who use models to question or validate an employee’s work before forming their own opinion. That shifts AI from a drafting tool into a shadow authority. When Claude or ChatGPT becomes a kind of second boss, the problem is not only tone. It is that leaders can offload judgment while still preserving the appearance of oversight. That creates frustration because employees end up defending their work against a system that has no real accountability for its critiques.

The practical lesson is not that managers should avoid AI entirely. Several examples suggest that employees accept AI help when it supports, rather than replaces, human intent. The line seems to be authenticity. If leaders use AI to save time but keep ownership of tone, context and decisions, the tool remains invisible. If they let the model speak for them, the efficiency gain can come at the expense of credibility, morale and trust.
