# The Zig project's rationale for their firm anti-AI contribution policy

**Source**: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Apr/30/zig-anti-ai
**Date**: April 30, 2026
**Author**: Simon Willison
**Keywords**: open-source, llms, zig, governance, contributors

## Elevator pitch
Simon Willison highlights Zig's strict anti-LLM contribution policy as a deliberate bet that open source projects should optimize for developing trusted contributors, not merely landing more code.

## Takeaways
- Zig bans LLM use across issues, pull requests, and tracker comments, including AI-based translation.
- The rationale is that review time should help cultivate reliable human contributors rather than validate machine-generated patches.
- Loris Cro frames open source maintenance as 'betting on the contributor, not the first PR.'
- The policy contrasts with companies such as Bun, which rely heavily on AI assistance and therefore do not expect easy upstreaming into Zig.
- The debate reframes AI contribution policies around reviewer return on effort, not only code quality.

## Synthesis
Simon Willison points to Zig as one of the clearest examples of an open source project drawing a hard line against LLM-assisted contribution. What makes the post interesting is not the existence of the ban itself, but the reasoning behind it. Drawing on Loris Cro's explanation, the argument is that successful projects eventually receive more pull requests than maintainers can productively process. In that context, review is not just a filtering mechanism for code quality. It is also an investment in building up a pool of trusted contributors who understand the project and can become long-term collaborators. If a patch is largely generated by an LLM, that investment dynamic breaks down. The maintainer may still spend significant effort reviewing the change, but that effort does little to strengthen the contributor behind it. This framing shifts the debate away from simplistic claims about whether AI code is good or bad and toward a more structural question about community development. Zig's position is that contributor growth is the scarce asset, not raw patch volume. The piece is a useful reminder that open source governance decisions often optimize for project sustainability and reviewer leverage, not just throughput.
