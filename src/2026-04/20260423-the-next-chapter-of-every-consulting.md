# The Next Chapter of Every Consulting
**Source**: https://every.to/on-every/the-next-chapter-of-every-consulting
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Natalia Quintero
**Keywords**: AI adoption, consulting, enterprise automation, finance, compound engineering

## Elevator pitch
Every Consulting uses its own client work to argue that AI adoption is best understood as a maturity curve, where the biggest gains come from documenting valuable workflows and turning them into increasingly autonomous systems.

## Takeaways
- The article presents AI adoption as a four-level maturity model, from simple chat usage to agent-centric work environments.
- Every argues that partial automation already delivers major value, so companies do not need to jump straight to full autonomy.
- The best starting point depends on where high-value, documentable work sits inside a company, not on which department seems most glamorous.
- Industry differences matter: finance and tech firms vary in data richness, compliance, and where automation can create leverage.
- The practical recommendation is to document repetitive high-value tasks first, then evolve them from prompts to GPTs, skills, or scheduled agents.

## Synthesis
Natalia Quintero’s article is essentially a packaging of consulting lessons into an operating model for enterprise AI adoption. The strongest idea is that companies should stop treating AI transformation as a binary choice between casual experimentation and fully autonomous agents. Instead, they should see it as a progression through distinct maturity levels. That reframing matters because it makes adoption less theatrical and more manageable. A company can move from ad hoc chat use to repeatable workflows, then to defined agents, and only later to environments where agents become the primary interface to work.

The four-level maturity model is especially helpful because it maps technical progress to organizational readiness. At level one, people use ChatGPT or Claude as a better search box or drafting partner. At level two, they start creating custom prompts or agents for recurring tasks, but they still supervise closely. At level three, an agent can own a well-bounded job with less permissioning friction. Level four is the most radical state, where the agent environment becomes the workspace itself and the human focuses more on orchestration than on direct task execution. Whether or not companies reach that final stage soon, the model gives them a language for discussing where they are and what must change next.

The article is also pragmatic about where automation actually works. Every’s consulting work suggests that the best entry point is not always the most prestigious team. In one case, the back office of a hedge fund offered more leverage than the investment front office because operational bottlenecks were limiting the rest of the organization. That is a useful correction to AI strategy that starts from hype rather than process analysis. The right question is not “Where can we showcase AI?” but “Where do repetitive, high-value, documentable tasks sit?”

Another strong thread is the emphasis on documentation as the substrate of automation. Tasks that can be described clearly, with repeatable criteria and usable data inputs, are the ones that move fastest up the maturity curve. That fits the article’s examples: investment memos, earnings analysis, content transformation, product-feedback synthesis. In each case, the firm translates tacit know-how into structured instructions and data access so an agent can do real work.

Overall, the piece extends Every’s broader thesis that AI adoption is less about prompt cleverness than about turning human workflows into explicit systems. The immediate value is not in building magical agents from scratch, but in identifying painful repetitive work, documenting how good outcomes are produced, and then progressively operationalizing that logic. The consulting pitch is obvious, but the underlying framework is solid: AI maturity is earned through workflow design, not declared through enthusiasm.
