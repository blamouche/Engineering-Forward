# Straight Lines on Graphs: AI Progress Follows Regular Patterns
**Source**: https://joelbkr.substack.com/p/straight-lines-on-graphs
**Date**: March 31, 2026
**Author**: Joel Becker
**Keywords**: AI progress, METR, timelines, straight lines, compute slowdown, reinforcement learning, forecasting

## Elevator pitch
Despite uneven capabilities across domains, AI progress follows remarkably regular patterns across task domains according to METR data — straight lines on log-scale graphs — raising questions about whether RL created a step-change or merely revealed favorable measurement artifacts.

## Takeaways
- Most people initially doubt regular AI progress patterns, then observe they predict outcomes better than intuition
- METR data shows similar improvement trajectories across different task domains despite jagged absolute performance levels
- Skeptical of RL-as-step-change narrative: alternative explanations include measurement task correlation with training tasks and statistical noise
- Pre-training advances produce broad cross-domain improvements; post-training produces targeted domain-specific gains
- Compute growth slowdowns might delay milestones, but algorithmic progress and AI labor could counteract supply constraints

## Synthesis
Becker's analysis of AI progress regularity engages with one of the more counterintuitive empirical findings in AI forecasting: that despite the jagged, uneven nature of AI capabilities across different tasks and domains, the rate of improvement tends to be surprisingly regular when measured on appropriate scales.

The implication of regular progress is that forecasting based on observed trajectories may be more reliable than forecasting based on intuition about which tasks are "near" or "far" from AI capability. Intuition systematically underestimates progress in some domains (reasoning tasks that appeared to require general intelligence) and overestimates progress in others (apparently simple tasks that turned out to require capabilities not captured in training). Regular straight lines on log-scale graphs, if the pattern holds, suggest that extrapolation from observed progress is a better forecasting tool than domain-by-domain assessment.

The RL-as-step-change skepticism is worth examining carefully. The narrative around 2024-2025 is that reinforcement learning techniques created a step-change in AI reasoning capabilities. Becker raises three alternative explanations: measurement tasks became increasingly correlated with training tasks (not a genuine capability improvement), scoring mechanisms in certain benchmarks were favorable (overfitting to specific evaluation formats), or the data is too sparse to distinguish step-change from noise. None of these alternatives are definitive, but they suggest caution about attributing capability jumps to specific techniques before ruling out measurement artifacts.

The pre-training vs. post-training distinction provides a useful framework for understanding where progress comes from. Pre-training improvements — larger models, better data, improved architectures — produce improvements across many domains simultaneously because they improve the model's general capabilities. Post-training improvements — RLHF, fine-tuning on specific tasks — produce targeted improvements in specific capabilities that companies prioritize. The straight-line pattern likely reflects pre-training progress; post-training effects may produce local deviations from the trend.

The compute slowdown consideration is important for medium-term forecasting. If semiconductor manufacturing constraints limit compute growth, the pre-training scaling that drives broad capability improvements slows. Becker notes that algorithmic improvements (achieving the same capability with less compute) and AI labor (using AI systems to accelerate research) could maintain progress trajectories even under compute constraints — suggesting the straight lines may prove more durable than hardware pessimism implies.
