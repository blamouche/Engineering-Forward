# Exclusive: Microsoft Moving All GitHub Copilot Subscribers To Token-Based Billing In June

**Source**: https://www.wheresyoured.at/exclusive-microsoft-moving-all-github-copilot-subscribers-to-token-based-billing-in-june
**Date**: April 22, 2026
**Author**: Ed Zitron
**Keywords**: GitHub Copilot, Microsoft, billing, tokens, AI economics, enterprise pricing

## Elevator pitch
Microsoft is reportedly replacing Copilot’s request-based quotas with pooled token credits, a sign that the economics of AI coding assistants are forcing pricing models closer to underlying compute costs.

## Takeaways
- Internal documents reportedly describe token-based billing for Copilot Business and Enterprise starting in June.
- Business plans would include pooled AI credits rather than simple request counts.
- The shift follows other Copilot plan changes such as tighter limits and model restrictions.
- The pricing move reflects pressure from rising model and inference costs.
- The broader implication is that coding assistants may become usage-metered infrastructure products rather than flat-rate SaaS tools.

## Synthesis
This report matters because pricing changes often reveal more about the economics of AI products than product launches do. According to the leaked documents, Microsoft is preparing to move GitHub Copilot Business and Enterprise customers from request-based quotas to token-based billing with pooled credits. That sounds like a billing tweak, but it points to a deeper issue: AI coding assistants are expensive to run, and flat subscription pricing may no longer map cleanly to actual usage.

Request-based systems were always an abstraction. They hid variation in token counts, context lengths, output sizes, and model choice behind simple per-plan allowances. But as organizations start using more powerful models, more agentic workflows, and longer-running coding tasks, that abstraction becomes harder to sustain. A single “request” can vary wildly in underlying cost. Token accounting aligns price more directly with compute consumption, which is better for the vendor’s margins but potentially worse for customer predictability.

The report also fits a broader pattern. Microsoft has already tightened some Copilot plans, suspended some sign-ups, and reduced access to more expensive models on lower tiers. A shift to token credits suggests the company is trying to keep Copilot attractive while reducing the open-ended liability created by heavy users. In enterprise settings, pooled credits also make budgeting feel more cloud-like: one shared spend bucket rather than hard user-by-user ceilings.

The strategic takeaway is that AI software may increasingly move away from the SaaS illusion of unlimited flat-rate access. As products become more agentic and more compute-intensive, vendors will keep looking for pricing models that track real resource consumption. Copilot may be an early example of that transition becoming visible to customers.
