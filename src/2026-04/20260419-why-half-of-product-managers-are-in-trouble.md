# Why Half of Product Managers Are in Trouble

**Source**: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/why-half-of-product-managers-are-in-trouble
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Nikhyl Singhal (Lenny's Newsletter interview)
**Keywords**: product management, AI, PM careers, job market, organizational change, AI-first teams

## Elevator pitch
Nikhyl Singhal argues that AI is about to split product management into winners and losers, rewarding PMs who reinvent themselves around AI-native work while exposing those who rely on legacy coordination-heavy roles.

## Takeaways
- The next two years may be the most chaotic period in product management history as AI changes how products are built and how teams are staffed.
- Many current PM roles are vulnerable because AI reduces demand for coordination, documentation, and middle-layer process work.
- The PMs who thrive will be those who actively experiment with AI and find personal “moments of joy” that pull them into new workflows.
- Resume prestige and logo collecting matter less in an AI-first market than adaptability, speed, and evidence of reinvention.
- Singhal predicts large companies may cut tens of thousands of people and rehire much smaller AI-native teams optimized around leverage.

## Synthesis
Nikhyl Singhal’s argument is intentionally provocative, but it tracks with a broader shift happening across knowledge work: AI does not simply speed up existing roles, it changes which parts of those roles remain valuable. Product management is especially exposed because a substantial share of PM work in many organizations consists of coordination, translation, roadmapping, stakeholder management, and document production. Those are precisely the areas where AI tools are getting surprisingly strong.

Singhal’s claim is not that product management disappears. It is that the median PM job changes fast enough that a large fraction of today’s practitioners may find themselves optimized for the wrong version of the role. The danger is highest for PMs who built their careers on being communication routers, process owners, and polished presenters, rather than on sharp judgment, customer insight, prioritization, and the ability to drive teams through ambiguity.

What replaces the old model is an AI-native PM. This person uses AI constantly, not as a novelty but as a core operating layer. They prototype with it, research with it, pressure-test strategy with it, and use it to compress execution cycles. More importantly, they learn to enjoy this. Singhal’s phrase about finding “moments of joy” with AI matters because reinvention is not purely analytical. People rarely sustain transformation through fear alone. They need some direct felt reward, some moment where the new mode of work clicks.

The emotional side of the transition also matters. Singhal describes “smiling exhaustion” across the PM community, which feels accurate. Many product people are publicly enthusiastic while privately overwhelmed. They know expectations are changing faster than their habits. That mismatch creates anxiety, defensiveness, and denial, all of which slow adaptation.

His point about brand-name resumes losing value is also important. In a world where roles are being redefined, past credentials become weaker signals. Employers care less that someone previously worked at Meta or Google if they cannot demonstrate AI-native leverage today. The new signal is practical proof: can you think clearly, move fast, use AI well, and deliver outcomes?

For engineering and product leaders, the implication is blunt. Teams should stop assuming their current PM structure will survive intact. They need fewer process-heavy roles and more product people who combine strategy, taste, systems thinking, and AI fluency. For PMs individually, the advice is even simpler: experiment aggressively, rebuild your operating model now, and do not wait for the org chart to tell you what changed.
