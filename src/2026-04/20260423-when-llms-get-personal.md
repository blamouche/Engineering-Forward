# When LLMs Get Personal

**Source**: https://joshbudman.substack.com/p/when-llms-get-personal
**Date**: April 22, 2026
**Author**: Josh Budman
**Keywords**: AI search, personalization, LLMs, retrieval, probabilistic systems

## Elevator pitch
Josh Budman argues that personalization changes LLM answers without making them arbitrary, because different users still see outputs shaped by the same retrieval bottlenecks, model priors, and semantic cores.

## Takeaways
- The article rejects both extremes: total answer uniformity and total answer fragmentation.
- Budman argues that LLM answers can vary while still sharing a stable semantic core.
- He focuses on materially distinct answers rather than token-level variation.
- Personalization is framed as changing the margins of an answer more often than its core substance.
- The piece suggests AI search optimization remains possible, but the object of optimization is recurring answer patterns, not one fixed SERP-like surface.

## Synthesis
Josh Budman’s essay is useful because it tries to bring precision to a conversation about AI search that is often dominated by vibes. The question is whether personalization makes LLM-generated answers too unstable to study or optimize. His answer is no. He argues that even when different users receive different outputs, those outputs are still constrained by the same model priors, retrieval neighborhoods, and context-construction bottlenecks. In other words, personalization changes answers, but it does not dissolve structure.

That distinction matters because many debates around AI visibility and optimization collapse too quickly into false extremes. One extreme says personalization means every user lives in a bespoke answer universe, so nothing stable remains. The other says personalization barely matters and answers are effectively the same for everyone. Budman’s middle position is that the interesting object is semantic overlap. Token sequences may vary endlessly, but the number of materially distinct answers is much smaller than the number of possible strings.

This is a good framing because it shifts the conversation from surface output to answer archetypes. If the core entities, conclusions, and practical guidance recur across many generated answers, then optimization does not disappear. It becomes a problem of influencing recurring semantic patterns rather than winning one rank position on a static page. That is a harder problem, but it is still a structured one.

The essay’s broader value is conceptual. As AI search grows, people need a better language for thinking about personalization than either classical SEO analogies or total rupture narratives. Budman’s argument suggests continuity and change at once: LLM-based answers are more fluid than search rankings, but they are not random. That is probably the right mental model for anyone trying to understand how brands, recommendations, and informational guidance show up in AI systems.
