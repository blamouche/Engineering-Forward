# Building a best-selling game with a tiny team – with Jonas Tyroller

**Source**: https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/thronefall
**Date**: April 8, 2026
**Author**: Gergely Orosz
**Keywords**: game development, indie games, prototyping, Unity, software engineering practices, AI tools

## Elevator pitch
Jonas Tyroller’s story is a good reminder that engineering process is context-dependent: for a two-person indie game, rapid prototyping, direct playtesting, and market-sensitive design can matter far more than textbook software practices.

## Takeaways
- A two-person indie game can succeed by optimizing for fast learning, not process completeness.
- Gameplay and commercial viability are tested through many tiny prototypes before polishing begins.
- Traditional practices like code review and unit tests may be optional in small, high-trust, one-shot projects.
- Pathfinding, balancing, and playtester feedback mattered more than architectural purity.
- AI tools help with scaffolding and translation, but the hard work remains design judgment and iteration.

## Synthesis
This interview is useful because it pushes back against the lazy belief that there is one universal set of engineering best practices. Jonas Tyroller and his collaborator built a hit game with a tiny team, minimal ceremony, no code reviews, and little emphasis on tests. In many software contexts that would sound irresponsible. In theirs, it was rational. They were shipping a self-contained product with two experienced builders, tight feedback loops, and extremely high sensitivity to whether the game was actually fun. That changes the optimal process dramatically.

The most important idea is the centrality of prototyping. Jonas describes building many small concepts in one or two days, then using those experiments to search for the overlap between what feels exciting to build and what might succeed in the market. That is closer to venture-style exploration than classic product roadmapping. Once they found the right concept, the real work became tuning experience: balancing difficulty, shaping progression, getting pathfinding to feel right, and listening to playtesters. Those are not problems you solve by adding more process. They are solved by repetition, intuition, and direct contact with player behavior.

For a broader engineering audience, the article is a good reminder to fit methodology to economics. Indie games are often one-shot bets with tiny teams and strong creative constraints. Overinvesting in process can be a tax that delays learning. The caveat, of course, is that what works for a two-person game would break down in a larger, longer-lived system. But that is exactly the point. Good engineering is not about importing rituals from prestigious environments. It is about choosing the smallest set of practices that maximizes learning, quality, and shipping speed for the system you are actually building.
