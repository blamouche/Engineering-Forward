# Why women aren't ‘missing’ the AI train

**Source**: https://sifted.eu/articles/women-missing-ai-train
**Date**: April 7, 2026
**Author**: Malin Frithiofsson
**Keywords**: AI adoption, gender equity, unpaid labor, parental leave, venture capital, startups

## Elevator pitch
Malin Frithiofsson argues that women are not mysteriously missing AI; unequal access to uninterrupted time, household support, and capital is shaping who can participate early.

## Takeaways
- The article rejects the idea that AI participation is just a matter of individual ambition and reframes it as a question of who actually gets uninterrupted time to learn and build.
- Frithiofsson argues that caregiving and household labor still fall disproportionately on women, which directly constrains experimentation, skill building, and startup creation.
- Her personal example flips the usual story: she is ahead in AI largely because her husband is absorbing the family logistics that often subsidize men’s careers.
- AI lowers some software costs, but the current tooling stack still advantages domains with abundant historical data and strong existing capital networks.
- The article ties women’s underrepresentation in AI to structural bottlenecks in parental leave, domestic labor, and venture funding rather than to lack of interest or talent.

## Synthesis
This essay is less a commentary on AI skills than a critique of the conditions under which people are able to acquire them. Frithiofsson pushes back against the familiar framing that women are somehow failing to “get on the AI train.” Her argument is that the metaphor itself is dishonest because it assumes everyone is waiting on the same platform with the same amount of free time, disposable energy, and room to experiment. In reality, the early AI advantage often goes to people who can protect long stretches of uninterrupted work.

The strongest part of the piece is its insistence that productivity is usually subsidized. Frithiofsson says she is ahead in AI not because of superior technical talent but because her husband handles much of the family logistics that would otherwise fragment her time. That inversion makes the hidden support structure visible. Many narratives about AI adoption celebrate speed, curiosity, and initiative while ignoring the domestic arrangements that make those traits actionable. Her point is that the system rewards the people whose time is defended by someone else.

She also argues that the current AI boom does not distribute opportunity evenly even when software itself becomes cheaper to build. Some categories benefit from rich datasets, well-understood workflows, and obvious funding paths. Other categories, such as women’s health, often require more bespoke data collection, more manual effort, and more capital before they can benefit from the same tooling. So the headline claim that AI lowers the barrier to entrepreneurship is only partly true. It lowers barriers most for founders already aligned with the data, tools, and capital structure that the ecosystem has accumulated.

The broader lesson is that discussions about who is participating in AI need to move upstream. If the goal is broader inclusion, the levers are not just training programs or more encouragement. They are parental leave, household labor distribution, and capital allocation. Frithiofsson’s argument is blunt but useful: before diagnosing who is behind, look at who has time, who has money, and who is carrying the invisible work that keeps everyone else moving.
