# OpenAI #16: A History and a Proposal

**Source**: https://thezvi.wordpress.com/2026/04/07/openai-16-a-history-and-a-proposal
**Date**: April 7, 2026
**Author**: Zvi Mowshowitz
**Keywords**: OpenAI, Sam Altman, governance, policy, Anthropic, TBPN, AI politics

## Elevator pitch
Zvi Mowshowitz surveys a week of OpenAI news by combining a critique of Sam Altman’s history and credibility, a rejection of OpenAI’s latest policy framing, and commentary on the company’s media and acquisition moves, arguing that governance and trust remain the core unresolved issue.

## Takeaways
- The post treats the New Yorker’s long profile of Sam Altman as mostly confirmation that long-running concerns about his trustworthiness and governance style remain valid.
- Zvi revisits episodes around the OpenAI board conflict, internal investigations, safety disputes, and relationships with Musk, Amodei, and Microsoft.
- He argues that OpenAI’s public policy positioning should be read skeptically because the company’s incentives and history do not support easy trust.
- The piece frames Anthropic’s Mythos cybersecurity coalition as the day’s most consequential actual news, even while the essay focuses on OpenAI.
- The overall throughline is that AI power concentration cannot be separated from organizational character and institutional accountability.

## Synthesis
This is classic Zvi: part roundup, part annotated history, part prosecutorial brief. The core move is to shift attention away from any single headline and toward the pattern underneath. Rather than debating whether one new article or memo changes the picture, he argues that the accumulation of episodes around Altman and OpenAI already establishes the key fact: governance quality and truthfulness are central variables, not side issues.

That framing matters because many AI discussions still compartmentalize capability, policy, safety, and leadership. Zvi refuses the separation. In his reading, the board coup, the missing investigations, the disputes with former safety leaders, and the company’s policy proposals are all manifestations of one structural problem: a powerful organization asking for trust without having earned it.

The essay is also notable for contrast. Zvi opens by saying the more important news of the day may actually be Anthropic’s attempt to coordinate a cybersecurity response around Mythos-discovered vulnerabilities. That juxtaposition implies a deeper divide in frontier AI narratives: one company trying to present a collective defense framing around dangerous capabilities, another still trapped in credibility and power struggles around its leadership.

Even if readers disagree with Zvi’s tone, the piece is useful as a map of the anti-OpenAI case among serious AI observers. It suggests that for frontier labs, governance reputation is becoming part of the product surface. As capabilities rise, the market and policymakers will increasingly evaluate not just what a model can do, but whether the institution releasing it deserves discretion in the first place.
