# Meet the startups using AI to discover new materials

**Source**: https://sifted.eu/articles/meet-the-startups-using-ai-to-discover-new-materials
**Date**: April 30, 2026
**Author**: Éanna Kelly
**Keywords**: AI, materials discovery, startups, deeptech, venture capital

## Elevator pitch
A cascade of funding is backing startups that use AI to accelerate the discovery of new materials with major energy and climate implications.

## Takeaways
- The piece frames AI-led materials discovery as a deeptech category attracting fresh investor attention.
- The startup thesis is that machine learning can compress the time and cost needed to identify promising compounds and materials.
- The target outcomes are highly practical, including better energy storage, carbon capture, and advanced industrial materials.
- The article positions Europe-based startups as part of a broader race to turn scientific discovery into commercial advantage.
- The available public excerpt points to funding momentum more than mature product outcomes, suggesting the market is still early.

## Synthesis
Sifted spotlights a small but increasingly well-funded group of startups applying AI to one of the hardest innovation problems in industry: discovering new materials faster than traditional lab-driven methods allow. The article’s framing is notable because it connects materials discovery not to abstract frontier research, but to concrete bottlenecks in energy, climate and manufacturing. Better batteries, scalable carbon capture and more capable industrial materials all depend on finding useful compounds faster, cheaper and with a higher hit rate.

The core claim is that AI can narrow the search space. Instead of relying only on long experimental cycles, startups use machine learning to predict which candidate materials deserve physical testing. That does not remove the need for lab validation, but it changes the economics of exploration. If models can reduce the number of failed experiments, they can meaningfully cut both capital intensity and time-to-discovery.

What stands out in the public excerpt is the investment angle. The story is less about one breakthrough material and more about the emergence of a funding wave around the category. That matters because materials discovery has often struggled with long timelines and difficult commercialization paths. Investor interest suggests a growing belief that AI can shift the risk-reward equation enough to justify building companies in the space.

At the same time, the article implies an early market. The promise is large, but the evidence highlighted publicly is still mostly directional: strong ambition, meaningful use cases and increasing capital. For operators, the practical takeaway is that AI’s industrial impact may come less from chat interfaces and more from domain-specific systems that improve discovery workflows in science-heavy sectors. If that thesis holds, materials discovery could become a good example of AI creating leverage far away from the typical software productivity story.
