# Productize Yourself

**Source**: https://linas.substack.com/p/productizeyourself
**Date**: April 4, 2026
**Author**: Linas Beliunas
**Keywords**: Naval Ravikant, leverage, one-person company, code, media, entrepreneurship, AI

## Elevator pitch
Revisiting Naval Ravikant through the lens of AI, Linas argues that the winning strategy is to pair specific knowledge with code and media leverage so one person can build assets that scale beyond their time.

## Takeaways
- Specific knowledge matters because generic skills become commoditized faster in an AI-rich economy.
- The most accessible forms of leverage for small teams are code and media because they replicate at near-zero marginal cost.
- AI compresses the gap between expertise and deployment, making solo and micro-team businesses more plausible.
- Authentic positioning comes from rare skill combinations, not from competing head-on in crowded categories.
- The distinction between creating assets and merely selling labor becomes even more important as AI increases individual output.

## Synthesis
This essay is essentially a Naval Ravikant riff updated for 2026, but it is sharper than most AI-era entrepreneurship commentary because it focuses on leverage rather than hype. Linas Beliunas argues that “productize yourself” is not motivational fluff. It is a structural strategy for a world where AI lowers the cost of building and distributing work. The key remains the combination Naval described years ago: specific knowledge plus scalable leverage. Without the first, you are replaceable. Without the second, you are still trading time for money.

What AI changes is the threshold at which that combination becomes actionable. More people can now turn expertise into software, content, workflows, and products without hiring full teams. That makes code and media—the two most permissionless forms of leverage—even more important. You can build once and distribute widely, which means the economic upside accrues to people who can identify a narrow, defensible edge and package it into something repeatable.

The essay is strongest when it treats authenticity as strategy rather than self-help. Competition is hardest when multiple people attack the same problem with the same skillset and the same narrative. It gets much easier when someone operates from an unusual combination of domain knowledge, communication ability, and technical execution. AI amplifies that advantage because it expands what one polymath can ship. A broad operator with taste, judgment, and a few strong intersections can now cover terrain that used to require a larger org chart.

The main caveat is that not all sectors become one-person-company territory. Regulation, hardware, biotech, and deep research still reward scale and specialization. But for a wide middle of software, education, services, and niche products, the direction feels right. The article captures a real economic shift: as AI reduces implementation friction, the scarce asset becomes distinctive insight packaged into systems that compound.
