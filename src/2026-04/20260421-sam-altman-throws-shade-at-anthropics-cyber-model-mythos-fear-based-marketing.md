# Sam Altman throws shade at Anthropic’s cyber model, Mythos: ‘fear-based marketing’

**Date**: April 21, 2026
**Author**: Unknown
**Keywords**: OpenAI, Anthropic, Mythos, cybersecurity, AI competition

## Elevator pitch
Sam Altman’s criticism of Anthropic’s Mythos rollout shows that frontier-model competition now includes narrative warfare over safety, access, and who gets to control powerful systems.

## Takeaways
- Altman accused Anthropic of using “fear-based marketing” around its Mythos cyber model.
- Anthropic has argued Mythos is too powerful for public release because of misuse risks.
- TechCrunch notes that fear and hype have long been part of AI industry messaging, including from OpenAI.
- The dispute reflects a deeper conflict about distribution, safety framing, and market positioning.
- Safety rhetoric is increasingly functioning as both governance language and competitive strategy.

## Synthesis
Sam Altman’s criticism of Anthropic’s Mythos launch is more than a bit of inter-company sparring. It highlights how safety discourse around frontier AI has become entangled with product positioning, access control, and market narrative. According to TechCrunch, Altman argued that Anthropic was using “fear-based marketing” by portraying Mythos as so dangerous that public release would be irresponsible.

Anthropic’s framing of Mythos has been that the model is unusually capable in cybersecurity contexts and therefore should be released only to a limited enterprise audience. That kind of argument can be read in two ways at once. On one level, it is a genuine risk-management position: some model capabilities may create asymmetric misuse opportunities. On another level, it is an excellent way to make a product sound uniquely powerful and premium while restricting supply.

Altman’s critique goes directly after that second interpretation. His “bomb” and “bomb shelter” analogy suggests that selective access and fear messaging can serve as a way to keep advanced AI concentrated in the hands of a small group. In that reading, safety rhetoric is not just about preventing misuse. It is also a mechanism for market control and elite gating.

But TechCrunch also points out the obvious irony. The AI industry as a whole, OpenAI included, has often benefited from dramatic rhetoric about transformative or dangerous capabilities. Doom-laced narratives can attract attention, justify urgency, and elevate the perceived importance of the companies telling the story. That makes Altman’s critique fair in one sense and opportunistic in another.

What is really happening here is a fight over legitimacy. Companies want to be seen as both responsible and ambitious, both open and prudent, both accessible and exceptionally powerful. Those goals often conflict. When a competitor picks one balance point, rivals can attack it from the opposite angle: too reckless, too closed, too hyped, too timid.

For observers, the key lesson is not to take either company’s framing at face value. Safety claims may be sincere, but they also shape distribution strategy, pricing power, and brand identity. Similarly, calls for openness may reflect principle, but they can also be a way to undermine a competitor’s scarcity premium.

As frontier AI gets more commercially important, expect more of this. The competitive game is no longer just about benchmark wins. It is also about who gets to define what counts as responsible access, what counts as dangerous capability, and who deserves to sit behind the gate.
