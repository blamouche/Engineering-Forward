# The Shelf Life of Intelligence

**Source**: https://jigarkdoshi.bearblog.dev/the-shelf-life-of-intelligence
**Date**: April 11, 2026
**Author**: Jigar Doshi
**Keywords**: frontier models, AI economics, discovery, labs, drug discovery, proprietary data

## Elevator pitch
Jigar Doshi argues that frontier intelligence is becoming a rapidly decaying commodity, so the durable profits in AI will come from using the best models to generate proprietary discoveries and exclusive real-world data rather than from selling raw model access.

## Takeaways
- The essay argues that each frontier model generation becomes more expensive to build and serve while staying state-of-the-art for less time.
- It claims the agent layer inherits the same pressure, making many fast-growing products more like customer-acquisition channels than durable businesses.
- The proposed durable value pools are proprietary discoveries and proprietary instrumentation that generates unique real-world data.
- Science, materials, biotech, and other knowledge-production domains are presented as especially attractive because outputs can appreciate after creation.
- The piece reframes the strategic gap between labs and everyone else as an access gap to compounding knowledge production, not just model quality.

## Synthesis
Jigar Doshi’s essay is built around a strong metaphor: frontier-model intelligence is grape juice, while durable discoveries are wine. The point is that model capability decays quickly. What looks extraordinary today is soon matched, distilled, or undercut. If that is true, then selling access to raw intelligence—tokens, inference, or even agent labor—becomes a structurally weak business unless you can continuously outrun commoditization.

From there, the essay argues that the real strategic move for frontier labs is to climb the value curve. Instead of monetizing the model directly, they should use the best internal systems to produce things that last: drug candidates, materials insights, chip designs, or other discoveries with long-lived economic value. In that framing, the model is no longer the product. It is the engine behind a proprietary research pipeline.

The second frontier Doshi identifies is instrumentation. If a lab or partner owns the wet lab, reactor, trial network, marketplace, or other system that generates new proprietary data, then it can create compounding informational advantages that rivals cannot easily copy. That is a sharper moat than API access because the underlying data is born inside the workflow rather than scraped from a shared internet commons.

Whether or not every number in the piece holds up, the strategic thesis is compelling. Frontier AI may be structurally pushing the strongest players away from pure model vending and toward vertically integrated knowledge-production businesses. If so, the biggest asymmetry between labs and everyone else will not be who has a slightly smarter chatbot. It will be who can translate fleeting intelligence into durable assets.
