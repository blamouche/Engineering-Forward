# State of the Product Job Market in Early 2026

**Source**: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/state-of-the-product-job-market-in-ee9
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Lenny Rachitsky
**Keywords**: job market, product management, hiring, engineering jobs, AI roles, remote work, Bay Area

## Elevator pitch
Despite layoffs and AI anxiety, Lenny’s survey of TrueUp data shows tech hiring in early 2026 is stronger than headlines suggest, with PM openings at multi-year highs and AI roles exploding, even as remote work continues to decline.

## Takeaways
- Global PM openings at tech companies are at their highest level in more than three years, up sharply from the 2023 low.
- Engineering hiring also remains strong, with more than 67,000 open engineering roles globally in the dataset.
- AI-focused roles are growing especially fast, suggesting AI is creating new hiring demand even while it disrupts existing workflows.
- Design hiring has plateaued, remote opportunities continue to shrink, and the Bay Area is becoming more central again.
- Rising recruiter hiring is a useful leading indicator that companies expect demand for talent to remain elevated.

## Synthesis
This job-market snapshot is a useful corrective to the dominant narrative that tech hiring is simply collapsing under the weight of layoffs and AI automation. The picture Lenny presents is more uneven and, in some respects, more optimistic. Hiring remains difficult for many individuals, but aggregate demand, especially in product, engineering, and AI-related functions, is meaningfully stronger than the mood suggests.

The headline result is that product management openings have climbed back to levels not seen since 2022. That matters because PM hiring is often one of the cleaner signals of company confidence. Firms do not expand product orgs aggressively unless they believe there is roadmap surface area worth staffing and executing against. The rebound suggests that, despite organizational turbulence, companies still believe product investment matters.

Engineering demand is even more striking. Tens of thousands of open roles remain across tech companies in the dataset. That does not tell us whether AI would have produced even more openings in a counterfactual world, but it does show that AI has not yet collapsed engineering demand in any simple way. If anything, it may be raising expectations, broadening project scope, and increasing the appetite for engineers who can work effectively with AI systems.

The strongest growth appears in AI roles themselves. That is unsurprising, but still important. AI is not just a labor-saving tool, it is also a major new domain of product development, infrastructure investment, and organizational experimentation. Every company that decides AI is strategic creates second-order demand in research, product, design, engineering, safety, data, and GTM roles.

The report is also valuable because it preserves nuance. Remote work is still declining. The Bay Area’s importance is reasserting itself. Design is not keeping pace with the same momentum. And a healthier market at the aggregate level does not automatically translate into an easy search for candidates. Frictions remain high.

The recruiter signal is particularly useful. Companies do not rebuild recruiting teams unless they expect hiring demand to persist. Recruiter openings recovering toward earlier highs suggests firms are planning for sustained hiring rather than one-off bursts.

For people navigating the market, the implication is mixed but encouraging. The market is not uniformly friendly, but it is more alive than doom-laden discourse implies. For leaders, the data suggests this is a moment to hire selectively into high-leverage roles, especially those that combine domain expertise with AI fluency. The next wave of hiring is likely to reward adaptability more than pedigree.
