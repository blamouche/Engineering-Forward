# The AI Labs Have A $7 Doritos Problem

**Source**: https://vinvashishta.substack.com/p/the-ai-labs-have-a-7-doritos-problem
**Date**: April 13, 2026
**Author**: Vin Vashishta
**Keywords**: AI economics, pricing, ROI, OpenAI, Anthropic

## Elevator pitch
Vin Vashishta argues frontier AI labs are trying to price AI like a necessity before most customers experience it that way, creating a dangerous mismatch between infrastructure costs, weak ROI, and buyers’ willingness to pay.

## Takeaways
- Vin Vashishta argues frontier AI labs are trying to price AI like a necessity before most customers experience it that way, creating a dangerous mismatch between infrastructure costs, weak ROI, and buyers’ willingness to pay.
- Using PepsiCo’s $7 Doritos problem as an analogy, Vin Vashishta argues that most AI products are still discretionary purchases dressed up as essential infrastructure. The point is not that AI lacks value, but that many labs are pricing and packaging it as though customers cannot easily cut it—while both consumers and enterprises increasingly show that they can.
- The article’s practical lens is unit economics. Labs are carrying enormous inference and compute costs, yet many users remain low-conviction free or lightly paying customers. In that environment, “shrinkflation” shows up as tighter caps, weaker free tiers, or subscriptions that nominally stay the same while delivering less real utility.
- The sharpest takeaway is that model capability alone is not the product. Buyers pay for recurring usefulness, measurable outcomes, and operational fit. If labs cannot convert those into clear necessity-level value, the economics of ever-larger models become much harder to justify, especially against cheaper, narrower, or more workflow-native alternatives.

## Synthesis

Using PepsiCo’s $7 Doritos problem as an analogy, Vin Vashishta argues that most AI products are still discretionary purchases dressed up as essential infrastructure. The point is not that AI lacks value, but that many labs are pricing and packaging it as though customers cannot easily cut it—while both consumers and enterprises increasingly show that they can.

The article’s practical lens is unit economics. Labs are carrying enormous inference and compute costs, yet many users remain low-conviction free or lightly paying customers. In that environment, “shrinkflation” shows up as tighter caps, weaker free tiers, or subscriptions that nominally stay the same while delivering less real utility.

The sharpest takeaway is that model capability alone is not the product. Buyers pay for recurring usefulness, measurable outcomes, and operational fit. If labs cannot convert those into clear necessity-level value, the economics of ever-larger models become much harder to justify, especially against cheaper, narrower, or more workflow-native alternatives.
