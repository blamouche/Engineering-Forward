# Not all AI agents are created equal

**Source**: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/not-all-ai-agents-are-created-equal
**Date**: April 13, 2026
**Author**: Hamza Farooq, Jaya Rajwani
**Keywords**: AI agents, product strategy, workflow automation, multi-agent systems, ROI, agent architecture

## Elevator pitch
This guide argues that “AI agent” is too broad to prioritize effectively, and proposes a three-bucket framework—deterministic workflows, reasoning agents, and multi-agent systems—to match ambition, tooling, and ROI with the actual problem.

## Takeaways
- The piece argues that many teams fail at agent prioritization because they compare fundamentally different architectures as if they had the same cost, risk, and operating model.
- Deterministic automation workflows should often come first because they ship faster, cost less, and capture a large share of practical agent opportunities.
- Reasoning agents and multi-agent systems make sense only when the task genuinely requires dynamic tool choice, ambiguity handling, or coordination across roles.

## Synthesis
Lenny’s newsletter is most useful as a product triage lens, not as another generic “agents are the future” essay. Its core claim is that companies keep prioritizing agent ideas badly because they collapse very different systems into one label. A workflow that classifies inbound requests and routes them through fixed steps is not the same thing as an agent that plans dynamically across tools, and neither is the same thing as a multi-agent network. Once those categories are separated, roadmap choices get less mystical and much more operational.

The practical value is in how strongly it biases teams toward starting with deterministic automation. That advice cuts against the current market instinct to jump straight to high-autonomy systems, but it reflects where the fastest ROI often lives: repetitive processes with known steps, clear failure modes, and measurable outcomes. In that sense, the article is really about resisting overengineering. If a problem can be expressed as software plus a few LLM-powered interpretation steps, wrapping it in an elaborate reasoning stack just adds cost and fragility.

The broader strategic point is that architecture choice is product strategy. The category you pick determines staffing, infra, safety requirements, evaluation, and time-to-value. Teams that get that distinction early can build a credible sequence of wins instead of funding a grab bag of “agent” experiments that have nothing in common except the label.
