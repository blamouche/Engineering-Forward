# Good Taste the Only Real Moat Left

**Source**: https://rajnandan.com/posts/taste-in-the-age-of-ai-and-llms
**Date**: April 3, 2026
**Author**: Rajnandan
**Keywords**: AI, LLMs, taste, product judgment, design, writing, builders

## Elevator pitch
Rajnandan argues that AI makes competent output abundant, so advantage shifts toward taste and judgment, but warns that humans should use taste to actively shape work under real constraints rather than become passive curators of machine-generated drafts.

## Takeaways
- LLMs flood the market with plausible 7/10 work, which makes the middle of the quality distribution crowded and generic.
- Taste matters less as aesthetic preference than as the ability to notice what is off, reject it, and explain precisely why.
- The new bottleneck is judgment: knowing which drafts are context-blind, dishonest, or merely polished averages.
- AI can help train taste by generating many variants and forcing sharper critique, especially through rejection-focused exercises.
- Taste alone is not enough; authorship still requires ownership of stakes, direction, and constraints that models cannot truly carry.

## Synthesis
This essay captures an important second-order effect of generative AI: the problem is no longer getting from zero to a decent artifact. The problem is deciding what deserves to survive once “decent” becomes cheap. Rajnandan’s strongest point is that taste is only useful when it becomes diagnostic rather than mystical. Saying that something feels off is weak. Saying exactly how a draft hides a trade-off, sounds interchangeable, or ignores a user constraint is where value starts.

That matters because LLMs optimize for plausibility. Their default failure mode is not nonsense but generic competence. As a result, teams can ship polished mediocrity faster than ever: clean copy, modern design, persuasive decks, and little real specificity. In that environment, judgment becomes a filtering and shaping mechanism, not an optional finishing touch.

The essay is also right to reject the comforting idea that humans can retreat into the role of selector. If AI generates and humans merely rank outputs, the human contribution gets narrowed to post-processing. Rajnandan’s corrective is that serious work still comes from authorship under consequence: regulatory risk, support burden, brand damage, strategic trade-offs, and lived knowledge of users. Those are not just more inputs for prompts; they are the substance of building.

For product and engineering teams, the practical lesson is that AI should speed exploration while humans own the constraints that make work matter. The moat is not “having taste” as a vibe. It is using judgment to push drafts closer to reality, context, and responsibility than the statistical average would ever go on its own.
