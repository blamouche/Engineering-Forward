# Lovable CEO apologises after security scare: ‘I take accountability’
**Source**: https://sifted.eu/articles/lovable-ceo-anton-osika-security-apology
**Date**: April 23, 2026
**Author**: Unknown
**Keywords**: Lovable, security, data exposure, privacy, vibe coding

## Elevator pitch
Lovable’s CEO says the startup mishandled a security scare, turning a debate over privacy defaults and disclosure into a broader trust test for AI app builders.

## Takeaways
- Lovable says the issue was fixed, but the incident exposed a gap between product defaults and what users believed was private.
- The company learned about the problem through social media because its vulnerability disclosure process had broken down.
- The episode shows how AI builders must treat chat histories and prompt data as sensitive product infrastructure, not incidental logs.
- Security response now depends as much on communication speed and clarity as on the underlying technical fix.
- For fast-growing AI startups, privacy UX and disclosure processes are becoming core parts of category trust.

## Synthesis
Lovable’s latest security episode is less about a narrowly defined breach than about the fragile trust model behind AI-native product development. CEO Anton Osika apologised after users raised concerns that chat histories and personal information were visible in ways they did not expect. The company says the issue has now been fixed and stressed that it was not a classic data breach. But from a user’s perspective, the distinction matters less than the underlying experience of discovering that supposedly private work may have been more exposed than assumed.

That is what makes the story important beyond Lovable itself. Vibe-coding products do not just store generic account information. They often hold prompts, product ideas, source code, client context and personal data pasted into conversations. Once a platform becomes a working environment, ambiguous visibility rules become a product risk and a security risk at the same time. A confusing privacy model can undermine confidence almost as fast as a malicious intrusion.

Osika’s admission that Lovable first learned of the issue through social media is also telling. It suggests the problem was not limited to one implementation detail. The company’s vulnerability disclosure and escalation process had broken down enough that an external complaint went public before the internal response kicked in. For startups operating at speed, this is a warning that security posture includes the reporting pipeline, not just the codebase. If outside researchers or users cannot reliably reach the team, the public internet becomes the incident queue.

The timing raises the stakes further. Lovable is trying to define a category while facing growing competition and scrutiny. In that environment, users evaluate not only features and velocity but also whether the company behaves like a credible steward of sensitive data. An apology helps, but the longer-term test is whether the company changes defaults, clarifies what is public, and improves how it communicates incidents before they become viral.

The broader lesson for AI app builders is straightforward. If your product captures user conversations and generated assets, privacy expectations are part of the interface. Teams cannot afford to leave those expectations implicit. They need conservative defaults, clear labels, durable disclosure channels and a response process that works before a screenshot hits social media. Lovable’s incident shows that in AI products, trust breaks first at the UX layer and only later becomes a security story.
