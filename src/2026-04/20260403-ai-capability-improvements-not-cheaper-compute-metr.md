# AI's Capability Improvements Haven't Come from It Getting Less Affordable
**Source**: https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/E6ELHguZFNF3Czp55/ai-s-capability-improvements-haven-t-come-from-it-getting
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Anders Cairns Woodruff
**Keywords**: AI capabilities, compute costs, METR, frontier time horizons, algorithmic progress, scaling, timelines

## Elevator pitch
METR research challenges the assumption that AI capability improvements are driven by reduced compute costs, finding that frontier time horizons double every few months primarily through algorithmic advances rather than economic accessibility gains.

## Takeaways
- METR data shows frontier time horizons are doubling every few months
- Capability improvements appear driven primarily by algorithmic advances and architectural innovations, not reduced compute costs
- The distinction matters for forecasting when AI will reach various capability thresholds
- Empirical grounding for AI timeline discussions that separates capability from economic drivers
- Understanding the source of improvements is essential for predicting future capability trajectories

## Synthesis
This LessWrong analysis engages with a common misconception in AI capability forecasting: the conflation of capability improvements with cost reductions. While inference costs have declined substantially — and this democratization matters for deployment breadth — Woodruff's reading of METR research argues that the capability gains themselves are primarily algorithmic, not economic.

The distinction has practical implications for forecasting. If capability improvements were primarily cost-driven, the trajectory of improvement would be bounded by how much compute can be purchased per dollar, and hardware cost curves would be the primary predictor. If improvements are primarily algorithmic, the trajectory is driven by research progress — a less predictable but potentially faster-moving variable, since algorithmic improvements can deliver step-changes that hardware cost reductions cannot.

METR's frontier time horizon metric — measuring the complexity of software engineering tasks models can complete, expressed in equivalent human working time — provides a concrete capability measure that is less gameable than traditional benchmarks. The doubling cadence METR observes suggests AI systems are gaining the ability to handle meaningfully more complex autonomous tasks on a regular schedule.

The research supports a nuanced view: compute cost reductions matter enormously for accessibility and deployment, enabling more organizations to use capable AI systems, but they are not the primary driver of capability at the frontier. Organizations waiting for AI to become "affordable enough" to matter for their use cases may be waiting for the wrong variable. The relevant question is whether capability has crossed the threshold for their specific tasks, which is a function of algorithmic progress rather than cost.

For practitioners reasoning about AI timelines, this framing suggests tracking algorithmic milestones (new training techniques, architectural improvements, post-training advances) rather than compute cost curves as the leading indicator of when AI will be capable enough for specific high-value applications.
