# The Economics of Software Teams: Why Most Engineering Organizations Are Flying Blind

**Source**: https://www.viktorcessan.com/the-economics-of-software-teams
**Date**: April 13, 2026
**Author**: Viktor Cessan
**Keywords**: engineering management, software economics, ROI, platform teams, product strategy, LLMs

## Elevator pitch
Viktor Cessan argues that most software organizations manage engineering as a major capital expense without exposing teams to the financial logic of their work, leaving prioritization detached from the actual return required to justify headcount.

## Takeaways
- The post translates team size into concrete monthly and daily costs, then asks what time savings or revenue impact would be required for a team to break even—or to earn a healthy return.
- It argues that common delivery metrics like velocity or shipped features are poor substitutes for economic measurement because they can improve while business value worsens.
- The arrival of LLMs raises the stakes by making large engineering organizations harder to justify if they cannot explain which work truly creates leverage.

## Synthesis
This essay is strong because it forces engineering management back onto first-principles economics. A software team is not just a collection of talented builders; it is a recurring capital allocation decision. By pricing an eight-engineer team in monthly and daily terms, Viktor Cessan makes the hidden question unavoidable: what measurable value is this team generating relative to what it costs? That reframes prioritization from roadmap theater into investment logic.

The most useful part is the distinction between break-even and genuinely healthy returns. Many internal platform or product teams implicitly justify themselves with vague claims of efficiency or customer happiness. But if initiatives fail often, systems accumulate maintenance debt, and capital has alternative uses, then matching cost is not enough. Teams need to produce multiples of their expense to be clearly worthwhile. That is uncomfortable, but it is a much more honest standard for deciding which work matters.

The LLM angle makes the argument timely. If AI lowers the cost of some engineering labor or increases the output of smaller teams, organizations will face sharper scrutiny around headcount, architecture rewrites, and platform investments that are hard to tie to economic outcomes. Teams that understand their leverage will look disciplined and strategic. Teams that only report activity metrics may increasingly look like expensive black boxes.
