# What Is Taste, Really?
**Source**: https://every.to/p/what-is-taste-really
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Jack Cheng
**Keywords**: taste, creativity, AI, judgment, culture

## Elevator pitch
Jack Cheng argues that “taste” in the AI era is not one thing but a blend of personal preference, cultural legitimacy, and practiced discernment built by making things and explaining why they feel right.

## Takeaways
- The article distinguishes between personal taste and socially recognized “good taste,” arguing that AI discussions often blur the two.
- Personal taste acts as a filter that helps people navigate abundance and define what feels like “them.”
- Good taste is culturally constructed and often tied to status, influence, and tastemaking power.
- Discernment improves when people repeatedly make things and articulate why they prefer one choice over another.
- AI tools can strengthen taste by forcing users to specify what they want and by moving them from passive consumption toward active creation.

## Synthesis
Jack Cheng’s essay is valuable because it slows down a word that gets thrown around lazily in AI discourse. “Taste” is often treated as the human remainder once machines can generate text, code, images, and strategy. But Cheng argues that this framing hides an important ambiguity. When people say taste matters, they may mean the set of personal preferences that helps someone recognize what feels right to them. Or they may mean socially validated taste, the version that a culture or status group recognizes as refined, serious, or worth copying. Those are related, but they are not the same thing.

The personal version of taste is the most immediately practical one. It comes from accumulated exposure and repeated choices. Over time, people build a sense of what resonates with them and what does not. That becomes a filter for navigating abundance. In an AI environment, where almost any variation can be generated cheaply, that filtering function becomes more important. If models can produce endless options, then the person with a strong point of view becomes the one who can eliminate ninety percent of them quickly and steer toward the few that feel coherent.

The essay is equally strong on the cultural side of taste. What counts as “good taste” depends on the norms of a community, whether in design, literature, engineering, or fashion. Good taste is partly aesthetic judgment and partly social recognition. Tastemakers help define what a group notices and values, and other people orient themselves relative to that standard. This matters for AI because many professional judgments are not purely objective. People often want outputs that feel current, legible, and culturally aware, not just technically correct.

Cheng’s deepest point is that taste is sharpened through practice. It is not just a matter of liking certain things. It grows when people create, compare, and explain. Being able to say why one choice works and another does not turns preference into a usable tool. That maps well onto AI workflows. Prompting, revising, and steering model outputs all require people to become more explicit about what they want. In that sense, AI can actually train taste, because it rewards specificity and exposes vague judgment quickly.

The essay also carries an optimistic view of creation. By lowering the cost of making things, AI can move more people out of passive consumption and into active experimentation. Once someone is building instead of only browsing, they become more sensitive to craft choices and more capable of developing discernment. The larger implication is that taste is not a magical innate gift that survives automation. It is a capability that becomes more visible, more necessary, and potentially more teachable as AI makes execution abundant.
