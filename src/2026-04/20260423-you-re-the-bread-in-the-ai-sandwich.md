# You’re the Bread in the AI Sandwich
**Source**: https://every.to/context-window/you-re-the-bread-in-the-ai-sandwich
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Unknown
**Keywords**: AI workflows, compound engineering, human judgment, agent management, Every

## Elevator pitch
This piece argues that in AI-heavy workflows, humans add the framing, diagnosis, and taste around execution, while models increasingly handle the labor-intensive middle.

## Takeaways
- The article frames AI work as a sandwich where models are the filling and humans are the bread that set direction and evaluate outcomes.
- Kieran Klaassen’s compound engineering loop splits work into plan, work, review, and compound, with AI strongest in the execution phase.
- Humans still outperform agents at diagnosing problems from multiple angles and choosing among competing solution paths.
- Taste, in this framing, is the ability to judge whether AI output matches an intended vision and to keep iterating until it does.
- Every’s consulting team is also testing whether one general-purpose agent with shared plugins can outperform a collection of narrower specialist agents.

## Synthesis
This article is useful because it avoids both extremes that dominate AI work discussions. It does not claim humans are being removed from the loop, and it does not pretend AI is just a minor assistant. Instead, it proposes a cleaner division of labor. AI systems are increasingly good at sustained execution: following steps, producing drafts, handling repetitive work, and pushing through long tasks without fatigue. Humans, meanwhile, remain most valuable at the edges of that execution cycle, where ambiguity is highest and judgment matters most.

That is the logic behind the “AI sandwich” metaphor. The filling, the large volume of productive work, is increasingly machine-generated. But the bread still determines the shape of the meal. On the front end, that means framing the problem, choosing the objective, and deciding what kind of answer or artifact is needed. On the back end, it means reviewing what came back, deciding whether it is right, and refining the prompt or plan until the result feels aligned with the original intent. In other words, AI handles more of the doing, while humans stay responsible for direction and evaluation.

The piece ties this framing to Kieran Klaassen’s compound engineering model, which breaks work into planning, execution, review, and compounding of lessons. That framework matters because it treats execution as only one part of engineering, not the whole thing. If AI becomes very good at implementation, then the scarce human skill shifts toward defining the work properly and preserving the lessons learned so future work gets easier. This is a management and systems-design view of AI, not a pure automation view.

The article also makes an important point about diagnosis. Humans are still better at reasoning sideways, not just forward. When a problem appears, people can entertain multiple causal stories, combine context from different domains, and generate alternative interventions. Agents are improving, but they still tend to follow the path implied by the prompt rather than challenging the framing itself. That means human value increasingly sits in asking the better initial question and spotting when the system is solving the wrong problem.

Finally, the emphasis on taste is a reminder that output quality is not only about correctness. In many creative, strategic, and product contexts, what matters is whether the result fits a specific vision. That requires judgment that is contextual, aesthetic, and comparative. The article’s deeper message is that AI raises the value of this judgment layer. As execution gets cheaper, the people who can frame, diagnose, and assess well become more important, not less.
