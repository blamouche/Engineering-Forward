# Europe lost the B2C tech race. Can it win in B2B?

**Source**: https://sifted.eu/articles/europe-lost-b2c-can-it-win-b2b
**Date**: April 6, 2026
**Author**: John Thornhill
**Keywords**: Europe, startups, B2B software, AI, go-to-market, global expansion, venture capital

## Elevator pitch
John Thornhill argues that Europe’s best shot in the AI era is not to recreate American consumer-tech winners but to turn AI-enabled B2B startups into globally scaled businesses.

## Takeaways
- The article contrasts Europe’s weak historical position in large-scale consumer internet businesses with its stronger base in enterprise and industrial technology.
- AI is pushing many new European founders toward B2B opportunities where domain expertise, operational depth, and industry relationships matter more than pure consumer scale.
- The next challenge is not starting these companies but helping them expand internationally before they stall as regional winners.
- The piece implies that Europe must solve for growth, ambition, and cross-border execution if it wants AI-enabled B2B companies to become global category leaders.
- Rather than copying the old B2C playbook, Europe may benefit more from building around sectors where it already has enterprise strengths and differentiated data.

## Synthesis
Thornhill’s argument is essentially that Europe should stop measuring itself against the consumer internet era it failed to dominate and focus instead on the type of companies it is better positioned to build. The continent did not produce the defining B2C giants of the mobile and social era at anything like US scale. But AI may reset the opportunity in ways that favor enterprise workflows, operational expertise, and vertical domain knowledge, all areas where European founders often have a more credible starting point.

That does not make the opportunity automatic. Thornhill’s framing suggests that Europe already has plenty of entrepreneurial activity around AI-enabled B2B startups, but the harder question is whether those companies can expand beyond fragmented home markets and become international leaders. Historically, Europe has often produced technically strong companies that remain smaller than their American counterparts because they globalize later, raise less aggressively, or treat cross-border growth as a second phase instead of a design constraint from day one.

What makes the B2B angle plausible is that AI can amplify the value of specialized knowledge and existing industrial relationships. In enterprise markets, founders can win with sharper understanding of regulated sectors, operational pain points, and workflow complexity rather than relying on pure network effects. That potentially gives Europe a more natural lane than consumer categories where giant domestic markets and distribution advantages mattered enormously. The article therefore reads as a strategic argument for building where local strengths actually compound.

The deeper takeaway is that Europe’s AI opportunity depends less on producing a new generation of copycat consumer apps and more on turning serious enterprise startups into global businesses. That requires ambition in distribution, capital, and market entry, not just product quality. If Europe wants AI to reshape its startup position, it needs founders and investors to treat scale as part of the original thesis rather than an optional upgrade once local traction is proven.
