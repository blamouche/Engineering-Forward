# Random thoughts while gazing at the misty AI Frontier

**Source**: https://blog.eladgil.com/p/random-thoughts-while-gazing-at-the
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Elad Gil
**Keywords**: AI economy, compute constraints, labor markets, coding agents, AI strategy

## Elevator pitch
Elad Gil sketches a wide-angle view of the AI economy, arguing that explosive revenue growth, compute scarcity, closed-loop automation, and labor-market shifts are converging into a new industrial phase with coding at the center.

## Takeaways
- Gil argues that OpenAI and Anthropic are already individually approaching 0.1% of US GDP in revenue run-rate terms, underscoring how fast AI has scaled economically.
- He sees compute supply, especially memory and hardware bottlenecks, as a near-term ceiling that may preserve an oligopolistic market structure through at least 2028.
- The essay frames tokens and compute budgets as a new operating currency for both product design and company economics.
- Gil expects AI to reshape labor unevenly, first hitting outsourced and closed-loop work such as coding, support, and research workflows.
- Across the notes, he returns to the idea that this may be a temporary “golden era” where humans still direct AI systems while extracting large productivity gains from machine-generated slop.

## Synthesis
Elad Gil’s post is deliberately informal, but its value lies in how it stitches together many of the macro questions hanging over the AI market. Rather than making a single linear argument, Gil presents a set of connected observations about revenue scale, talent, compute, labor, and automation. The underlying message is that AI is moving from a software story into an economic and industrial one, with consequences that extend far beyond model benchmarks.

One of the most striking claims is that OpenAI and Anthropic may each already be operating at around 0.1% of US GDP in revenue run-rate terms. Whether those exact numbers hold or not, Gil uses them to emphasize the speed of adoption. In only a few years, AI has gone from near-zero direct economic footprint to something that could plausibly account for a meaningful share of national output. That invites questions not only about future market size, but also about measurement. Productivity gains may be real long before they are cleanly captured in GDP statistics, which could distort policy responses and public narratives.

Compute is the other major thread. Gil suggests that chip and memory constraints may impose an artificial asymptote on near-term model progress. If all major labs are supply-constrained, the frontier could remain oligopolistic because no one can pull dramatically ahead through brute-force scaling alone. At the same time, this makes compute allocation a strategic choice. Labs may oscillate between spending scarce capacity on better models and using it to build profitable applications. In that framework, tokens stop being a technical metric and become a business currency that shapes pricing, product scope, and company structure.

The labor discussion is equally important. Gil argues that visible layoffs blamed on AI often mask earlier overhiring, but he still expects real displacement to come first in closed-loop and outsourced work. Coding stands out because it offers tight feedback loops for model learning and rapid iteration. That is why coding agents matter beyond developer tooling. They may be an early template for how AI automates economically valuable work that can be measured and improved continuously.

Overall, the essay works as a map of the frontier rather than a polished thesis. Gil’s recurring point is that AI’s next phase will be shaped by infrastructure scarcity, market concentration, and changing labor economics as much as by raw capability gains. The result is a useful snapshot of an industry starting to behave like a new industrial base, not just a new software category.
