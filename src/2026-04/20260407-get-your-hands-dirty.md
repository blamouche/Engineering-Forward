# Get Your Hands Dirty

**Source**: https://every.to/context-window/get-your-hands-dirty
**Date**: April 7, 2026
**Author**: Every Staff
**Keywords**: AI adoption, executive strategy, Anthropic, OpenAI, workflows, AI organizations

## Elevator pitch
Every launches a compact AI-newsletter format that mixes reporting, commentary, and workflows, arguing that companies need hands-on executive engagement with AI while highlighting how compute constraints are starting to shape product strategy and competitive advantage.

## Takeaways
- Every is experimenting with a shorter briefing format that bundles a featured essay, market signals, hiring clues, internal observations, and practical workflows.
- The lead story argues executives should use AI tools directly instead of evaluating them only as software purchases.
- The newsletter frames Anthropic’s restrictions on third-party agent harnesses as a competitive opening for OpenAI.
- Several sections suggest AI adoption is less about raw technical skill than about reaching a concrete “aha moment” through useful daily workflows.
- The publication continues to treat AI-native organizations as combinations of media, software, consulting, and workflow design rather than just tool usage.

## Synthesis
This newsletter is less a single argument than a snapshot of how one AI-native media company thinks about the current moment. The central theme is that the next phase of adoption will be driven by people getting operationally involved with tools, not by passively watching the market from a distance. In that sense, the title is literal: “get your hands dirty” is presented as both a management principle and a competitive necessity.

The strongest editorial signal in the issue is the contrast between Anthropic and OpenAI. Anthropic’s decision to limit subscriber use through third-party agent harnesses is interpreted as a compute-allocation problem disguised as a product-policy choice. The newsletter suggests that once AI systems are used continuously, business model assumptions about flat-rate subscriptions break down. That makes infrastructure economics a first-order strategic variable. OpenAI, by raising more capital and vertically integrating more deeply into compute, is portrayed as better positioned to tolerate this demand and therefore win users who want unrestricted agent workflows.

The issue also argues that AI adoption is organizationally uneven in a way that the usual technical versus non-technical framing misses. What matters more is whether someone has crossed from abstract awareness into concrete use. The “aha moment” becomes a threshold after which AI stops looking like a toy and starts looking like leverage. Examples such as inbox summarization, recruiting support, and workflow automation are used to make this threshold feel practical rather than visionary.

A second recurring idea is that AI-assisted work separates rough structure from final judgment. The workflow section on generating pages in HTML before refining them in Figma is emblematic: AI can accelerate the first pass, but taste still determines whether the output feels considered. That same distinction appears in the authorship note, which separates having ideas from drafting them, and in the hiring note, which implies that AI abundance shifts value from production volume toward curation and reuse.

Overall, the newsletter captures a familiar but increasingly important pattern: AI advantage is not simply model access. It comes from combining tool fluency, workflow design, judgment, and infrastructure awareness. The publication’s experiment in format mirrors its thesis about work itself: shorter loops, tighter feedback, and more direct contact with the tools.
