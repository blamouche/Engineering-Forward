# OpenAI is making its own phone to compete with the iPhone: report
**Source**: https://9to5mac.com/2026/04/27/openai-is-making-its-own-phone-to-compete-with-the-iphone-report/
**Date**: April 27, 2026
**Author**: Ryan Christoffel
**Keywords**: OpenAI, hardware, smartphone, Jony Ive, AI agents

## Elevator pitch
The report says OpenAI is now exploring a smartphone as a core AI hardware surface, suggesting the company sees operating systems and device interfaces as strategic territory in the agent era.

## Takeaways
- Analyst Ming-Chi Kuo says OpenAI is working with chipset and manufacturing partners on a phone project targeting 2028 mass production.
- The project would extend OpenAI’s broader hardware collaboration with Jony Ive beyond speakers or ambient devices.
- The article frames the smartphone as a still-central distribution layer for AI, rather than a device category about to be displaced.
- Sam Altman’s comments about rethinking operating systems and interfaces reinforce the idea that OpenAI wants influence over the computing surface itself.
- If real, the move would place OpenAI in more direct competition with Apple, Google, and other platform owners.

## Synthesis
This report is less important for the product rumor itself than for what it suggests about OpenAI’s strategic direction. For months, the company’s hardware ambitions had been described as adjacent to the phone: speakers, glasses, cameras, or new ambient devices designed with Jony Ive. A smartphone project changes the meaning of that effort. Instead of inventing a new category around AI, OpenAI may be concluding that the most important AI device is still the one people already carry all day.

The article leans on analyst supply-chain reporting, so the operational details should be treated cautiously. Still, the claimed setup is notable. If OpenAI is already exploring suppliers such as MediaTek, Qualcomm, and Luxshare for a 2028 production window, then the company is moving beyond design speculation and into platform planning. That would imply a serious belief that AI-native interfaces need tighter hardware and software integration than current partner ecosystems can provide.

The larger argument is about control of the user relationship. If AI agents become the main way people search, plan, buy, and coordinate work, then the operating system becomes a high-leverage layer again. Altman’s public comment about rethinking operating systems and interfaces fits this framing. OpenAI may not want to remain only an application inside Apple’s or Google’s world. It may want to shape the interaction model directly.

That matters because the phone is still the default personal computer for much of the world. Even if AI makes assistants more ambient, identity, payments, sensors, notifications, and always-available access are still bundled most effectively inside the smartphone. A dedicated OpenAI phone would give the company a way to experiment with agent-first workflows, persistent context, and new interface conventions without waiting for incumbent platforms to expose the right hooks.

The article also indirectly challenges the common assumption that AI will mainly create lightweight companion devices. The stronger possibility is that AI reinforces the value of the central device while changing how people use it. In that scenario, the fight is not over whether the phone disappears, but over who defines the agent layer that sits on top of it. This rumor matters because it places OpenAI in that fight explicitly.
