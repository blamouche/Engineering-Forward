# Why experts writing AI evals is creating the fastest-growing companies in history | Brendan Foody (CEO of Mercor)

**Source**: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/experts-writing-ai-evals-brendan-foody
**Date**: April 22, 2026
**Author**: Lenny Rachitsky
**Keywords**: Mercor, evals, expert labor, AI startups, Brendan Foody

## Elevator pitch
Brendan Foody argues that expert-written evals and training data have become one of the main bottlenecks in AI progress, which is why companies that can supply high-quality expert judgment are growing at extraordinary speed.

## Takeaways
- Mercor’s growth thesis is that AI progress is increasingly constrained by access to expert evaluators.
- Foody frames evals as both product requirements and model-improvement infrastructure.
- The business opportunity sits at the intersection of frontier labs and scarce specialized human knowledge.
- The conversation suggests many jobs with elastic, high-level judgment may remain valuable longer than expected.
- The interview illustrates how AI value is shifting toward data quality and evaluation quality, not only raw compute.

## Synthesis
Brendan Foody’s core argument is that the AI industry has moved into an “era of evals,” where the bottleneck is no longer only compute or model architecture. It is expert judgment. Frontier labs need people who can produce high-quality training data, evaluate outputs rigorously, and translate subtle domain knowledge into signals models can learn from. Mercor’s rapid growth is presented as a consequence of sitting directly on top of that constraint.

That framing is persuasive because it matches what many AI teams have started to discover. Strong base models are necessary, but getting them to perform reliably in valuable domains often depends on fine-grained evaluation and carefully curated supervision. Those inputs are hard to scale with generic crowdsourcing alone. You need clinicians, lawyers, finance specialists, high-end developers, and other experts who know what good looks like.

The interview also has a broader labor-market angle. If AI systems improve by absorbing expert judgment, then some forms of high-skill work may remain economically valuable precisely because they are difficult to commoditize quickly. That does not mean automation stops. It means the shape of labor demand changes. People who can create, validate, or refine expert-quality signals may become more important even as lower-level execution gets cheaper.

The deeper lesson is that AI competition is not only about who has the best model or the most GPUs. It is also about who can secure the most useful feedback loops for improving those systems. Companies like Mercor are benefiting from that shift because they help labs convert scarce human expertise into scalable model progress. That makes evaluation infrastructure look less like a side industry and more like a core part of the AI stack.
