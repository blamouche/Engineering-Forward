# The Economics of Generative AI: Two Years Later
**Source**: https://apoorv03.com/p/the-economics-of-generative-ai-two
**Date**: April 1, 2026
**Author**: Apoorv Agrawal
**Keywords**: AI economics, NVIDIA, hyperscalers, OpenAI, Anthropic, semiconductors, infrastructure, applications, margins

## Elevator pitch
The AI industry has grown from $90B to $435B in two years but remains structurally inverted: semiconductors (led by NVIDIA at ~$250B) capture 79% of gross profits while applications (OpenAI + Anthropic at 75% of $60B) capture only ~33% margins.

## Takeaways
- Three-layer stack: semiconductors (~$300B, ~73% margins), infrastructure (~$75B, ~55% margins), applications (~$60B, ~33% margins)
- NVIDIA captures ~$250B of semiconductor revenue (~80% layer share); Broadcom at ~$34B; hyperscaler direct chip purchases ~$25B
- OpenAI and Anthropic command ~75% of application layer revenue (~$45B combined)
- Top 5 hyperscalers spent $443B in 2025 (up 73% YoY); ~$450B projected for 2026
- Applications would need over a decade at current rates to reach cloud-era profit levels — assuming custom silicon doesn't compress margins first

## Synthesis
Agrawal's two-year retrospective documents an industry that has grown dramatically while maintaining an unusual economic structure. In traditional tech markets, the application layer captures the majority of value — software has higher margins than hardware infrastructure. In AI, this pattern is currently reversed: semiconductor manufacturers capture the largest share of gross profit, while application companies operate at margins that would be unremarkable in traditional software.

The semiconductor dominance reflects NVIDIA's structural position. Data centers are investing at unprecedented rates — $443B in 2025 at 73% year-over-year growth — and NVIDIA supplies the GPUs that power virtually all frontier model training and inference. With GPU supply constrained relative to demand, NVIDIA commands pricing power that produces the ~73% gross margins driving semiconductor layer dominance. This is an infrastructure spending wave being channeled through a near-monopoly supplier.

The application layer concentration is equally striking from the opposite direction. OpenAI and Anthropic together represent 75% of application layer revenue, suggesting that despite the proliferation of AI applications across industries, revenue is consolidating at the foundation model providers rather than at vertical application companies. This is unusual compared to the cloud era, where the application layer was highly fragmented across many successful companies.

The hyperscaler custom silicon development — Google's TPUs, Amazon's Trainium, Microsoft's Azure Maia, Meta's MTIA — represents the systematic attempt to reduce NVIDIA dependency. If successful, custom silicon would compress semiconductor layer margins over time by creating supply alternatives. The timeline for this compression is uncertain; NVIDIA's software ecosystem (CUDA) creates switching costs that extend beyond hardware capability comparisons.

The decade-long timeline for applications to reach cloud-era profit levels is grounded in the current margin trajectory. For applications to capture cloud-era proportions of gross profit, they would need either dramatically higher margins (requiring stronger pricing power as models commoditize) or much larger revenue at current margins (requiring continued growth). Both require continued AI adoption expansion rather than market maturation.
