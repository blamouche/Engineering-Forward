# The Android of Commerce - How Google Is Building the Interface Between AI & Money
**Source**: https://linas.substack.com/p/fintechpulse1045
**Date**: 2026-04-28
**Author**: Linas Beliūnas
**Keywords**: Google, WebMCP, payments, commerce, agent protocols

## Elevator pitch
The article argues that Google is stacking open protocols into an agentic commerce operating system, using openness and distribution to control how AI agents browse, transact, and pay.

## Takeaways
- Google’s payments, commerce, and browser protocols are presented as a coordinated stack rather than isolated launches.
- WebMCP is framed as the missing browser-native bridge that lets websites expose structured tools to agents.
- The strategic play is compared to Android: open infrastructure plus ecosystem control through distribution.
- The real prize is influence over the interface between AI systems and financial transactions.
- Companies in payments and banking may face dependency on protocol layers they do not control.

## Synthesis
This piece expands the protocol argument by describing Google’s recent launches as parts of a single operating system for AI-driven commerce. Instead of treating payments APIs, commerce standards, and browser capabilities as separate announcements, the author combines them into one strategic narrative. The result is a persuasive claim that Google is trying to define the interface between intelligent agents and the commercial web.

The article identifies three layers in that stack. The first is payments, where Google introduced a protocol for agent-compatible transactions. The second is commerce, where the Universal Commerce Protocol helps standardize product discovery and purchasing behavior. The third is browser interaction, where WebMCP allows websites to expose structured tools directly to agents rather than forcing them to navigate pages like unreliable screen-scraping bots. Each layer solves a discrete technical problem, but together they create a controlled path from intent to execution.

The strongest insight is that the story is not really about efficiency, even though efficiency gains matter. Of course it is better if agents can call functions instead of guessing at buttons and DOM structures. But the larger implication is governance over a new commercial surface. If AI systems become routine buyers, then whoever defines the interfaces they use can shape discovery, transaction flow, data access, and platform economics. That is why the article compares the strategy to Android: make the infrastructure open enough to attract broad ecosystem participation, then capture value through reach, defaults, and coordination power.

For banks, payment companies, and commerce platforms, the article suggests a familiar but uncomfortable future. They may continue to participate in transactions while losing control over the user-facing layer that determines how those transactions originate. In that world, margins compress at the execution layer while strategic leverage accumulates at the orchestration layer. The companies that own the standards and distribution points can influence the market even when they do not own every downstream participant.

The article is especially useful because it treats protocols as competitive strategy, not mere engineering detail. It shows how a company can use standards to make itself central to a new ecosystem without insisting on full vertical ownership. Whether or not Google ultimately wins, the piece captures a real shift: the battle for AI commerce may be decided less by which chatbot is smartest and more by who controls the interfaces through which agents touch money.
