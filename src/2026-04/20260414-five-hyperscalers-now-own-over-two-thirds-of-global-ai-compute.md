# Five hyperscalers now own over two-thirds of global AI compute
**Source**: https://epochai.substack.com/p/five-hyperscalers-now-own-over-two
**Date**: April 14, 2026
**Author**: Luke Emberson, Josh You, Venkat Somala
**Keywords**: AI compute concentration, hyperscalers, Google, Microsoft, Meta, Amazon, Oracle, AI infrastructure

## Elevator pitch
Five dominant tech companies now command two-thirds of global AI computing resources, creating significant dependencies for AI researchers and startups while concentrating gatekeeping power over technological advancement in relatively few hands.

## Takeaways
- Five companies—Google, Microsoft, Meta, Amazon, and Oracle—control approximately two-thirds of global AI computational capacity, up from ~60% at the start of 2024
- Major AI laboratories including OpenAI and Anthropic depend almost entirely on these hyperscalers for access to compute
- This concentration gives hyperscalers significant influence over which AI projects receive computational resources
- Smaller organizations face high costs and limited alternatives when accessing necessary computational capacity
- The lack of diversified computational sources creates potential systemic risks if major providers experience disruptions

## Synthesis
The technology landscape has undergone a dramatic consolidation in artificial intelligence infrastructure. Five major corporations—Google, Microsoft, Meta, Amazon, and Oracle—have accumulated control over about two-thirds of the world's compute, up slightly from approximately 60% at the start of 2024. This trend represents an accelerating concentration of computational resources in the hands of a small number of organizations.

This consolidation has profound implications for the broader AI ecosystem. Many AI labs including OpenAI and Anthropic depend almost entirely on these hyperscalers for access to their compute. This dependency creates a critical vulnerability in the AI development pipeline, where innovation increasingly relies on infrastructure controlled by a handful of corporations. Emerging startups and independent research institutions face significant barriers to accessing the computational resources necessary for training advanced models.

The data originates from Epoch AI's new AI Chip Owners datahub, suggesting this represents the most current assessment of market distribution. The modest uptick from 60% to approximately 67% over roughly two years indicates an ongoing trend of centralization rather than a sudden shift, yet the trajectory remains concerning for those advocating for decentralized AI development.

The implications extend beyond mere market concentration. When computational capacity becomes scarce and controlled by relatively few entities, these organizations effectively gatekeep AI advancement. They determine which projects receive resources, which research directions receive funding, and ultimately which innovations reach the market. This creates potential bottlenecks for beneficial AI applications while potentially accelerating development of projects aligned with these corporations' strategic interests.

Economic implications are equally significant. Organizations seeking to develop or deploy AI systems must negotiate with these hyperscalers, giving them considerable pricing power and negotiating leverage. This dynamic may disadvantage smaller competitors and limit market competition in AI-derived products and services. There are counterarguments worth considering—these hyperscalers invest heavily in infrastructure development, and their scale enables efficiencies in computing resource allocation. Nevertheless, the concentration remains noteworthy regardless of its causes, and raises questions about resilience, diversity, and equitable access to the infrastructure underlying the AI economy.
