# Scoop: NSA using Anthropic’s Mythos despite blacklist

**Source**: https://www.axios.com/2026/04/19/nsa-anthropic-mythos-pentagon
**Date**: April 19, 2026
**Author**: Maria Curi, Sam Sabin
**Keywords**: anthropic, mythos, nsa, pentagon, cybersecurity

## Elevator pitch
Axios reports that the NSA is using Anthropic’s Mythos Preview even while senior Defense Department officials publicly frame Anthropic as a supply-chain risk, highlighting how capability needs are overruling political hostility.

## Takeaways
- The NSA is reportedly using Mythos Preview despite the Pentagon’s public blacklist posture toward Anthropic.
- The story suggests operational cybersecurity needs are winning out over interagency politics.
- Mythos is treated as strategically valuable enough to bypass normal reputational and procurement resistance.
- The gap between public criticism and private usage exposes how dependent government actors already are on frontier AI capability.
- The dispute also underscores the growing national-security importance of model access and vendor trust.

## Synthesis
Axios reports a striking contradiction inside the U.S. national security establishment: the National Security Agency is using Anthropic’s Mythos Preview even though senior Defense Department officials have cast Anthropic as a supply-chain risk. The basic significance is not only the scoop itself, but what it reveals about the hierarchy of incentives. When a model appears meaningfully useful for cybersecurity or intelligence work, practical need can outrun official hostility. That makes the Pentagon’s feud with Anthropic look less like a hard operational block and more like a contested political signal. The story points to a broader pattern in frontier AI adoption, where the institutions most concerned about safety, control, and vendor dependence are also among the least able to ignore capability advantages. If Mythos is good enough to be used under those conditions, it implies that model performance and mission relevance are already reshaping procurement norms. The article also reinforces the idea that AI competition is increasingly a state capacity issue rather than just a commercial platform story. Access to specific models, confidence in their behavior, and judgments about supplier risk now sit at the intersection of defense policy and day to day operations. The result is a messy but revealing reality: public blacklists and private deployments can coexist when the underlying strategic pressure is high enough.
