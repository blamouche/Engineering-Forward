The mirror test originates in comparative animal cognition research — the experiment in which an animal is observed to determine whether it recognizes its own reflection, commonly used as a proxy for self-awareness. Great apes, dolphins, elephants, and a small number of other species pass the test; most do not. Applying an analogous concept to large language models poses immediate challenges because LLMs lack the continuous identity and persistent embodiment that make the original test meaningful.

A "mirror test for LLMs" in the LessWrong context likely refers to attempts to probe whether LLMs have some form of accurate self-model — whether they can correctly describe their own capabilities and limitations, predict their own outputs, or identify themselves in some meaningful sense. This is distinct from the original visual recognition test and more analogous to metacognitive self-assessment.

The LessWrong community's interest in this question connects to broader debates about AI consciousness, deceptive alignment, and whether current LLMs have any properties that should be morally considered. A model that has an accurate self-model is more tractable from an alignment perspective because its stated beliefs about its own capabilities are more reliable.

The practical implications of LLM self-modeling accuracy are significant for deployment: models that accurately represent their own uncertainty and capability limits are more trustworthy in high-stakes applications than models whose self-assessments are systematically biased.
