# Your Best AI Strategy Starts at the Top

**Source**: https://every.to/p/your-best-ai-strategy-starts-at-the-top
**Date**: April 7, 2026
**Author**: Natalia Quintero and Mike Taylor
**Keywords**: executives, AI adoption, leadership, organizational design, management, skills

## Elevator pitch
Natalia Quintero and Mike Taylor argue that executives should stop treating AI like a normal software procurement problem and instead approach it like managing a new class of highly capable but error-prone employees whose effectiveness depends on leadership judgment.

## Takeaways
- Leadership teams cannot sensibly set AI strategy without using the tools themselves.
- The authors argue AI behaves less like a software product and more like a junior-to-mid-level worker that needs delegation, evaluation, and context.
- Senior leaders have an advantage with AI because they understand what “good” looks like in their domain.
- A major opportunity is turning tacit organizational knowledge into reusable AI skills, evaluation frameworks, and documentation.
- The article proposes five concrete actions: suspend disbelief, use tools daily, evaluate outputs fairly, hire for taste, and improve organizational documentation.

## Synthesis
This article makes a useful shift in framing. Instead of asking executives to become technical experts, it argues that they should recognize AI management as an extension of work they already know how to do. The claim is not that AI is simple, but that its practical use depends less on product comparison and more on managerial habits: setting goals, defining quality, delegating clearly, and checking outputs critically.

That framing matters because many leadership teams still treat AI as a procurement category. They compare vendors, features, integrations, and pricing as if they were buying another SaaS tool. Quintero and Taylor argue that this approach fails because frontier AI does not create value automatically once plugged into a stack. It requires active direction. In their analogy, AI is closer to a new employee than to a traditional piece of software. It can produce a lot of work quickly, but only if given context, structure, and feedback.

The article is especially strong on the idea that seniority remains valuable in an AI-native organization. The common assumption is that younger, more tool-native employees will dominate because they know the interfaces better. The authors push back by arguing that experienced leaders possess the scarcer asset: judgment. They know what a good decision, a good draft, or a sound analysis looks like inside their business. That makes them unusually well positioned to guide AI effectively, even if they are slower to adopt the tools at first.

Another important argument concerns institutional knowledge. Organizations rely heavily on tacit expertise that lives in people rather than systems: how to evaluate investments, what the brand voice really is, which product requests matter, what warning signs precede attrition. The authors argue AI makes it more feasible to codify that knowledge into repeatable skills and evaluation processes. That turns scattered expertise into reusable organizational infrastructure instead of leaving it trapped in individuals.

The five recommended actions are practical and coherent. “Suspend disbelief” is really a reminder to test tools empirically rather than dismiss them abstractly. “Get your hands dirty” emphasizes direct usage over secondhand opinion. “Be a fair evaluator” stresses measurement and comparison instead of anecdote. “Hire for taste” recognizes that low-cost execution increases the premium on judgment. “Treat your company like a file system” underscores that documentation and structure are prerequisites for effective AI use.

The broader implication is that AI strategy is becoming a leadership discipline, not an innovation side project. Companies that wait for certainty may discover that their competitors have already converted managerial know-how into scalable systems. In that world, the strategic bottleneck is not access to models alone. It is whether the people at the top are willing to engage directly enough to define how intelligence should be used across the organization.
