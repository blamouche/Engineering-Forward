## Elevator pitch
Generic AI prompt advice is ineffective; Linas Beliūnas presents 10 framework-based prompt skills using measurable constraints and named methodologies (SCQA, PAS, AIDA) that fundamentally alter model output structure across writing, research, coding, and analysis.

## Takeaways
- Contrast: vague "write concisely" vs. specific "Executive brief: 50-75 words, first sentence must be a conclusion, zero background context"
- Measurable constraints change output structure, not just model effort level
- Named methodologies (SCQA, PAS, AIDA) provide proven real-world structures applicable across AI platforms
- Completion criteria — severity ratings, evidence grades, "done when" checkpoints — prevent vague, non-committal responses
- Ten skills apply across writing, research, video, code review, competitive analysis, and workflow planning

## Synthesis
The distinction Beliūnas draws — between vague directives and specific, measurable constraints — identifies a practical failure mode in AI usage. "Write concisely" tells the model to reduce something without defining what reduction means. "50-75 words, first sentence must be a conclusion, zero background context" specifies the exact structural requirements, leaving no ambiguity about what success looks like.

This distinction matters because AI models optimize for plausible continuation of the prompt, not for the intent behind vague qualifiers. A model given "write concisely" has no reliable way to determine what length the user considers concise, so it falls back on whatever length pattern its training suggests for similar prompts. A model given specific word count constraints has an unambiguous target and can structure its output accordingly.

The named methodology approach extends this principle to higher-level structure. SCQA (Situation-Complication-Question-Answer), PAS (Problem-Agitate-Solution), and AIDA (Attention-Interest-Desire-Action) are communication frameworks with established usage patterns across professional writing, consulting, and marketing. By specifying these frameworks rather than vague structural directives like "make it persuasive," users leverage the model's training on documents that used these frameworks successfully.

Completion criteria — "done when" checkpoints and quality gates like severity ratings and evidence grades — address the non-committal response problem. Models tend toward hedged, qualified responses that avoid strong claims. Requiring a severity rating (1-5) for identified issues or an evidence grade (Strong/Moderate/Weak) for findings forces the model to commit to assessments rather than presenting infinite caveats.

The practical implication is that prompt engineering skill is less about discovering prompting tricks and more about applying communication and specification practices that work in human-to-human contexts to human-to-AI interactions. Clear acceptance criteria for human deliverables produce better outcomes; the same principle applies to AI prompts.
