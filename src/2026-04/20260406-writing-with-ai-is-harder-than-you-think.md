# Writing With AI is Harder Than You Think

**Source**: https://every.to/working-overtime/writing-with-ai-is-harder-than-you-think
**Date**: April 6, 2026
**Author**: Katie Parrott
**Keywords**: AI writing, editorial workflow, Claude, writing agents, criticism, style guides, Every

## Elevator pitch
Katie Parrott argues that serious AI-assisted writing is not shortcutting thought but restructuring the editorial process around interviewing, outlining, critique, and repeated revision—while still demanding taste and judgment from the human.

## Takeaways
- The strongest defense of AI writing is to make the process visible rather than let critics imagine lazy autocomplete.
- Parrott treats AI as an editorial workflow with phases, critics, and style checks, not as a one-shot text generator.
- Interviewing the writer before drafting helps surface genuine thinking instead of replacing it.
- Good AI writing still depends on human judgment about argument, pacing, and what is not good enough yet.
- The hard part of AI-assisted writing is often better taste and revision discipline, not fewer keystrokes.

## Synthesis
Katie Parrott’s essay is persuasive because it refuses the lazy binary at the center of the AI-writing discourse. Critics often imagine the worst-case workflow: type prompt, receive prose, publish slop. Parrott instead shows a process where AI acts more like an editorial apparatus—interviewer, outliner, critic, line editor—while the human remains responsible for the thinking, selection, and taste that turn raw material into writing worth reading.

That distinction matters. Writing is not one thing; it is a chain of activities: noticing, clarifying, structuring, drafting, testing, revising. AI can accelerate some of those steps without eliminating the need for judgment. In fact, Parrott’s account suggests the opposite: a serious AI-assisted workflow often demands more explicit judgment because the writer has to choose what to keep, reject, sharpen, or rethink at every phase.

The most compelling part is the interview-first workflow. Having the agent interrogate the writer before drafting forces latent intuitions into language and surfaces the real thesis before any polished prose appears. That feels much closer to thinking-with-tools than outsourcing thought. It also shows why critiques that focus only on “who typed the sentence” miss the larger process question.

The broader takeaway is that AI does not make good writing automatic. It can make the invisible editorial infrastructure more available to individual writers, but it still cannot supply conviction, discernment, or the willingness to throw away weak ideas. That work remains stubbornly human.
