# Amazon CEO takes aim at Nvidia, Intel, Starlink, more in annual shareholder letter

**Source**: https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/09/amazon-ceo-takes-aim-at-nvidia-intel-starlink-more-in-annual-shareholder-letter
**Date**: April 9, 2026
**Author**: TechCrunch
**Keywords**: Amazon, AWS, Trainium, Graviton, Project Kuiper, capex, AI infrastructure, cloud competition

## Elevator pitch
Andy Jassy uses Amazon’s shareholder letter to argue that custom silicon, satellite connectivity, and massive data-center spending are turning Amazon into a broader infrastructure challenger to Nvidia, Intel, and Starlink.

## Takeaways
- Jassy says AWS demand for Trainium chips is so strong that upcoming capacity is nearly sold out well before delivery.
- Amazon is also highlighting Graviton adoption as a direct challenge to Intel’s x86 dominance in cloud compute.
- The letter positions Project Kuiper as a real Starlink competitor with early contracts already signed.
- Amazon frames its roughly $200 billion 2026 capex plan as demand-backed rather than speculative.
- The message to shareholders is that Amazon wants to own more of the infrastructure stack instead of just renting access to others.

## Synthesis
The real significance of Jassy's shareholder letter is not the bravado. It is the way Amazon is trying to reframe itself from a hyperscaler that buys a lot of infrastructure into a company that increasingly wants to define the infrastructure layer on its own terms. Trainium is the clearest example. By stressing price-performance advantages over Nvidia and highlighting future capacity that is already spoken for, Jassy is telling investors that Amazon's chip program is no longer a defensive side project. It is becoming part of the company's core AI and cloud growth story.

The Graviton point matters for the same reason. Custom CPUs were initially easy to view as margin optimization for AWS, but the scale Jassy describes suggests something more strategic. If top customers increasingly run on Amazon-designed silicon, AWS gains both economic leverage and product differentiation. That is the same pattern Apple used with its own chips in consumer devices: tighter vertical control creates cost, performance, and roadmap advantages simultaneously.

Project Kuiper also fits this pattern. Mentioning Delta, AT&T, Vodafone, and NASA in the same letter is Amazon's way of signaling that it is not content to let SpaceX own the narrative around satellite internet. Kuiper is still behind Starlink operationally, but Amazon wants investors to see a credible second infrastructure moat taking shape beyond cloud and retail.

The larger through-line is capex justification. Amazon is spending at a level that invites skepticism, especially in an AI market where bubble fears are everywhere. Jassy's defense is that these investments are not isolated bets. They are mutually reinforcing layers—chips, cloud capacity, networking, and possibly robotics—that all increase Amazon's control over the cost and distribution of compute-heavy products. Whether every claim pays off is still open, but the letter makes one thing clear: Amazon wants to be judged less as a retailer with a cloud division and more as an infrastructure empire competing across the full stack.
