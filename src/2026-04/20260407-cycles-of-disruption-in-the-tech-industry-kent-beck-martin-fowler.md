# Cycles of disruption in the tech industry: with software pioneers Kent Beck & Martin Fowler

**Source**: https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/cycles-of-disruption-in-the-tech
**Date**: April 7, 2026
**Author**: Gergely Orosz
**Keywords**: Kent Beck, Martin Fowler, AI, software engineering, agile, disruption, developer workflows

## Elevator pitch
Gergely Orosz summarizes a live discussion with Kent Beck and Martin Fowler, who compare the AI wave to earlier shifts like microprocessors, object-oriented programming, the internet, and agile, while arguing that today’s disruption is broader, faster, and more entangled with organizational incentives.

## Takeaways
- Beck and Fowler see AI as larger and faster-moving than previous software shifts, even compared with agile and the internet.
- They argue that skepticism is healthy, but must be paired with active experimentation because capabilities change too quickly for static conclusions.
- Both expect a widening gap between developers and teams that learn to use AI well and those that do not.
- They warn that hype cycles, “snake oil,” weak metrics, and managerial fantasies about eliminating programmers are reappearing.
- They also suggest AI may reshape collaboration patterns, with tension between solo work with agents and more social team practices like pairing.

## Synthesis
This discussion is valuable because it places the current AI moment in a longer history of software-industry transitions without pretending it is just another cycle. Fowler and Beck both acknowledge earlier discontinuities such as the microprocessor, the internet, object-oriented programming, and agile methods, but they argue that AI combines technical significance with a speed of adoption that is unusually intense. In prior transitions, the industry often had to be persuaded that the new thing mattered. With AI, that basic argument is largely over.

One of the clearest themes is epistemic instability. Fowler describes the need to stay skeptical while also being skeptical of one’s own skepticism. Beck pushes the same idea from a different angle: the tools change week to week, so any confident static view can become obsolete almost immediately. That makes AI unusual as a professional challenge. The task is not merely to form a judgment about the technology, but to keep updating that judgment through repeated contact with shifting capabilities.

Their reflections on agile are especially useful. They note that agile was often sold as faster, better, cheaper software, but actual adoption was shaped by organizational incentives, consulting theater, and uneven execution. They expect similar patterns with AI. There will be real gains, but also an “AI industrial complex” full of inflated claims and fashionable metrics. That matters because it means the winners will not simply be the companies that buy tools first. They will be the ones that align incentives, define quality, and learn where the tools genuinely help.

Both speakers also highlight a likely divergence in developer outcomes. They do not frame AI as eliminating software engineering outright. Instead, they see it as amplifying people differently depending on how they learn, adapt, and apply judgment. Younger developers may learn faster; experienced developers may work at a higher level; a middle tier that relied on routine implementation may feel the most pressure. This is less a clean replacement story than a redistribution of leverage.

The conversation becomes most interesting when it turns to collaboration. Beck worries that AI could accelerate a return to isolated, tool-mediated work: one person managing many agents instead of engaging deeply with other humans. Fowler is less certain, suggesting pair programming and other collective practices may evolve rather than disappear. The open question is whether AI compresses software work into solo orchestration or frees teams to coordinate at a higher level.

Overall, the piece suggests that the AI transition is not only about productivity. It is about professional identity, organizational design, and the habits that distinguish serious engineering from opportunistic hype. Beck and Fowler’s underlying message is pragmatic: stay curious, keep testing, protect quality, and avoid confusing apparent speed with real progress. That is a grounded way to read a period where disruption is real, but the direction of adaptation is still unsettled.
