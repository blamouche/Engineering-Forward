# Introducing the OpenAI Safety Fellowship

**Source**: https://openai.com/index/introducing-openai-safety-fellowship
**Date**: April 7, 2026
**Author**: OpenAI
**Keywords**: OpenAI, AI safety, alignment, fellowship, research talent, evaluation, governance

## Elevator pitch
OpenAI has launched a new external safety fellowship to fund and mentor researchers working on alignment, robustness, misuse prevention, privacy, and other high-impact questions around advanced AI systems.

## Takeaways
- The fellowship runs from September 2026 to February 2027 and targets external researchers and practitioners.
- Priority areas include safety evaluation, robustness, scalable mitigations, privacy-preserving methods, and agentic oversight.
- Fellows will receive mentorship, a stipend, compute support, and a Berkeley-based optional workspace via Constellation.
- OpenAI says it values demonstrated research ability and technical judgment over formal credentials alone.
- The program is a talent-pipeline move as well as a research-output initiative.

## Synthesis
This fellowship announcement is partly straightforward philanthropy and partly strategic institution-building. OpenAI is trying to strengthen the external safety research ecosystem while also building a recruiting and influence pipeline around the questions it believes matter most. That does not make the program cynical; it just means it serves multiple purposes at once, which is normal for serious research fellowships.

What stands out is the scope of the priority areas. The list spans technical evaluation, scalable mitigations, privacy, agentic oversight, and misuse domains. That suggests OpenAI is acknowledging that “AI safety” is no longer a single alignment problem in the abstract. It is a portfolio of operational, technical, and governance questions that become more urgent as models move into broad deployment.

The program also reflects a broader market reality: safety talent is now a scarce resource. Labs want credible external work, but they also want researchers close enough to their ecosystem to understand real system behavior and constraints. Fellowships are a relatively elegant way to do that without requiring everyone to become an employee.

The deeper takeaway is that safety is becoming professionalized infrastructure. Instead of occasional grants or broad public rhetoric, we are seeing more formal programs designed to generate benchmarks, datasets, and technically grounded work. That is a sign the field is maturing—even if the underlying disagreements about what safety should mean are far from settled.
