# What next for the compute crunch?

**Source**: https://martinalderson.com/posts/what-next-for-the-compute-crunch
**Date**: April 7, 2026
**Author**: Martin Alderson
**Keywords**: AI infrastructure, compute crunch, GPU supply, datacenters, pricing

## Elevator pitch
Martin Alderson argues that the frontier AI market is entering a prolonged compute shortage driven by explosive inference demand, delayed infrastructure rollouts, and hard supply constraints in chips, memory, power, and labor.

## Takeaways
- The author sees a sharp jump in AI usage, especially around coding agents, as evidence that inference demand is accelerating faster than supply.
- Large compute commitments do not instantly create capacity because datacenter, power, cooling, and supply-chain buildouts lag by years.
- GB200 liquid-cooling rollout challenges and memory constraints are described as major bottlenecks.
- Usage rationing can smooth demand but likely cannot fully close the gap if adoption keeps compounding.
- The article predicts price increases as the last major lever once operational and product constraints are exhausted.

## Synthesis
Alderson’s thesis is that the AI industry is still underestimating how violent the supply-demand imbalance may become over the next couple of years. The interesting part is not merely that compute is scarce; it is that demand seems to be compounding at a pace that makes conventional capacity planning look slow and linear.

The essay usefully broadens “compute shortage” beyond GPU availability. The real system includes memory fabrication, power delivery, cooling, datacenter construction, plumbing, networking, and skilled labor. Once model labs operate at gigawatt scale, each of those becomes a gating factor. That makes giant capex announcements look less like solutions and more like reservations against an already stressed industrial pipeline.

The argument about pricing is especially important. If everyone is compute-starved, price may become the only reliable demand-management tool left. That would mark a shift from AI as aggressively subsidized software toward AI as rationed industrial capacity, at least temporarily.

Whether or not every detail of the forecast holds, the piece is strong on one core point: frontier AI economics are increasingly being shaped by physical infrastructure constraints. The next phase of competition will be won not just by model quality, but by who can secure, deploy, and finance scarce capacity fastest.
