# OpenAI slams Anthropic in memo to shareholders as its leading AI rival gains momentum

**Source**: https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/09/openai-slams-anthropic-in-memo-to-shareholders-as-rival-gains-momentum.html
**Date**: April 9, 2026
**Author**: CNBC
**Keywords**: OpenAI, Anthropic, compute, investor memo, AI competition, infrastructure, IPO

## Elevator pitch
OpenAI is telling investors that its biggest strategic edge over Anthropic is not branding or model demos but a faster and larger compute buildout that it believes will compound into lower costs and stronger products.

## Takeaways
- OpenAI says it expects to reach roughly 30 gigawatts of compute by 2030, well ahead of Anthropic’s projected capacity.
- The memo portrays Anthropic as operating on a meaningfully smaller and more constrained infrastructure curve.
- OpenAI argues that better infrastructure compounds into smarter tokens, lower serving costs, and stronger free and paid products.
- The memo arrives as both companies position themselves for possible public-market scrutiny and deeper enterprise competition.
- The fight between the labs is increasingly being framed as an infrastructure race, not just a model leaderboard race.

## Synthesis
The most revealing part of this investor memo is how explicitly OpenAI is choosing to define the contest with Anthropic. It is not emphasizing consumer distribution, brand affinity, or even benchmark superiority. Instead, it is making an infrastructure argument: more compute leads to better models, better models lower the effective cost of intelligence, and that in turn feeds stronger products and more revenue. In other words, OpenAI is pitching itself as a compounding machine whose main moat is scale.

That framing matters because it shifts the AI narrative away from the surface-level product race that dominates media coverage. Public conversation often focuses on whichever company launched the most interesting feature this week. OpenAI is telling investors to care about the production system underneath those launches. If the company can sustain a materially larger compute ramp, it believes the downstream effects will show up everywhere: training, inference economics, developer generosity, and consumer product reach.

The competitive subtext is also clear. Anthropic has tried to present its more conservative approach as thoughtful and disciplined. OpenAI is countering by implying that caution can become constraint in a market where capability and cost curves still reward aggressive scaling. Whether that proves true long term is uncertain, but it is a meaningful disagreement about strategy, not just about execution speed.

The broader implication is that the frontier-model market is looking more like heavy industry. Investors once had room to imagine these companies primarily as software stories. Memos like this push the opposite view: the winners may be the companies that can finance, secure, and efficiently operate absurd amounts of compute. If so, the central question is no longer only which lab has the smartest researchers. It is which one can turn capital, infrastructure, and model progress into a reinforcing loop faster than the rest.
