# Andrej Karpathy’s Method to 10X Your Claude Skills
**Source**: https://linas.substack.com/p/10xclaudeskills
**Date**: March 23, 2026
**Author**: Linas
**Keywords**: Claude Skills, autoresearch, optimization, workflows

## Elevator pitch
A guide to improving Claude Skills reliability using Karpathy’s autoresearch loop, turning inconsistent skills into repeatable, high‑accuracy workflows.

## Takeaways
- Skills often drift without systematic evaluation and feedback loops.
- Karpathy’s autoresearch method can optimize skill performance.
- The approach uses measurable targets and iterative refinement.
- Example improvements show accuracy gains from ~70% to 90%+.
- The guide provides templates and pitfalls to avoid in optimization runs.

## Synthesis
Linas’ essay argues that the main limitation of Claude Skills is reliability: a skill might work most of the time but still drift into inconsistent outputs. The author frames this as a system problem, not a prompt problem. Without feedback loops, skills degrade into “fancy autocomplete.”

The proposed fix is to apply Andrej Karpathy’s autoresearch methodology to skill optimization. Autoresearch is described as a loop that defines a clear objective, evaluates performance, and iteratively refines the artifact based on measurable results. The method was originally built for optimizing ML training code, but the author claims it applies equally well to skills where “good” can be measured. By running structured evaluations and feeding back results, skills can be tuned to behave consistently.

The essay reports concrete improvements: a fundraising skill’s correct pitch‑deck structure improved from ~70% accuracy to 94%, and a sales qualification skill improved from ~65% to 91%. These gains were achieved with a few hours of optimization and minimal cost, suggesting that systematic iteration can yield disproportionate returns.

The guide promises practical assets—step‑by‑step walkthroughs, evaluation templates, and files that automate the optimization loop. It also emphasizes common mistakes that waste runs, reinforcing that structure and measurement are the key to consistent results.

Overall, the piece reframes skill building as an engineering discipline. Instead of relying on ad‑hoc prompting, it encourages users to treat skills like software: define targets, run experiments, and iterate. The result is a more reliable “AI operating system” for repeatable workflows.
