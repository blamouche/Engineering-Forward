# How Main Street Companies Are Using AI
**Source**: https://every.to/thesis/ai-for-boring-businesses
**Date**: 2026-03-10
**Author**: Sam Gerstenzang
**Keywords**: AI implementation, Main Street businesses, funeral homes, medical spas, practical AI adoption, operationally complex businesses

## Elevator pitch
A venture partner shares hard-won lessons from deploying AI in unglamorous, regulated service businesses—proving that competitive advantage lies not in flashy tech but in thoughtfully integrating AI to solve real operational challenges.

## Takeaways
- AI accelerates research cycles: Using ChatGPT, Clay, and Claude reduced their market research and product development timeline from three months to three weeks.
- Outcomes matter more than adoption: Teams that use AI without accountability for results tend to generate poor outputs; emphasis should be on improved quality and speed, not mere tool usage.
- AI-powered marketing follows familiar patterns: Generative Engine Optimization mirrors SEO tactics, and this channel will become pay-to-play as OpenAI monetizes ChatGPT results.
- Human workers prove difficult to replace: Even when AI performs identically to humans, customers show greater forgiveness toward people, leading to lower churn in customer-facing roles.
- Integration complexity exceeds initial implementation: Moving AI from pilot to production requires ongoing engineering investment for edge cases, maintenance, and system integration.

## Synthesis
Sam Gerstenzang argues that the AI hype cycle has overlooked a crucial opportunity: operationally complex, Main Street service businesses that lack the "Y Combinator darling" profile but offer genuine resilience. Through ventures like Meadow (a contemporary funeral home) and Moxie (a platform for independent aesthetic clinics), his incubator Boulton and Watt has discovered that AI's real value lies not in replacing business models but in amplifying existing operations.

The firm's most tangible success involves research acceleration. By combining ChatGPT for market analysis, Clay for lead enrichment, and Claude for transcript analysis, they've compressed multi-month discovery processes into weeks. However, this advantage proved conditional on organizational discipline. Early "AI initiatives" failed because teams treated AI adoption as a checkbox rather than a means to better outcomes.

Gerstenzang challenges prevailing assumptions about AI's disruptive power. Customer acquisition through AI-generated signals mirrors Google's SEO playbook: high-intent queries followed by algorithmic gaming. This pattern will likely collapse as AI platforms monetize their feeds, mirroring Google's dominance. More intriguingly, his receptionist automation pilot revealed that even when AI and humans performed identically, customers churned more readily from AI-powered services. This suggests that perceived forgivability—the human advantage—remains economically significant.

Perhaps most sobering is the infrastructure gap. Generating a landing page in an hour looks impressive until integration demands arise. Good engineers remain expensive; edge cases, maintenance, and rollout complexity consume engineering hours that AI-generated code merely defers rather than eliminates.

Gerstenzang concludes by invoking Costco's dot-com survival strategy: adopt technology when it genuinely serves customers, not because the industry deems it necessary. For Main Street businesses, this pragmatism offers competitive advantage precisely because so few adopt AI systematically. The lesson isn't that AI transforms everything—it's that thoughtful, outcome-focused integration in unglamorous sectors may prove more durable than headline-grabbing breakthroughs in venture-backed software.
