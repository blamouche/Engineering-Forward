# What Do AI-Based Layoffs Say About TAM?
**Source**: https://www.elliotcsmith.com/what-do-ai-based-layoffs-say-about-tam/
**Date**: 2026-03-16
**Author**: Elliot Smith
**Keywords**: AI layoffs, TAM, growth ceiling, SaaS, WiseTech, Block, Atlassian, efficiency, valuation

## Elevator pitch
Elliot Smith argues that AI-driven efficiency layoffs at SaaS companies like WiseTech, Block, and Atlassian inadvertently expose growth ceilings that contradict the high-revenue multiples their valuations depend on.

## Takeaways
- Companies claiming 2x efficiency gains from AI should theoretically be able to double output—but if that doesn't increase revenue proportionally, they've revealed a growth ceiling
- WiseTech, Block, and Atlassian are cutting headcount while citing AI productivity improvements
- If AI doubled efficiency but revenue doesn't double, the extra efficiency has no market to absorb it
- This tension contradicts the high-growth tech valuations premised on substantial future expansion
- Upcoming financial results will reveal whether efficiency gains translate to sustained business improvement or mask growth constraints

## Synthesis
Elliot Smith identifies a logical trap embedded in the efficiency justifications that technology companies have been using for AI-driven workforce reductions. The argument goes like this: if AI genuinely doubled engineering efficiency—the claim underlying many recent layoff announcements—then cutting staff by 10-15% should not merely preserve output, it should dramatically increase it. If that increased output doesn't translate into proportionally increased revenue, the inescapable conclusion is that the company is operating near its revenue ceiling.

The companies in Smith's analysis—WiseTech, Block, and Atlassian—are emblematic of a broader category: mature SaaS businesses with defensible market positions but increasingly bounded growth opportunities. For years, their valuations have been premised on continued high-growth rates sustained by expanding TAM (total addressable market). But if AI makes their engineers twice as productive and revenue doesn't respond proportionally, the growth premium embedded in their stock prices may be misplaced.

Smith is careful not to frame this as a certainty—he acknowledges that efficiency gains could drive growth through lower costs enabling product investment, through expansion into new market segments previously too expensive to address, or through quality improvements that increase customer retention. These alternative paths exist, but they require active strategy to realize and are harder to execute than the narrative of efficiency-equals-growth implies.

The financial results test is clear: in the quarters following these layoff announcements, do the companies show accelerating revenue growth that validates the efficiency argument, stable revenue growth that suggests the efficiency gained is being reinvested, or decelerating growth that suggests the growth ceiling hypothesis is correct? The answer will have implications not just for individual stock prices but for the valuation frameworks applied to the entire category of established SaaS businesses claiming AI transformation.
