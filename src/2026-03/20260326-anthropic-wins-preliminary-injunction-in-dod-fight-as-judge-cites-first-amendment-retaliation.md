# Anthropic wins preliminary injunction in DOD fight as judge cites 'First Amendment retaliation'
**Source**: https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/26/anthropic-pentagon-dod-claude-court-ruling.html
**Date**: March 26, 2026
**Author**: CNBC
**Keywords**: Anthropic, Pentagon, injunction, AI policy, government contracts

## Elevator pitch
A federal judge granted Anthropic a preliminary injunction blocking the Trump administration and the Pentagon from enforcing a ban on Claude, calling the actions likely unconstitutional retaliation.

## Takeaways
- The court paused enforcement of the federal directive and Pentagon blacklisting against Anthropic.
- Judge Rita Lin framed the actions as likely First Amendment retaliation.
- The dispute stems from DoD efforts to label Anthropic a supply-chain risk.
- Anthropic argues the designation harms its business and reputation while litigation continues.
- The case highlights tensions between AI vendors and government procurement over model use constraints.

## Synthesis
A federal judge in San Francisco granted Anthropic a preliminary injunction in its lawsuit against the Trump administration, halting enforcement of a directive that barred federal agencies from using Claude and limiting the Pentagon’s ability to designate the company as a national security risk. Judge Rita Lin’s order, issued two days after a hearing, stated that the government’s actions appeared to be classic First Amendment retaliation for Anthropic’s public criticism of its contracting treatment. While the litigation will continue for months, the injunction offers immediate relief to Anthropic by pausing actions that could inflict reputational and monetary harm.

The dispute traces back to a series of moves by the Department of Defense and the White House. In late February, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth publicly described Anthropic as a supply-chain risk, a label historically reserved for foreign adversaries. Shortly after, the Pentagon notified Anthropic of the designation, and President Trump issued a directive ordering federal agencies to stop using Claude, with a six‑month phase‑out. The designation obliges defense contractors such as Amazon, Microsoft, and Palantir to certify they do not use Anthropic’s models in military work.

Anthropic’s suit argues that the government’s actions exceed statutory authority and punish the company for disagreeing with procurement terms and public policy. In the injunction order, Judge Lin said the relevant statutes do not support branding an American company as a potential adversary for expressing dissent. She emphasized that the government is free to stop using Claude and select another vendor, but that the legal question is whether the government violated the law in doing so.

The conflict is tied to negotiations over how Anthropic’s models would be deployed within the Defense Department. Anthropic previously won a $200 million DoD contract and became the first AI vendor to deploy models on classified networks. However, discussions stalled when the Pentagon sought broad access to Claude for all lawful purposes, while Anthropic sought guarantees that its models would not be used for fully autonomous weapons or domestic mass surveillance. With no agreement, the parties moved into legal confrontation.

The case underscores a broader tension between AI providers and government customers: national security agencies want expansive access and fewer restrictions, while leading labs increasingly insist on usage guardrails to align with their safety policies. The preliminary injunction buys Anthropic time and preserves its ability to serve federal customers while the courts review the legality of the government’s actions. Regardless of the final outcome, the ruling signals that AI procurement disputes are likely to be litigated around constitutional and administrative law, not merely contract terms.
