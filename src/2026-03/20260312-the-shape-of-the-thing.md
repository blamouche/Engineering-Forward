# The Shape of the Thing
**Source**: https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/the-shape-of-the-thing
**Date**: 2026-03-12
**Author**: Ethan Mollick
**Keywords**: AI capability, recursive self-improvement, RSI, co-intelligence, managing AI, StrongDM Software Factory, rolling disruption, autonomous agents

## Elevator pitch
AI has crossed from "co-intelligence" (human-AI collaboration) to the threshold of "managing AIs" as autonomous agents—and recursive self-improvement is no longer science fiction but explicitly on major AI companies' roadmaps.

## Takeaways
- Exponential capability gains across multiple benchmarks: from image generation to complex task completion, AI systems are reaching or exceeding human-level performance on sophisticated challenges.
- Transition from co-intelligence to managing AIs: autonomous agents now handle multi-hour work tasks in minutes, shifting humans from operators to managers.
- StrongDM's Software Factory experiment: "code must not be written by humans"—testing agents validate products without human intervention.
- Rolling disruption: AI capability crossing critical thresholds creates unpredictable cascading effects—a single week in February saw stock market fluctuations, major layoffs, and government conflicts over AI governance.
- Recursive self-improvement (RSI) is explicitly on major AI companies' roadmaps, no longer science fiction.
- Uncertainty is not helplessness: organizations establishing norms for AI integration today set precedents for everyone else.

## Synthesis
Mollick's "shape of the thing" framing attempts to describe the aggregate form of AI's impact rather than individual capabilities—and the shape he identifies is one of accelerating nonlinearity. Individual capability improvements have been visible; what's changing is the interaction effects between capabilities as they reach sufficient levels. An AI that can reason well enough to coordinate other AIs, while those AIs can write code reliably enough to run autonomously, creates emergent organizational capabilities that didn't exist when each piece operated below threshold.

The StrongDM Software Factory example is worth examining carefully. "Code must not be written by humans" is a design constraint, not just a productivity metric—it tests whether a software development organization can function when the primary human activity of that organization (writing code) is removed. The answer appears to be yes, with testing agents providing the validation function. This suggests the organizational model, not just the tools, can be redesigned around AI execution.

Rolling disruption describes a real phenomenon: when AI capabilities cross thresholds in multiple domains within short timeframes, the causal chains between developments become too complex to track, let alone predict. The February "week of disruption" illustrates how capability improvements in one area (autonomous business operation) interact with economic conditions (cost reduction pressures), governance uncertainty (regulatory conflict), and financial markets (expectation repricing) to produce outcomes no individual prediction would have captured.

The RSI observation is the most consequential long-term point. Self-improving AI systems—where AI helps build better AI—would change the pace and direction of capability development in ways that current forecasting frameworks don't capture. That this is now on explicit roadmaps rather than theoretical speculation represents a meaningful shift in the industry's self-reported trajectory.
