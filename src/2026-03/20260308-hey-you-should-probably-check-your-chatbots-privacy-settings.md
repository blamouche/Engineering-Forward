# Hey, You Should Probably Check Your Chatbot’s Privacy Settings
**Source**: https://www.bigtechnology.com/p/hey-you-should-probably-check-your
**Date**: March 08, 2026
**Author**: Unknown
**Keywords**: privacy, data collection, chatbots, AI training, consent

## Elevator pitch
Most major chatbot providers still opt users into training by default, so unless you change settings, your conversations—potentially including sensitive data—can be reused for model training.

## Takeaways
- Leading AI labs default to collecting chat conversations for training unless users opt out.
- Settings are buried and framed as “help improve the model,” which obscures the real trade‑off.
- Sensitive data in chats (medical, financial, personal) increases privacy risk if retained for training.
- Researchers warn that retraining on AI‑generated content can cause model collapse, making user data especially valuable.
- Users can disable training in ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini through specific settings menus.

## Synthesis
The piece argues that most major chatbot platforms quietly rely on an opt‑out model for training, which means users grant permission by default for their conversations to be used as training data. The author emphasizes the personal nature of many chatbot interactions—people increasingly paste in medical, financial, and intimate details—so the default data collection posture creates a real privacy exposure. The argument is not that chatbots are uniquely invasive, but that the interface makes it easy to forget that the data is being captured and repurposed at scale. This is framed as a structural issue: even careful users can slip because the tools feel conversational rather than transactional.

A privacy researcher cited in the article underscores that the settings are hard to find and are phrased in language like “Improve the model for everyone,” which makes opting out feel like a selfish choice rather than a reasonable safeguard. The author interprets this language as a subtle persuasion tactic that hides the underlying reality: the company wants your data for training, and it will get it unless you explicitly refuse. The point is not that training itself is malicious, but that consent is not truly informed if it depends on users hunting through menus and deciphering ambiguous copy.

The article adds a strategic angle from the AI labs’ perspective. After years of scraping public web data, the most valuable remaining source of fresh, human‑generated text is now direct user conversations with AI assistants. That makes the data in chats especially coveted. The researcher notes an additional technical risk: models can degrade if they are trained too heavily on AI‑generated data, which means high‑quality human conversations are even more precious. This incentive intensifies the pressure for labs to retain and reuse user interactions, reinforcing the opt‑out default.

The author acknowledges that companies say they strip identifying information and apply guardrails to prevent personal data from being regurgitated. But the scale and complexity of the data make it difficult to guarantee safety, especially when the data includes high‑stakes personal details. That leads to the practical recommendation: users should proactively change settings to opt out of training. The piece provides concrete steps for ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini, each of which has a toggle in privacy or data controls that stops training on conversations.

Overall, the article is a call for user vigilance and a critique of the current consent model. It frames privacy not as a hypothetical concern but as a concrete, everyday issue for people who already rely on AI assistants. The implied policy stance is that opt‑in should be the default, or at least that the opt‑out should be transparent and easily discoverable. Until then, users need to treat chatbots as data‑collection systems by default and actively manage privacy settings if they want to keep their conversations out of training pipelines.
