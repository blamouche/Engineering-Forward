# Do AI-enabled companies need fewer people?
**Source**: https://seldo.com/posts/do-ai-enabled-companies-need-fewer-people/
**Date**: 2026-03-08
**Author**: Laurie Voss
**Keywords**: artificial intelligence, startups, hiring, venture capital, tech layoffs, compute-for-labor substitution

## Elevator pitch
AI-native startups operate with significantly smaller teams while raising larger funding rounds, suggesting that compute is genuinely replacing labor—though the predicted wave of new tech jobs hasn't materialized yet.

## Takeaways
- Structural Shift in Startup Economics: Seed-stage startups now average under 3.5 employees (down from 6.4 in 2022), while Series A companies maintain 47 headcount versus 57 in 2020.
- AI Startups Show Extreme Efficiency: AI-native companies achieve 6x higher revenue per employee ($3.48M vs. $580K) and require approximately 60% fewer workers at $10M annual recurring revenue.
- Record Funding Despite Smaller Teams: In February 2026, venture capital hit "$189 billion flowed into startups in 28 days," yet hiring growth has flatlined since 2023.
- Divergence Between Capital and Jobs: While AI companies captured roughly 50% of the "$425 billion into startups globally in 2025," new tech job creation hasn't followed traditional boom patterns.
- Compute-for-Labor Substitution: The data confirms companies' claims about operating with fewer people, but this hasn't unlocked the broader economic expansion previously anticipated.

## Synthesis
Laurie Voss examines whether AI truly enables companies to operate with reduced workforces or whether the pattern simply reflects prior over-hiring. The evidence strongly supports the former. Across multiple datasets from Carta, Revelio Labs, and Crunchbase, startups have demonstrably shrunk their headcounts while expanding funding rounds—a reversal of historical tech-boom patterns where more capital typically meant more hiring.

The contrast between AI-native and traditional SaaS companies is particularly stark. AI startups achieve $51.9M average Series A rounds compared to $39.9M for conventional competitors, yet maintain teams roughly 40% smaller. At the $10M revenue milestone, AI companies operate with 15-20 employees versus 50-70 for traditional software firms.

What makes this trend notable is its consistency across the entire startup ecosystem. Monthly hiring fell over 50% between early 2022 and early 2024, while 2025 saw more than 126,000 tech workers laid off, with dismissals continuing into 2026. This isn't isolated belt-tightening but systematic structural change.

However, Voss identifies a crucial disconnect: her previous prediction anticipated that while AI would automate existing roles, it would unlock new demand and job creation. Instead, tech employment growth has flatlined since 2023, creating what she describes as another "K-shaped graph"—divergence between haves and have-nots rather than broad expansion.

The author remains optimistic that additional companies and capabilities will eventually materialize, yet candidly acknowledges the predicted job boom hasn't arrived. The fundamental insight is that startups are clearly "substituting compute for labor at an increasing rate." Whether this represents temporary dislocation or permanent structural change remains the critical unanswered question underlying the AI revolution's employment implications.

For investors, founders, and policymakers, the data presents a challenging picture: unprecedented capital flow into AI startups combined with shrinking employment bases raises serious questions about AI's distributional effects on the broader economy. The efficiency gains are real, but their benefits appear concentrated rather than diffuse.
