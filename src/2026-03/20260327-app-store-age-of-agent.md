# App Store | Age of Agent
**Source**: https://robonomics.substack.com/p/app-store-age-of-agent
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Unknown
**Keywords**: agents, discovery, payments, MCP, platform economics

## Elevator pitch
The piece argues the App Store model will be unbundled in the agent era, with open connection protocols commoditizing integration while discovery and payments become the primary battlegrounds for value capture.

## Takeaways
- MCP (Model Context Protocol) makes service connection a commodity, not a moat.
- The App Store’s four functions (discovery, distribution, trust, payments) will separate for agents.
- Discovery is the most strategic layer because agents decide and transact for users.
- Payment aggregation could be lucrative but less monopolistic without Apple‑style lock‑in.
- Platform power will hinge on ranking algorithms and preference data governance.

## Synthesis
The essay frames the rise of AI agents as a structural break from the App Store era. Apple’s App Store bundled discovery, distribution, trust, and payments into a single, tightly controlled layer because humans needed a safe, curated marketplace to browse, install, and pay for apps. Agents don’t browse; they execute. When a user asks an agent to book a table or buy a product, the agent needs APIs, not a GUI. That changes where value sits in the stack.

The author argues that connection will be commoditized by open protocols such as MCP, which make it easy for agents to plug into services via a standard interface. If every service can be reached through a universal connector, the connection layer offers little room for monopolistic capture. This is analogized to HTTP: a foundational protocol that enables value but doesn’t itself command high margins.

With connection commoditized, the App Store unbundles into two remaining high‑value layers: discovery and payments. Discovery becomes critical because agents will choose among competing services without the user ever seeing the alternatives. In a human web, search ads sell “the right to be discovered.” In an agent web, the conversion rate could approach 100% because the agent doesn’t merely recommend—it completes transactions. This amplifies both the economic stakes and the regulatory risk, as ranking algorithms effectively determine market access.

Payments are the other prize. The App Store’s real margin engine was not discovery but its 30% fee on in‑app transactions, enabled by Apple’s ecosystem lock‑in. The author suggests that level of control is unlikely in the agent era: open protocols, portable data, and competitive models reduce the chance that a single platform can impose Apple‑like tolls. As a result, payments may become a competitive, lower‑margin layer, even though the transaction volume could be massive.

The piece sketches possible futures: AI platforms could sell priority placement in rankings (high margin but high trust risk), user preference data could drive decisions (best for users but hard to monetize), or neutral ranking utilities could emerge that feed structured data into many agents. In practice, the outcome will likely be a mix depending on industry and regulatory pressure.

The conclusion is that the App Store was a solution to a human‑centric distribution problem, while the agent era demands new structures. Open connection protocols are already eroding centralized app marketplaces. The next battles will be over who controls discovery algorithms and who becomes the trusted payment aggregator for autonomous agents—roles that will define power and profit in the agent economy.
