# Is the FDE role becoming less desirable?
**Source**: https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/is-the-fde-role-becoming-less-desirable
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Gergely Orosz
**Keywords**: forward-deployed engineers, hiring, software engineering roles, sales engineering, product development

## Elevator pitch
Demand for forward-deployed engineers is rising, but many engineers avoid the role because day‑to‑day work looks more like customer delivery and sales enablement than core product engineering.

## Takeaways
- FDE hiring demand has surged, yet candidate interest remains low.
- The role often tilts toward customer engagement and deployment rather than platform building.
- Mismatched expectations during interviews drive early attrition.
- Prestige and career narratives for product engineering still outweigh field delivery roles.
- Companies must clarify scope and growth paths to attract the right talent.

## Synthesis
Forward-deployed engineer (FDE) roles are becoming increasingly common, but the appetite among engineers is not keeping pace. The article highlights a widening gap: job postings and corporate mentions of FDEs have surged, yet only a small fraction of engineers actively want the job. That imbalance suggests the market is sending a signal that the role is less desirable than the hiring demand implies.

A core reason is how the role is experienced in practice. While some companies position FDEs as highly technical, product‑adjacent builders, the reality often resembles sales engineering, solutions consulting, or implementation work. FDEs may spend large portions of their time on customer deployments, integration projects, and relationship management rather than writing platform code. For engineers who want to build foundational systems and ship product features, that mix can feel like a departure from what they consider “real” engineering.

The article draws on conversations with engineers who took FDE roles expecting deep technical work and discovered a different reality. In their accounts, the job leaned heavily on engagement leadership and delivery services, with minimal product contribution. This mismatch leads some candidates to walk away during interviews when they detect the job is more client‑facing than advertised. Others accept and then quit quickly once they realize the work is closer to consulting than software development.

The broader takeaway is not that FDE roles are inherently bad, but that they are a distinct career path with different incentives and outcomes. Companies can benefit from FDEs because they help close complex deals, reduce deployment friction, and accelerate adoption in enterprise settings. Yet the roles are often perceived as less prestigious than product engineering or platform roles, and the expectations of “engineering” are misaligned with the day‑to‑day reality.

To improve hiring outcomes, organizations need to be explicit about what success looks like: the ratio of coding to customer work, how much platform contribution is expected, and what growth paths exist. If FDEs are expected to be closer to revenue teams, that should be part of the narrative rather than a surprise. Conversely, if a company wants FDEs to feed product insights back into the platform, it should create the mechanisms and time for that to happen.

Ultimately, the FDE role sits at the intersection of technical delivery and customer enablement. As demand grows, the gap between job perception and reality will continue to determine whether the role is seen as a stepping stone, a dead end, or a respected specialization. Clearer scope and honest positioning can reduce churn and attract engineers who want to thrive in that hybrid space.
