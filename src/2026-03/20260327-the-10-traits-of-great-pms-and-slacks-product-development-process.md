# The 10 traits of great PMs, how AI will impact your product, and Slack’s product development process
**Source**: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-10-traits-of-great-pms-how-ai
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Lenny Rachitsky (interview with Noah Weiss)
**Keywords**: product management, Slack, AI strategy, product culture, PM skills

## Elevator pitch
An interview with Slack’s CPO Noah Weiss covers what distinguishes great product managers, how Slack builds product culture, and how the company is approaching AI adoption within product teams.

## Takeaways
- Strong PMs blend customer empathy with strategic judgment rather than simply mirroring user demands.
- Slack’s product culture emphasizes clear principles, prioritization discipline, and rituals that reinforce quality.
- “Complaint storms” are used to surface user pain and build empathy across teams.
- AI/ML is treated as a pragmatic tool, embedded where it meaningfully improves user outcomes.
- Senior PM growth requires better communication, data fluency, and judgment under ambiguity.

## Synthesis
This Lenny’s Podcast episode with Noah Weiss, Slack’s Chief Product Officer, is framed around three themes: the traits of great product managers, Slack’s product development approach, and how AI is influencing product strategy. The discussion is practical and grounded in Weiss’s experience at Slack, Foursquare, and Google, emphasizing how high-performing PMs consistently create clarity, cultivate strong team culture, and make sound tradeoffs under uncertainty.

The interview highlights a classic tension in product management: being “customer-aware but not customer-obsessed.” Weiss suggests that great PMs listen deeply to customers but filter requests through product vision and strategy. The most effective PMs are not merely conduits for feedback—they are synthesizers who balance user insights, company goals, and product principles. Traits of strong PMs include judgment, communication, prioritization rigor, and an ability to lead without formal authority. As PMs progress, they need greater data fluency, sharper narrative skills, and comfort with ambiguity.

Slack’s product culture is portrayed as principle-driven and ritualized. The team relies on well-defined processes for prioritization and uses rituals to reinforce product quality and alignment. One specific practice—“complaint storms”—is used to immerse teams in user complaints and build empathy. This is a structured way to ensure product decisions remain grounded in real user pain points, while still allowing the team to prioritize strategically rather than reactively.

On AI, Weiss positions Slack’s approach as pragmatic rather than hyped. AI and ML are considered tools to embed into workflows where they produce tangible user value. The discussion suggests that AI integration must be tied to clear customer outcomes and product goals, not novelty. This reflects a broader product management principle: new technology should be evaluated by how it helps users achieve their goals, not by its technical sophistication alone.

The episode also touches on Slack’s growth and evolution, including how the product organization rebuilt momentum in self-serve and how it uses pilot programs to test new ideas. These examples reinforce the importance of learning cycles, iterative experimentation, and institutional feedback loops. The implied lesson for product leaders is to create systems that both encourage bold bets and rapidly surface whether those bets are paying off.

Overall, the interview provides a compact playbook for PM development: develop sound judgment, build product intuition through customer empathy, and cultivate the communication skills that translate strategy into action. It also illustrates how strong product cultures—like Slack’s—are built through explicit principles, empathetic practices, and disciplined prioritization. For teams adopting AI, the takeaway is to integrate it where it delivers measurable customer value rather than treating it as a general-purpose mandate.
