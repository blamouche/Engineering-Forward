# AI Models Are the New Rebar
**Source**: https://philippdubach.com/posts/ai-models-are-the-new-rebar/
**Date**: 2026-03-11
**Author**: Philipp D. Dubach
**Keywords**: AI model commoditization, open source AI, inference costs, OpenAI valuation, mini-mills, disruption, Qwen, LiteLLM, OpenRouter, market structure

## Elevator pitch
Open-source AI models now match proprietary performance at 97% lower cost—mirroring the mini-mill disruption of the steel industry—creating margin compression that makes OpenAI's $840B valuation and path to profitability structurally untenable.

## Takeaways
- Qwen 3.5-35B delivers comparable performance to Claude Sonnet 4.5 at $0.10/M tokens vs. $3.00—a 30x cost difference for equivalent capability.
- The open-source/proprietary performance gap narrowed from 8% to 1.7% annually (Stanford HAI 2025 AI Index).
- AI inference costs declining 50x annually for equivalent performance (Epoch AI)—GPT-4-level capabilities went from $30/M tokens (early 2023) to under $0.10 via open-source.
- Switching costs approach zero: LiteLLM unifies 100+ providers; OpenRouter manages 400+ models; 92% of Fortune 500 employees use multi-model platforms averaging 30 different models.
- OpenAI's financial pressure: $840B valuation requires $200-280B revenue by 2030; gross margins fell 40%→33% in 2025; inference costs quadrupled to $8.4B; $13.5B lost in H1 2025.
- Value concentrates at: infrastructure providers (NVIDIA, cloud platforms), application-layer companies embedding AI in domain workflows, and mega-platform distributors—not at the model API layer.

## Synthesis
The rebar analogy is precise and instructive. Mini-mills disrupted integrated steel producers by starting at the low end of the value chain (rebar, the lowest-margin product) and progressively moving upmarket—eventually threatening the entire integrated steel business model. Open-source AI models started with small, specialist tasks where the performance gap to frontier models didn't matter much, and have now closed the performance gap to the point where they're competitive for most enterprise use cases.

The "correct moves for current customers" observation—borrowed from Christensen's Innovator's Dilemma—is the central strategic insight. OpenAI's decisions to invest in frontier capability, build enterprise features, and expand into adjacent products make sense given their current customer base and commitments. They're simultaneously wrong strategically given the competitive dynamics of the open-source model market. U.S. Steel made similarly rational decisions throughout the mini-mill era; it didn't prevent the disruption.

The financial mathematics are stark. A valuation of $840B requires revenues of $200-280B by 2030 at reasonable multiples. Current revenues are a fraction of that figure. Achieving it requires either capturing a dominant share of enterprise AI spend at premium pricing—which becomes harder as open-source alternatives close the performance gap—or finding adjacent markets where the OpenAI brand and capabilities create durable advantages. Neither path is obvious.

The value concentration observation suggests where to look for durable businesses. Infrastructure (NVIDIA) wins because switching compute providers is genuinely hard. Application-layer companies that embed AI into specific workflows with deep integrations create switching costs that model-agnosticism enables. Mega-platform distributors leverage existing user relationships and data. The model API layer, by contrast, is architecturally designed to be interchangeable—which is exactly what LiteLLM and OpenRouter make literal.
