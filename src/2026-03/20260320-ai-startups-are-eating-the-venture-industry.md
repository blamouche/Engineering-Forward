# AI startups are eating the venture industry and the returns, so far, are good
**Source**: https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/20/ai-startups-are-eating-the-venture-industry-and-the-returns-so-far-are-good/
**Date**: March 20, 2026
**Author**: Dominic-Madori Davis
**Keywords**: venture capital, AI startups, funding, IRR, valuations

## Elevator pitch
Carta data shows AI startups captured a record 41% of venture dollars in 2025, driving early high IRRs for recent funds while concentrating capital into a small set of mega-rounds.

## Takeaways
- AI startups accounted for 41% of $128B in Carta-tracked venture dollars in 2025.
- Funding is increasingly concentrated in a few massive rounds.
- Mega-rounds for OpenAI, Anthropic, and xAI skew the market.
- Recent-vintage funds show higher early IRRs, though partly due to markups.
- Returns may hinge on real exits versus hype-driven valuations.

## Synthesis
TechCrunch reports that AI startups dominated 2025 venture funding, taking 41% of the $128B in Carta-tracked dollars—a record share. The capital is not evenly distributed: a small fraction of companies accounted for half of all funding, with the biggest beneficiaries being OpenAI, Anthropic, and xAI. The article frames this as a K‑shaped market: a handful of large firms raise huge rounds while the rest of the ecosystem competes for a smaller pool of capital.

The funding surge is accompanied by eye‑watering valuations. The piece notes multi‑billion dollar rounds across leading model labs, including a $20B Series E for xAI and a $110B round for OpenAI. Anthropic’s $30B round at a $380B valuation sits between them. These rounds have helped push overall venture deployment higher but also concentrate risk in a few bets.

Carta data also suggests that younger funds—those raised after the ChatGPT launch—are showing stronger early internal rates of return (IRR) than funds raised between 2017 and 2020. The article cautions that these numbers are “on paper” gains: when a portfolio company raises a new round at a higher valuation, fund IRR jumps even before any real liquidity event. This implies that reported IRR strength may reflect markups rather than realized exits.

The article highlights the cost structure of AI startups as one driver of large rounds. Unlike traditional startups, many AI companies need significant capital to fund compute and model training, even without large headcount. The result is bigger rounds with more capital per company, which amplifies concentration in a market already skewed toward a few headline firms.

The central question is whether the current enthusiasm will translate into returns through IPOs and acquisitions, or whether it reflects a bubble that will deflate when exit markets test these valuations. The piece does not take a definitive stance, but frames the outcome as contingent on the ability of large AI firms to achieve liquidity events at or above their current valuations.

Overall, the article paints a picture of an AI-driven venture market with massive capital inflows, high reported IRRs for recent funds, and extreme concentration. It suggests the near‑term narrative is strong, but long‑term returns remain uncertain until the exit market validates the valuations that drive today’s paper gains.
