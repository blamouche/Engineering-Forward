# The design process is dead. Here's what's replacing it.
**Source**: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-design-process-is-dead
**Date**: March 1, 2026
**Author**: Lenny Rachitsky
**Keywords**: design, product development, prototyping, collaboration, AI tools

## Elevator pitch
Traditional design handoffs are being replaced by continuous, cross-functional prototyping loops where designers and engineers co-own product shaping, with AI tools compressing iteration cycles and forcing new collaboration patterns.

## Takeaways
- Linear design phases are giving way to rapid, shared prototyping that merges design and engineering work.
- Designers are expected to be fluent in technical constraints and to think in systems, not just screens.
- AI tooling makes exploration cheap, shifting the hardest work to framing problems and making trade-offs.
- Shared artifacts, not handoffs, are becoming the primary coordination mechanism across teams.
- Organizations need to update roles, metrics, and decision rights to match this new workflow.

## Synthesis
The article argues that the classic design process -- discovery, wireframes, visual design, handoff, build -- is no longer a reliable description of how modern teams ship products. The collapse is driven by two forces. First, software has become more fluid: prototypes can now be produced quickly and iterated in minutes rather than weeks. Second, AI-assisted tooling makes it possible for many people to generate interfaces, flows, and working code without waiting on specialized steps. The consequence is that "design" is no longer a discrete stage but a continuous, collaborative activity that runs alongside engineering, product, and research.

In that environment, the most valuable design contributions are less about pixel-perfect deliverables and more about system-level thinking. Designers are pushed to reason about constraints, architecture, and implementation trade-offs because those constraints shape what can be prototyped quickly and what will survive into production. When designers can build or prototype directly, the cycle of "idea -> feedback -> revision" tightens, but it also removes the buffer that used to protect the team from premature decisions. The article's core thesis is that AI has made creation cheap, so the harder, higher-leverage work is deciding what to create and why.

This shift also changes collaboration. The handoff model implied a clean boundary where design was "done" and engineering "started." The new model replaces handoffs with shared artifacts and joint ownership. Instead of producing a static spec, teams collaborate in living prototypes, design systems, and codebases. That pushes responsibility for product quality and user experience into every role, but also demands a shared vocabulary for problem framing, user needs, and trade-offs. Designers become facilitators of product decisions, not just providers of deliverables.

Another consequence is organizational. Roles, reporting lines, and performance metrics were historically built around a phased process. If the process is now continuous, organizations need to reset expectations. Designers should be evaluated not only on aesthetic or interaction quality, but on how effectively they drive exploration, clarify trade-offs, and accelerate learning. Engineering should be rewarded for supporting rapid experimentation, not just delivery. Product leadership needs to create space for these continuous loops so that speed doesn't collapse into thrash.

Ultimately, the article frames the "death" of the design process as a positive evolution. It is not the end of design, but the end of a specific workflow. The replacement is a fluid, cross-functional system where prototyping is the default, AI removes many low-leverage barriers, and the critical skills are framing problems, aligning teams, and making decisions amid rapid iteration. In that world, the most resilient teams are those that treat design as a shared capability rather than a gated discipline.
