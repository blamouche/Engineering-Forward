## Elevator pitch
A designer explains why most AI design tools feel wrong—and why Flora’s canvas‑style workflow finally matches how creative work actually happens.

## Takeaways
- Linear “prompt → output → restart” flows clash with real design’s iterative nature.
- Designers need side‑by‑side exploration, recombination, and rapid pivots.
- Tool switching (Midjourney → Photoshop → Figma) breaks creative flow.
- A unified canvas that blends ideation, generation, and organization fits creative thinking.
- The best AI tools feel like an extension of the designer’s mind, not a slot machine.

## Synthesis
This piece is a first‑person review from a creative lead who shares work openly and values tools that mirror real design practice. The author frames most AI design tools as engineered around a linear interaction model: type a prompt, wait for a result, then either accept it or start over. That model might be efficient for demos, but it misunderstands how designers work. Design is iterative, non‑linear, and comparative; ideas evolve through exploration, recombination, and successive refinements. A system that forces a single, serial path turns the creative process into a lottery instead of a craft.

The article argues that the emotional bar for AI design tools is not “good output,” but “alignment with how I think.” When a tool matches a designer’s mental workflow, it stops being a set of commands and starts feeling like an extension of cognition. That “finally gets you” moment is the real product differentiator, especially in a market crowded with model‑centric features. The author uses this lens to describe Flora, a generative canvas that combines ideation, image generation, and organization in one place.

Two problems stand out in the critique of mainstream workflows. First, the typical prompt‑to‑image loop encourages brute‑force iteration with little structure; it is hard to see alternatives side by side or carry partial progress forward. Second, the constant tool‑hopping—export from Midjourney, import into Photoshop, check in Figma, hunt for references—shatters flow and creates cognitive overhead. The writer treats flow as the scarce resource in creative work; every context switch taxes attention and slows down insight.

Flora is presented as the closest approximation of how designers actually behave: a shared canvas where multiple variants can be generated, compared, and reorganized in real time. That environment supports non‑linear exploration and preserves context, which makes it easier to converge on a final direction without losing the history of how you got there. The implication is that AI in design should be less about single outputs and more about tooling for the whole decision‑making arc.

The broader thesis is that “AI for creatives” succeeds when it amplifies taste and judgment rather than replacing them. A system that respects craft will focus on workflow structure, transparency, and the ability to iterate with intention. In this framing, the real innovation is not just better models; it is the human‑aligned interface that lets designers explore and shape ideas at the speed of thought. The article positions Flora as a step toward that ideal and a template for what creative AI should prioritize.
