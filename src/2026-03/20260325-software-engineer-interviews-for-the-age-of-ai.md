# Software engineer interviews for the age of AI
**Source**: https://swizec.com/blog/software-engineer-interviews-for-the-age-of-ai/
**Date**: March 25, 2026
**Author**: Swizec Teller
**Keywords**: hiring, interviews, AI tools, system design, engineering

## Elevator pitch
Swizec argues that AI should be encouraged in coding interviews, and proposes a higher‑signal process focused on ownership, system thinking, and real-world problem solving.

## Takeaways
- Great engineers are defined by accountability for production systems, not just code output.
- Interviews are probabilistic; reduce noise with repeatable criteria and multiple evaluators.
- “Fractal knowledge” questions reveal depth by probing past project details.
- System design interviews show how candidates clarify requirements and spot operational risks.
- A practical coding task with full tooling (including AI) better mirrors real work than LeetCode.

## Synthesis
Swizec Teller outlines an interview approach built for a world where AI assistance is normal. He starts by reframing the hiring goal: companies need engineers who will own the systems they build, not just generate code. AI can produce plausible output, but someone still carries the pager and signs off on reliability. That responsibility, he argues, is what differentiates great engineers.

He acknowledges interviews are imperfect and inherently noisy, but argues they can still correlate reasonably with performance if the process is consistent. The key is to lower noise with prepared criteria and multiple interviewers, and to accept that hiring is probabilistic rather than deterministic.

For screening, he prefers “fractal knowledge” questions: ask a candidate to describe a real project, then keep digging for detail. The depth of their answers reveals what they actually worked on versus what they only skimmed. This line of questioning also surfaces how they learned from failures and how they recognized what needed to be done.

System design interviews come next, not as a memorized template exercise, but as a way to evaluate how candidates ask questions, push back on assumptions, and consider operational constraints. He argues the best engineers focus on getting the job done rather than building the most elegant bridge, and that practical clarity matters more than theoretical completeness.

For the coding exercise, he proposes a realistic, tool‑rich environment rather than LeetCode. Candidates get a stubbed codebase, documentation, a business problem, and access to AI, Google, and the interviewer. The goal is to build a working script that interacts with a database and external API, plus tests. This setup reveals how candidates navigate unfamiliar code, ask business questions, iterate toward a solution, and use AI to accelerate without shipping slop. He notes that allowing AI raises the bar; interviews can be more complex and closer to real work.

The essay ends with a caveat: the approach is still new, and AI tool differences might affect candidate performance. But the core claim stands—if AI is part of daily engineering, interviews should measure how candidates use it responsibly while still demonstrating ownership, systems thinking, and the ability to deliver under real constraints.
