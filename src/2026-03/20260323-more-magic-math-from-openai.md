# More Magic Math from OpenAI?
**Source**: https://om.co/2026/03/23/more-magic-math-from-openai/
**Date**: March 23, 2026
**Author**: Om Malik
**Keywords**: OpenAI, financing, private equity, IPO, revenue

## Elevator pitch
Om Malik argues that OpenAI’s reported offer of guaranteed returns to private equity firms signals deeper business strain, suggesting the company is borrowing against a future that is arriving slower than promised.

## Takeaways
- The 17.5% guaranteed return offer implies unusual financing pressure for a company of OpenAI’s stature.
- Industry insiders (Jensen Huang, Orlando Bravo) have questioned OpenAI’s business fundamentals.
- The market is shifting from capex-heavy hype to opex-driven revenue expectations.
- Joint venture structures may be designed to create IPO-friendly revenue narratives.
- The author sees OpenAI’s fundraising as a sign of a real cash crunch and IPO urgency.

## Synthesis
In this post, Om Malik interprets reports that OpenAI is offering private equity firms a guaranteed 17.5% return as a sign that the company’s financial position is more strained than it appears. He argues that such terms are not typical for a healthy, fast-growing technology business. Instead, they imply that OpenAI is leaning heavily on future expectations, while the near‑term economics are less compelling.

Malik ties the financing news to a broader industry shift he calls an “inference inflection.” In his view, AI is moving from a capital expenditure phase—where massive investments are made on faith—to an operating expenditure phase, where real revenues and margins matter. Companies like Anthropic are reportedly growing revenue quickly, while OpenAI appears to be grappling with the cost structure of enterprise deployments. The implication is that models and funding rounds are no longer enough; the market wants repeatable business outcomes.

He also cites comments from major industry figures. Jensen Huang has suggested OpenAI is not well run, and private equity leader Orlando Bravo reportedly walked away from a joint venture idea after questioning the company’s long‑term profit profile. Malik sees these signals as consistent: sophisticated capital is skeptical about OpenAI’s path to sustainable profitability.

The post suggests that OpenAI’s joint venture strategy may be less about product innovation and more about financial storytelling. The Reuters line that such structures could enable “clearer segment reporting that can support the IPO narrative” is interpreted as a clue. Malik reads this as OpenAI attempting to craft a line item that appears like recurring enterprise revenue, even if the economics are being subsidized by partners. In this framing, the “product” is not the AI itself but the ability to present a compelling IPO prospectus.

While acknowledging there may be information the public lacks—such as a breakthrough product—Malik remains skeptical. He argues that companies in strong positions do not need to offer unusually rich guarantees, and that OpenAI’s financing maneuvers point to an urgent need for cash and a quick path to going public. The “magic math,” in his view, is the difference between the narrative of limitless potential and the realities of operating a capital‑intensive AI business.
