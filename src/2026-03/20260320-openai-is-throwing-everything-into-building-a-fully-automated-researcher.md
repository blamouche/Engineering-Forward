# OpenAI is throwing everything into building a fully automated researcher
**Source**: https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/03/20/1134438/openai-is-throwing-everything-into-building-a-fully-automated-researcher/
**Date**: March 20, 2026
**Author**: Unknown
**Keywords**: AI agents, automated research, reasoning models, OpenAI, scientific discovery

## Elevator pitch
OpenAI is reorienting its R&D toward an “AI researcher,” starting with a near‑term autonomous research intern and aiming for a multi‑agent research system by 2028, betting that improved reasoning models and long‑running agents can tackle complex scientific and business problems while raising new reliability and safety questions.

## Takeaways
- OpenAI’s new “North Star” is a fully automated research system built from agents, reasoning models, and interpretability work.
- The roadmap targets an autonomous research intern by September and a larger multi‑agent researcher by 2028.
- Codex is framed as an early prototype for long‑running research agents, with broader domains beyond coding as the goal.
- OpenAI expects capability gains (reasoning, longer horizons, task decomposition) to unlock sustained autonomous work.
- The vision faces reliability and safety challenges, including compounding error rates and misuse risks.

## Synthesis
OpenAI is shifting its research agenda toward building an “AI researcher,” a fully automated, agent‑based system meant to tackle complex problems end‑to‑end. In an interview with MIT Technology Review, chief scientist Jakub Pachocki describes this as the company’s new “North Star.” The plan starts with an autonomous research intern—an agent that can handle a limited set of research tasks with minimal guidance by September—and scales toward a multi‑agent research system by 2028. OpenAI argues that such a system could take on problems too large or complex for humans, ranging from mathematics and physics to biology, chemistry, business, and policy, as long as tasks can be expressed in text, code, or other structured inputs.

Pachocki frames the effort as a convergence of several research threads: reasoning models, agent tooling, and interpretability. OpenAI’s recent agentic products, especially Codex, are positioned as early prototypes of the automated researcher. Codex can already carry out multi‑step computer tasks, analyze documents, generate charts, and run workflows for internal users. The company claims a large portion of its technical staff use it daily. For OpenAI, the next leap is extending these capabilities beyond coding and enabling agents to run longer with less supervision—moving from short tasks to multi‑day research efforts.

A key premise is that broader model capability translates into longer coherent task execution. Pachocki argues that each major model generation has extended the time horizons a model can manage, even without specialized training, and that reasoning‑optimized models further improve this by encouraging step‑by‑step work and backtracking. OpenAI is also training models on complex, multi‑part tasks—such as challenging puzzles from math and coding competitions—to teach them how to track large contexts, decompose problems, and manage sub‑tasks over long runs. The intent is not to optimize for benchmarks alone but to validate capabilities before applying them to real‑world research domains.

The automated researcher vision is not unique to OpenAI; other AI leaders have articulated similar goals. The interview acknowledges a broader industry narrative about AI accelerating science. Yet the article underscores real skepticism about whether the technology can deliver the reliability required for long‑running research. Researchers like Doug Downey at the Allen Institute for AI note that chaining tasks together compounds error rates, and even top‑tier models still make many mistakes on scientific tasks. OpenAI’s recent performance gains in math and science are promising, but they do not fully resolve concerns about robustness when agents operate with limited oversight.

Safety and governance are central to the conversation. Pachocki notes that an AI system capable of advancing research at scale introduces new risks, from misinterpretation of instructions to system failures or malicious exploitation. OpenAI’s current mitigation strategy relies heavily on making reasoning processes more transparent—such as chain‑of‑thought monitoring—to help humans understand and audit what models are doing during long‑running tasks. The article frames this as an unresolved area: the same autonomy that makes the system useful also increases the stakes when the system is wrong.

Overall, the piece paints a clear strategic pivot. OpenAI is placing its bets on agentic systems that can plan, execute, and iterate over extended time horizons, with Codex as a stepping stone. The technical bet is that better reasoning and training for long‑horizon tasks will make autonomous research viable; the organizational bet is that OpenAI’s focus and scale can outpace competitors. The open questions are whether the reliability curve can keep up with ambition, and how to keep humans in the loop as systems begin to perform research work that traditionally required teams of experts.
