# What 81,000 People Want from AI
**Source**: https://www.anthropic.com/features/81k-interviews
**Date**: 2026-03-18
**Author**: Saffron Huang et al.
**Keywords**: user research, AI aspirations, 81k interviews, multilingual study, productivity, displacement fears, economic security, global AI adoption

## Elevator pitch
Anthropic's study of 80,508 Claude users across 159 countries is the largest multilingual qualitative AI study ever—revealing that professional excellence (18.8%) and personal transformation (13.7%) are users' top aspirations, while hallucinations (26.7%) and economic displacement (22.3%) are their primary concerns.

## Takeaways
- Top aspirations: Professional Excellence (18.8%), Personal Transformation (13.7%), Life Management (13.5%), Time Freedom (11.1%), Financial Independence (9.7%).
- Top concerns: Unreliability/hallucinations (26.7%), Economic displacement (22.3%), Loss of autonomy (21.9%), Cognitive decline from over-reliance (16.3%).
- "Light and shade" tensions: the same AI capabilities produce both benefits and harms simultaneously—people appreciate AI's availability while fearing dependency.
- Benefits are grounded in experience (32% report actual productivity gains); systemic harms remain largely speculative.
- Regional variation: lower/middle-income countries view AI more optimistically than wealthy nations, particularly for entrepreneurship and learning.

## Synthesis
The study's scale—80,508 people, 70 languages, 159 countries—is unprecedented for AI user research. Most published AI user research involves hundreds or thousands of participants in English-speaking countries. The multilingual, global scope shifts the finding from "what affluent English-speaking professionals want" to something closer to a genuine global picture of AI aspirations and concerns.

The professional excellence ranking (18.8%) reflects the dominant use case: people want AI to handle routine work so they can focus on meaningful tasks. This is a vision of AI as leverage, not replacement—consistent with the productivity gains reported in actual use. The concern about economic displacement (22.3%) exists in tension with this aspiration: the same handle-my-routine-work use case that individuals value creates displacement risk at the aggregate.

The speculative vs. experienced benefit asymmetry is important for understanding current AI discourse. Benefits are reported from experience: 32% describe actual productivity gains. Fears are largely speculative: most users reporting displacement concerns haven't yet experienced displacement themselves. This asymmetry means negative AI coverage (which tends to focus on speculative harms) and positive coverage (which reports on experienced benefits) may both be accurate but are describing different things.

The regional optimism gradient is significant context for AI policy debates that often assume a Western perspective. Lower/middle-income countries see AI as enabling entrepreneurship and learning in ways that don't require the expensive intermediaries (lawyers, accountants, specialists) that constrain economic participation. For users in these regions, AI's potential to democratize expertise access is the salient promise; displacement of existing knowledge workers is less relevant when those workers were previously inaccessible anyway.
