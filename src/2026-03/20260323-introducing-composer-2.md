# Introducing Composer 2
**Source**: https://cursor.com/blog/composer-2
**Date**: March 23, 2026
**Author**: Unknown
**Keywords**: Cursor, Composer 2, coding model, benchmarks, pricing

## Elevator pitch
Cursor introduces Composer 2, a new coding model positioned as frontier‑level intelligence with aggressive pricing and improved benchmark scores.

## Takeaways
- Composer 2 claims large benchmark gains across CursorBench, Terminal‑Bench 2.0, and SWE‑bench Multilingual.
- The model is priced at $0.50/M input and $2.50/M output tokens, undercutting many peers.
- Improvements are attributed to continued pretraining and reinforcement learning on long‑horizon tasks.
- A faster variant is offered at higher prices but remains cheaper than many fast competitors.
- Cursor frames Composer 2 as its new default “fast” option with strong capability‑to‑cost ratio.

## Synthesis
Cursor’s announcement of Composer 2 positions it as a significant leap in coding‑model performance and cost efficiency. The post highlights gains across multiple benchmarks—including CursorBench, Terminal‑Bench 2.0, and SWE‑bench Multilingual—suggesting a broad improvement in coding and agentic task performance. These benchmarks are increasingly important in the market because they reflect not just single‑shot code generation, but multi‑step tool use and long‑horizon problem solving.

The company attributes the gains to two main factors: a continued pretraining run that strengthens the base model and a reinforcement‑learning phase focused on long‑horizon coding tasks. This implies a strategy similar to other frontier labs: build a stronger base model, then specialize it via RL to handle complex, multi‑action workflows. The emphasis on long‑horizon tasks suggests that Composer 2 is designed for agentic coding scenarios where the model must plan, iterate, and execute over extended sessions rather than produce a single code snippet.

Pricing is a core part of the positioning. Composer 2 is introduced at $0.50 per million input tokens and $2.50 per million output tokens, which is materially lower than most frontier models. Cursor also offers a faster variant priced higher but still positioned as cost‑effective relative to competing “fast” models. By making the fast variant the default, Cursor signals that it values responsiveness and affordability as much as raw capability.

The post also highlights the company’s evaluation infrastructure, including CursorBench and external benchmarks. It references Terminal‑Bench 2.0 and the Harbor evaluation framework, reinforcing that the model’s claims are tied to measurable outcomes rather than anecdotal performance. This is a subtle but important market signal: the company is leaning on standardized agentic benchmarks to establish credibility in a crowded model landscape.

Overall, Composer 2 is framed as a practical frontier‑level model optimized for day‑to‑day coding workflows. The combination of benchmark gains, long‑horizon training focus, and aggressive pricing suggests Cursor’s strategy is to win on accessibility: offer near‑frontier capability at a price point that makes the model viable for continuous use. The announcement reads less like a research milestone and more like a product move to make high‑end coding intelligence economically sustainable.
