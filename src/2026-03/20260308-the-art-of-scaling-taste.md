# The Art of Scaling Taste
**Source**: https://every.to/napkin-math/the-art-of-scaling-taste
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Unknown
**Keywords**: taste, branding, product strategy, MSCHF, creative organizations

## Elevator pitch
A case study of MSCHF argues that in a world of abundant production, the real competitive advantage is the ability to scale taste across an organization.

## Takeaways
- AI and automation commoditize production, making taste the differentiator.
- Taste is both an intuition and a developable organizational capability.
- MSCHF scales taste through fast, diverse drops that still feel coherent.
- Distribution and brand theatrics can be as strategic as the product itself.
- Scaling taste requires repeatable cultural systems, not just individual genius.

## Synthesis
The article tackles a core strategic question for the AI era: if creation becomes cheap and abundant, what helps a company stand out? The answer offered is “taste,” defined as a deep, often hard‑to‑explain sense of what is good and culturally resonant. The author argues that as automation lowers the cost of making things, the scarcest resource becomes judgment—specifically, the ability to pick the right ideas, execute them in a distinctive way, and build a brand that feels coherent even when the outputs are diverse.

Taste, in this framing, is not just personal preference; it is a skill that can be cultivated and, crucially, scaled. That scaling challenge is the focus of the MSCHF case study. MSCHF is described as an artist collective that releases a new product every two weeks, spanning wildly different categories—perfume, novelty tech, fashion collaborations—yet still feels unmistakably like MSCHF. The consistency comes from a shared sensibility: playful subversion of capitalism, cultural commentary, and a willingness to turn product launches into theatrical events.

The article highlights how MSCHF’s distribution choices are part of their taste system. Instead of relying on conventional retail or e‑commerce playbooks, they experiment with quirky channels, sometimes even getting banned from platforms. These choices reinforce the brand’s rebellious identity and generate outsized attention. The point is that “taste” extends beyond product aesthetics into the entire experience: how the product is launched, who participates, and the story told around it.

Financially, the story suggests that scaling taste can be commercially viable. MSCHF has built meaningful revenue and investor backing while remaining small in headcount. Their team is composed largely of generalists, which implies that taste is embedded culturally rather than tied to a single discipline. The organization can move fast because it is aligned around a shared vision and does not need heavy specialization to execute.

The author frames this as a broader lesson for startups in an era of abundance. Making things will be easier; choosing what to make, and doing so with a coherent voice, will be harder. Scaling taste means building systems—rituals, hiring patterns, creative reviews, and brand principles—that allow many products to reflect a single, recognizable identity. MSCHF succeeds not because every product is “perfect,” but because the collection of products creates a consistent cultural imprint.

Ultimately, the piece argues that taste is a strategic asset. It shapes product selection, distribution choices, and brand perception. In a market where AI and automation level the playing field on execution, the organizations that can scale taste across teams and outputs will stand out. The MSCHF example serves as a proof of concept: unconventional, fast‑moving, but unified by a clear aesthetic and narrative spine.
