# How Webflow’s CPO built an AI chief of staff to manage her calendar, prep for meetings, and drive AI adoption
**Source**: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-webflows-cpo-built-an-ai-chief
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Rachel Wolan (interview on Lenny’s Podcast)
**Keywords**: AI assistants, executive workflows, productivity, AI adoption, Webflow

## Elevator pitch
Webflow’s CPO Rachel Wolan describes how she built a personal AI chief-of-staff to streamline calendar and meeting prep, then used the experience to drive broader AI adoption across her organization.

## Takeaways
- Building a personal AI tool gave an executive concrete insight into AI’s practical capabilities.
- The AI chief-of-staff integrates calendar, email, and context files to support daily planning.
- “Brutally honest” feedback loops can help leaders see how they actually spend time.
- Company-wide “builder days” increased AI tool adoption by making experimentation social and safe.
- Executives who model AI usage make adoption more credible across teams.

## Synthesis
In this episode, Webflow CPO Rachel Wolan explains how she built a custom AI chief-of-staff to manage the realities of an executive workload—calendar coordination, meeting preparation, email triage, and context gathering. Rather than evaluating AI from a distance, she used hands-on development to test how AI could fit into her daily workflow. The result is a personalized system that preps her for meetings with just-in-time information, helps prioritize her schedule, and even provides candid feedback about where her time goes.

Her approach is notably practical. The tool integrates with calendar and email APIs and relies on structured notes (often in markdown) to create context for meetings and events. She also uses multiple LLM interfaces, suggesting that a flexible, modular architecture is useful when building personalized AI systems. The AI chief-of-staff acts less like a generic chatbot and more like a workflow orchestrator—one that pulls data, summarizes context, and helps reduce the cognitive overhead of daily coordination.

A key insight from the discussion is that building personal software can be a gateway for executives to understand AI’s actual strengths and limitations. Instead of relying on secondhand reports, Wolan’s direct experience gave her a grounded view of what AI can automate, what still needs human judgment, and what kinds of data structures make the system reliable. This experiential learning then shaped how she approached AI adoption inside Webflow.

She also describes organizational strategies for driving adoption, notably “builder days,” which are internal events that encourage teams to experiment with AI tools and share outcomes. By creating a social and time-boxed setting for experimentation, Webflow lowered the barrier to trying AI and made it easier for employees to see direct value. This kind of initiative also helps standardize best practices and surface use cases that are relevant to different teams.

Another lesson is the importance of leadership by example. Wolan argues that executives must personally use AI tools if they want credible organizational change. When leadership visibly uses AI in real workflows—rather than endorsing it from the sidelines—it signals that adoption is safe, valuable, and expected. This helps overcome resistance and accelerates the shift from curiosity to routine usage.

Overall, the episode illustrates how AI adoption becomes real when it is anchored in concrete, personal workflows. The AI chief-of-staff is less about novelty and more about reducing friction in daily decision-making. The broader message for organizations is that adoption is most effective when leaders build or use tools themselves, turn learning into shared practices, and create cultural moments—like builder days—that reward experimentation and practical outcomes.
