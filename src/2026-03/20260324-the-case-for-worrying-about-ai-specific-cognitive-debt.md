# The case for worrying about AI-specific cognitive debt
**Source**: https://www.natemeyvis.com/the-case-for-worrying-about-ai-specific-cognitive-debt/
**Date**: March 24, 2026
**Author**: Nate Meyvis
**Keywords**: cognitive debt, AI coding, software quality, organizational behavior

## Elevator pitch
Nate Meyvis outlines the strongest arguments for AI‑driven cognitive debt risk, even while remaining skeptical that those pessimistic views are ultimately correct.

## Takeaways
- He frames three broad stances on AI and cognitive debt, personally leaning toward “no major difference.”
- The post steel‑mans the pessimistic view that AI could worsen cognitive debt.
- Risks include working at the edges of competence, weak mitigation incentives, and superficial coherence in LLM outputs.
- Organizational behavior and psychology may favor debt accumulation over debt reduction.
- The author ultimately remains unconvinced but argues for taking the risks seriously.

## Synthesis
Meyvis positions his essay as a steel‑man of the “cognitive debt doomer” view. He distinguishes three camps: those who think agentic coding significantly worsens cognitive debt and that it is a major problem, those who think it worsens debt but not in a worrying way, and those who see no meaningful difference between traditional and AI‑assisted coding. While he aligns with the third camp, he argues that it is valuable to articulate the strongest case against his own view, given how new and uncertain the space is.

The first pessimistic argument he outlines is competence drift. AI may make developers more capable overall, but it also shifts work to the edges of competence, where people are barely qualified. Even if baseline ability rises, the “frontier” of what people attempt could still be at a low competence level, leading to brittle systems and higher cognitive debt.

A second cluster of arguments concerns incentives and human behavior. Even if AI provides tools that could help mitigate cognitive debt, organizations historically fail to adopt such practices widely. The path of least resistance is to accumulate debt rather than invest in its reduction. Individual psychology can compound this: the desire to build quickly and the reluctance to scrutinize one’s work tend to dominate, making sustained debt‑reduction practices unlikely to take hold.

Meyvis also points to structural limitations in LLM outputs. He argues that LLMs are strong at local, small‑scale coherence but often weak at macro‑level coherence unless constrained by rigid structures (like formal proofs). Codebases, which resemble evolving gardens or artworks more than proofs, may therefore become harder to understand when large portions are generated agentically. This could make systems less intelligible and raise long‑term cognitive debt.

Finally, he suggests that tooling alone is insufficient to address cognitive debt. Deep understanding of complex codebases requires training, mentorship, and experience—factors that AI could reduce by making it easier to ship without sustained learning. If AI lowers the barrier to producing changes without building understanding, teams could end up with fewer engineers who can truly reason about the system’s overall structure.

Despite presenting these arguments, Meyvis does not endorse them. He sees them as plausible but ultimately unconvincing; the point is to test optimism against the best counter‑case. The essay’s value is in framing the debate clearly and highlighting the real‑world behaviors—organizational inertia, incentive misalignment, and shallow understanding—that could make AI‑era cognitive debt a genuine risk even if the tools themselves improve.
