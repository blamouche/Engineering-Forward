# AI Style Guides: How to Help AI Write Like You
**Source**: https://every.to/guides/ai-style-guide
**Date**: 2026-03-17
**Author**: Katie Parrott and Claude
**Keywords**: AI writing, style guide, Claude, voice, tone, prompt engineering, content creation, Every.to

## Elevator pitch
Every.to's comprehensive guide to creating AI style guides argues that specificity about voice, structure, and anti-patterns—not generic tone descriptions—is what transforms AI output from generic to authentically personal.

## Takeaways
- Without a style guide, AI converges toward "average approximation of good writing"—competent but impersonal
- An AI style guide is an "operating manual" that makes instinctive writing knowledge legible to machines
- Effective guides include voice/tone descriptions, structural templates, sentence-level preferences, signature moves, anti-patterns with examples, and revision checklists
- Three implementation levels: Starter (20 minutes), Working (daily-use Claude Project), Compound (automated workflow integration)
- Build the guide by having AI interview you about reaction to specific writing samples rather than self-describing abstractly

## Synthesis
The tension at the center of "AI Style Guides" is that AI models have been trained to write well in a generic sense—grammatically correct, logically structured, appropriately toned—but "well" in this sense means matching the average of good writing rather than any individual's distinctive voice. For users who want AI to write in their style, generic capability is not a solution; it is the problem.

Katie Parrott and Claude's guide addresses this by framing AI style guides as the inverse of traditional style guides. Traditional style guides attempt to standardize diverse human writers toward a consistent organizational voice. AI style guides push a perfectly consistent model toward individual idiosyncrasies—the specific word choices, sentence rhythms, structural preferences, and tonal quirks that make writing recognizably someone's own.

The practical guidance is unusually specific. Effective guides don't describe voice abstractly ("conversational but authoritative")—they document structural patterns (the writer starts sections with specific question forms), sentence-level preferences (short declarative sentences after long complex ones), signature moves (specific rhetorical devices used repeatedly), and anti-patterns with concrete negative examples (phrases the writer never uses). The more granular the guidance, the more reliably the AI can apply it.

The three implementation levels create an accessible entry point. The Starter level—20 minutes of structured prompting—can produce a usable guide immediately. The Working level involves developing the guide into a Claude Project that's consulted daily, allowing iterative refinement based on observed outputs. The Compound level integrates the style guide into automated workflows, enabling consistent brand voice at scale without manual review of every output.

The building process insight is particularly valuable: rather than asking someone to describe their writing style abstractly (most people struggle with this), the guide recommends having AI interview the writer using reaction-based questions about specific writing samples. "What about this sentence doesn't sound like you?" generates more actionable guidance than "describe your tone." This method extracts the tacit knowledge embedded in taste without requiring the writer to articulate it analytically.
