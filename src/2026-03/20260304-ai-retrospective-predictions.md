# AI Retrospective, Predictions
**Source**: https://kellysutton.com/2026/03/04/ai-retrospective-predictions.html
**Date**: March 4, 2026
**Author**: Kelly Sutton
**Keywords**: AI interfaces, agents, SaaS evolution, latency, software engineering

## Elevator pitch
A founder’s candid set of observations predicts that AI will fade into the background of software, accelerate white‑collar work, and shift competition toward workflow, latency, and systems of record rather than chat interfaces.

## Takeaways
- Chat UIs are a temporary “hello world” for LLMs, not the final interface.
- AI becomes embedded as an implementation detail inside products and workflows.
- Agentic and asynchronous work patterns will feel more like coworker delegation.
- SaaS isn’t dead, but products that don’t adapt to AI workflows will be.
- Latency and infrastructure will become a new performance battleground for AI tools.

## Synthesis
This post is a collection of pragmatic observations on AI’s trajectory in software and knowledge work, framed as a personal retrospective rather than a formal thesis. The author argues that the industry is still in the “chat interface” phase, which he compares to early web gimmicks: novel, influential, but not necessarily the final interface. As models improve and context windows grow, AI becomes less visible and more integrated into applications. The prediction is that users will care about problem‑solving outcomes, not the presence of a chatbot; AI will fade into the product’s plumbing as an implementation detail.

A second theme is acceleration. The author describes software engineering as already shifting from manual code entry to orchestration and review. In this view, coding agents are moving from autocomplete toward “author” status: engineers spend more time shaping, reviewing, and refining outputs. This is presented as a productivity lift, but also as a source of ecosystem chaos. The post anticipates a market shakeout where a few model providers and systems of record dominate, while many middle‑layer tools struggle to survive as platform capabilities subsume them.

The piece distinguishes between synchronous and asynchronous AI work. The immediate interaction pattern—asking a model to respond in the same session—does not capture the full potential of agents. The author expects a rise in asynchronous delegation: send an AI off to do a task, receive a completed work product later. This shift is framed as the point where AI starts to feel like a coworker rather than a chat partner. He is skeptical of agents coordinating with each other, but still believes orchestration will become a core design space.

Latency emerges as another predicted battleground. Today, AI responses can take minutes for complex tasks; the author expects competition to push those times down to milliseconds. That evolution could shift infrastructure priorities, from GPU‑heavy backends to new hardware stacks optimized for low‑latency inference. He frames the “latency endgame” as a meaningful differentiator for AI products in the same way performance and responsiveness differentiate traditional software.

The post addresses the recurring “death of SaaS” narrative. While acknowledging that AI lowers the cost of building bespoke tools, the author argues that enterprises will not reliably maintain custom software or migrate internal systems just because AI can generate code. In his view, SaaS is not dead; it is SaaS that fails to adapt to AI workflows and integrations that will lose relevance. He highlights the opportunity for new entrants who can plug into legacy ecosystems with AI‑driven interoperability.

Finally, the author comments on white‑collar labor impacts, suggesting that AI will reduce demand for certain routine services while creating new categories of work. He ends with a historical analogy: spreadsheets eliminated some bookkeeping but created new roles in modeling. The implied lesson is that AI will drive a similar cycle of creative destruction—painful in the short term, generative in the long term.

Overall, the post reads as a grounded forecast from someone building in the space. It resists hype while acknowledging that AI is reshaping interfaces, workflows, and competitive dynamics. The core thesis is pragmatic: AI’s real impact will be in the invisible layers of software and in the speed at which work gets done, not in the novelty of a chat box.
