# Broad Timelines
**Source**: https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/6pDMLYr7my2QMTz3s/broad-timelines
**Date**: March 19, 2026
**Author**: Toby Ord
**Keywords**: AI timelines, uncertainty, forecasting, transformative AI

## Elevator pitch
Toby Ord argues that the right stance on AI timelines is not “short” or “long” but a broad distribution reflecting deep expert disagreement.

## Takeaways
- AI timeline estimates are highly uncertain and definitions are fuzzy.
- Expert disagreement implies we should hold a wide probability distribution.
- “Broad timelines” is proposed as the correct epistemic stance.
- Planning should account for both short and long possibilities simultaneously.
- The essay reframes timeline debates from winner‑picking to uncertainty management.

## Synthesis
In “Broad Timelines,” Toby Ord argues that the standard framing of AI timelines—short vs. long—is misguided. Instead of choosing sides, he proposes a “broad timelines” view: a wide probability distribution that reflects persistent expert disagreement and the inherent vagueness of concepts like AGI or transformative AI. This stance treats uncertainty itself as a key fact, rather than something to be resolved by picking a preferred camp.

Ord highlights that terms like AGI and superintelligence are not crisp thresholds, using the analogy of hikers entering and leaving a cloud: the boundary is fuzzy, but the concept still matters for planning. The key question is when AI will have transformative effects on society, and the uncertainty around that question is large. In his view, disagreements among well‑informed experts are too significant to justify narrow confidence intervals.

The essay argues that expert forecasts already embody broad distributions. Even short‑timeline proponents often include long tails, and long‑timeline proponents acknowledge earlier possibilities. Rather than treating these distributions as noise, Ord suggests we should explicitly embrace them. A broad distribution implies that actions should be robust across multiple possible futures: efforts that only pay off in one narrow window are riskier than strategies that are valuable across a wide range of timelines.

Ord also critiques the social dynamics of timeline debates. When people affiliate with one camp, they are incentivized to interpret evidence as wins for their side. This encourages polarization rather than nuanced uncertainty management. By focusing on broad distributions, the debate shifts from “who is right” to “how to plan under uncertainty.”

The practical implication is that planning should diversify across timelines. That could mean funding both near‑term safety work and longer‑term institutional readiness, or choosing career paths that remain useful across many possible AI arrival dates. Ord positions this as the most rational response to deep uncertainty.

Overall, the essay is a call for epistemic humility. It doesn’t deny that timelines might be short or long, but it argues that the evidence does not justify narrow bets. Instead, it promotes a planning mindset that treats uncertainty as a first‑class feature of the AI transition.
