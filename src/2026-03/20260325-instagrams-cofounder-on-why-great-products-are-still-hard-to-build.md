# Instagram’s Cofounder on Why Great Products Are Still Hard to Build
**Source**: https://every.to/context-window/instagram-s-cofounder-on-why-great-products-are-still-hard-to-build
**Date**: March 25, 2026
**Author**: Laura Entis
**Keywords**: product development, AI agents, Anthropic, vibe coding, technical skills

## Elevator pitch
A conversation with Instagram cofounder Mike Krieger argues that agent‑native products demand tighter tradeoffs and faster iteration, while Every’s team debates how persistence and technical depth shape reliable AI-built software.

## Takeaways
- Krieger says agent‑native products must balance freedom and reliability, since users push tools beyond original intent.
- AI shrinks build cycles from months to hours, making it easier to ship incoherent products without enough feedback.
- Anthropic Labs runs tiny product teams to move fast and avoid coordination drag.
- Every argues persistence and clear questioning can substitute for some technical skills in early prototypes.
- Reliable production systems still require technical depth to prevent failures when vibe‑coded prototypes hit real users.

## Synthesis
Laura Entis frames a podcast conversation with Instagram cofounder Mike Krieger around a central tension: AI dramatically accelerates software development, but the hard part of building great products—deciding what to cut and maintaining coherence—remains. Krieger, now co‑leading Anthropic Labs, argues that agent‑native products must be designed for flexibility while retaining reliability, because users inevitably push systems beyond what their creators anticipate. The freedom of AI tools comes with tradeoffs that teams must consciously manage.

Krieger contrasts the long feedback loops of Instagram’s early years with today’s AI‑driven cycles. At Instagram, it took months to discover dead ends; now, teams can build and discard in hours. That speed is double‑edged: the models can bolt on features quickly, but the resulting product can lack focus if teams don’t impose discipline. Krieger suggests that in this environment, many products will need to be rebuilt from scratch every few months as AI capabilities shift. To move fast enough, Anthropic Labs keeps experiments lean—often a two‑person unit of a product lead and engineer rather than large teams.

The article expands the discussion to the practical skills needed in the era of “vibe coding.” Within Every, staff debate whether technical ability still matters when tools can translate plain English into software. Some argue that persistence is the core skill: repeatedly asking for clarification, pushing the model to explain itself, and iterating until the prototype works. The piece points to Austin Tedesco’s growth‑operations agent as an example of a non‑technical builder who achieved useful outcomes through persistence and careful instruction.

Yet the article emphasizes that reliable production systems still demand technical depth. Every’s Monologue GM Naveen Naidu argues that the difference between a demo and a durable product is architecture: you must identify what cannot fail and design the system around that requirement. Vibe‑coded apps can skip foundational structure, which makes failures hard to debug and reliability difficult to guarantee. The overall message is that AI lowers the barrier to entry for building, but it does not eliminate the need for product judgment or robust engineering when systems are deployed at scale.
