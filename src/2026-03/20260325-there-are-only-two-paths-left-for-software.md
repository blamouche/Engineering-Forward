# There are only two paths left for software
**Source**: https://a16z.com/there-are-only-two-paths-left-for-software/
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Unknown
**Keywords**: software strategy, AI-native products, operating margins, restructuring, growth

## Elevator pitch
A16Z argues that public software companies now face a stark choice: reignite growth with genuinely AI-native products or rebuild for 40–50% operating margins, because the middle ground is no longer viable.

## Takeaways
- Public markets are repricing software, leaving companies stuck between slowing growth and insufficient profitability.
- Two viable paths remain: accelerate growth by 10+ points via new AI-native products or rebuild to 40–50% true operating margins.
- “Bolt-on” AI features are insufficient; companies need new products and organizational redesign to capture AI-driven growth.
- The margin path requires deep structural changes, not small layoffs, including flattening management and pricing changes.
- Both paths demand an AI-native operating model, with teams optimized for speed, product discovery, and token-based economics.

## Synthesis
The essay contends that the comfortable middle for software companies has effectively disappeared. Public markets are signalling that terminal value and growth expectations have changed, leaving a narrow set of viable strategies for durable value creation. According to the author, there are now only two credible paths: either reaccelerate revenue growth by 10+ percentage points through genuinely AI-native products within 12–18 months, or rebuild the company to achieve 40–50% true operating margins that fully include stock-based compensation. Anything between those poles will face persistent dilution, multiple compression, and a “no-man’s land” of weak growth and weak profitability.

The first path is presented as a growth sprint, not a cosmetic upgrade. It requires new AI-native products that materially move the growth rate, rather than adding chatbots or copilots to existing SKUs. The author emphasizes that success depends on a rapid organizational rebuild, starting with identifying a small group of high-impact internal leaders. These leaders should drive process-capture sprints across key workflows, turn scattered documentation into a living context layer, and instrument evaluation around accuracy, exceptions, latency, and cost. The organization must then make hard calls on leadership, reshaping the executive team to support a fast, AI-native product strategy. The operating model should prioritize small, cross-functional pods, rapid shipping, and intense customer-facing product discovery, while keeping core engineering architecture centralized to avoid fragmenting the stack.

The growth path also demands a shift in business model assumptions. The essay argues that the primary source of AI-driven savings for customers will be labor efficiency, which pressures seat-based pricing. The new budget growth is expected to sit in tokens, consumption, and outcomes-based automations. Companies that cannot support token-based economics or agent-driven usage patterns risk missing the fastest-growing parts of spend. The goal is to emerge with a refounded company that has a clear AI-native product line, leadership aligned to the urgency, and an operating cadence designed for rapid learning.

The second path focuses on profitability. For companies that do not see a credible line of sight to a major growth reacceleration, the author advocates rebuilding to 40–50% operating margins, including stock-based compensation. This requires more than incremental layoffs; it implies flattening management layers, standardizing implementation work, eliminating bespoke services, and raising prices where switching costs are strong. Long-tail customers may need to be moved to higher floor pricing or allowed to churn. The goal is to reset the business around shareholder-aligned economics rather than the old habit of treating dilution as a carve-out.

Despite the different objectives, both paths share a common requirement: become AI-native in how the company operates. That means reorganizing around small, fast teams, allocating meaningful budget to token usage per engineer, and accepting that individual engineer output is rising quickly with agentic tools. The essay frames this as a moment for bold management decisions, arguing that software companies must choose a clear direction and rebuild accordingly, because the market no longer rewards the vague middle ground.
