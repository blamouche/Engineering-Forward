# Labor market impacts of AI: A new measure and early evidence
**Source**: https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts
**Date**: March 08, 2026
**Author**: Unknown
**Keywords**: labor market, AI exposure, Anthropic Economic Index, employment, automation

## Elevator pitch
Anthropic proposes a new “observed exposure” metric that blends theoretical task capability with real usage data, finding that AI adoption still lags potential but is already correlated with lower projected job growth in more exposed occupations.

## Takeaways
- The paper introduces “observed exposure,” weighting real‑world AI usage and automation over pure theoretical capability.
- Actual AI usage covers only a fraction of tasks that could be automated, signaling a large adoption gap.
- Occupations with higher observed exposure are projected to grow more slowly through 2034.
- Highly exposed workers skew older, more female, more educated, and higher paid.
- There is no clear rise in unemployment yet, but hiring for younger workers in exposed roles appears to be slowing.

## Synthesis
This research note from Anthropic argues that existing measures of AI labor impact overstate near‑term disruption because they rely on theoretical capability alone. Instead, the authors introduce a new metric—“observed exposure”—that combines task feasibility with actual usage data from the Anthropic Economic Index, while weighting automated and work‑related use more heavily than augmentative or casual use. The goal is to detect early signals of displacement by measuring where AI is actually being used to perform job tasks, rather than where it could be used in principle.

The paper begins with a cautionary framing: past forecasts of technology‑driven labor displacement have often been wrong, whether for offshorability, industrial robots, or trade shocks. The authors argue that AI impacts may be subtle and slow‑moving, more like the internet’s gradual diffusion than the sudden shock of a crisis like COVID‑19. That makes it necessary to build better measurement tools before significant effects are visible in aggregate unemployment data.

To build observed exposure, the authors combine three sources: O*NET’s task database, theoretical exposure scores from Eloundou et al. (2023), and real‑world Claude usage patterns. Tasks are counted as “covered” if they are theoretically feasible and show sufficient work‑related usage in the platform’s traffic. Fully automated use gets full weight, while augmentative use counts half. Task coverage is then aggregated to the occupation level based on time spent on each task. This approach creates a gap between what AI could do and what it is actually doing; that gap becomes the signal of adoption.

Early results show that actual usage is far below theoretical capability. The paper notes that the vast majority of observed usage falls in tasks judged feasible by prior models, but that many feasible tasks still show little or no real usage. For example, within Computer & Math occupations, the theoretical capability is very high, yet only about a third of tasks show coverage in Claude usage. In other occupational categories, the gap is even wider, implying that diffusion and implementation barriers are slowing real impact.

The observed exposure metric correlates modestly with projected job growth from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. Occupations with higher observed exposure are projected to grow less between 2024 and 2034, suggesting the metric captures meaningful economic risk beyond abstract capability measures. The relationship is slight but directionally consistent. The paper also highlights demographic differences: workers in highly exposed roles are more likely to be older, female, more educated, and higher paid. This suggests AI exposure is not evenly distributed and may disproportionately affect white‑collar and knowledge‑intensive roles.

Importantly, the authors do not find systematic increases in unemployment for highly exposed workers since late 2022. However, there is suggestive evidence that hiring for younger workers in exposed occupations is slowing, which could indicate early substitution or changing entry‑level dynamics rather than immediate job loss. The paper frames this as a signal to monitor rather than a definitive conclusion.

Overall, the work positions observed exposure as a practical early‑warning measure for AI’s labor impact. It recognizes that AI capabilities are advancing quickly, but argues that adoption, tooling, legal constraints, and workflow integration create a slower path to real economic displacement. By tracking how the gap between capability and usage narrows over time, the authors hope to identify vulnerable occupations sooner and avoid the pitfalls of purely theoretical predictions.
