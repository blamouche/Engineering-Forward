# Anthropic Economic Index report: Learning curves
**Source**: https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report
**Date**: March 24, 2026
**Author**: Maxim Massenkoff, Eva Lyubich, Peter McCrory, Ruth Appel, Ryan Heller
**Keywords**: AI adoption, labor economics, usage patterns, Claude, learning curves

## Elevator pitch
Anthropic’s March 2026 Economic Index finds Claude usage is diversifying, shifting toward lower‑wage tasks, and that higher‑tenure users achieve higher success rates—evidence of learning‑by‑doing in AI adoption.

## Takeaways
- Claude.ai use cases are less concentrated; top‑10 tasks fell from 24% to 19% of traffic.
- Task value on Claude.ai dipped slightly as usage expanded to more personal and lower‑wage tasks.
- API usage concentrates more in coding and automation, while Claude.ai shifts to broader use.
- Higher‑tenure users have ~10% higher success rates and tackle higher‑value tasks.
- Adoption inequality persists globally, with top 20 countries at 48% of per‑capita usage.

## Synthesis
The March 2026 Anthropic Economic Index analyzes Claude usage data from early February 2026 and focuses on “learning curves” in adoption. The report uses a privacy‑preserving aggregation system to examine how usage patterns evolve over time, comparing results to prior reports covering late 2025. Two shifts stand out: usage diversification and experience effects.

First, Claude.ai is becoming less concentrated in a narrow set of tasks. The share of traffic attributable to the top‑10 O*NET tasks dropped from 24% to 19%, indicating broader adoption across tasks. This diversification aligns with a typical adoption curve: early adopters focus on high‑value tasks like coding, while later users explore more everyday and personal queries. As a result, the average wage value of tasks on Claude.ai slipped slightly (from $49.3 to $47.9 per hour), driven by growth in personal tasks such as sports queries, product comparisons, and home maintenance. Meanwhile, coding workloads continue to migrate from Claude.ai to the API, where they are increasingly automated and fragmented across many task labels.

Second, the report highlights learning‑by‑doing. Users with six months or more of tenure are more likely to engage in work‑related tasks, select higher‑value work, and achieve higher conversation success rates. After controlling for task type, country, model choice, and other factors, long‑tenure users still show a several‑point success advantage. This suggests that experience using Claude leads to better outcomes, though the authors note possible cohort effects and survivorship bias.

Model selection behavior reinforces this pattern. Users allocate more complex and higher‑paid tasks to Opus, Anthropic’s most capable model class, with stronger effects in API usage than in Claude.ai. This indicates that users are calibrating model choice to task difficulty and cost‑performance tradeoffs, especially in programmatic workflows.

The report also finds persistent inequality in adoption. Globally, usage is still concentrated: the top 20 countries account for 48% of per‑capita usage. In the U.S., per‑capita usage is slowly converging across states, but the convergence rate has slowed. This raises concerns about skill‑biased impacts: experienced and higher‑skill users may capture more benefit, potentially widening gaps as AI diffuses.

Overall, the report portrays AI adoption as maturing: broader task diversity, increased automation in API usage, and clear evidence that users improve with experience. The economic implications are mixed—wider adoption suggests democratization, but learning‑curve advantages and geographic concentration imply uneven benefits without targeted interventions.
