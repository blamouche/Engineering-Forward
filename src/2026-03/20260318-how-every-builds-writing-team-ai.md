## Elevator pitch
Every's Editor-in-Chief Kate Lee explains how she's integrating AI into editorial workflows—from screening hundreds of job applications to maintaining editorial standards and managing rapid AI model review publishing.

## Takeaways
- AI processes hundreds of job applications for editorial roles—handling the high-volume screening that previously consumed significant editor time
- AI maintains editorial standards and develops editorial judgment consistency across a distributed writing team
- Every published two major AI model release reviews within 24 hours using AI-accelerated workflows
- Copy editing bottlenecks remain a challenge for automation—human judgment still required at the final quality gate
- Lee's journey from literary agent to tech editorial leader (Medium, WeWork, Stripe Press, Every) provides perspective on publishing transformation

## Synthesis
Kate Lee's conversation with Dan Shipper on the AI & I podcast offers a ground-level view of how a small but influential publishing operation has integrated AI into editorial workflows over the past year. Every, which publishes newsletter-format long-form journalism and analysis about AI, occupies a useful vantage point: it is both a media company adapting to AI-transformed content economics and a close observer of the AI industry it covers.

The recruitment automation story captures a dynamic familiar to any organization receiving large numbers of applications for scarce creative roles. Editorial positions at prominent publications attract hundreds or thousands of applicants, and the triage process—identifying the small number of strong candidates from a large pool—is both high-stakes and time-consuming. Lee describes AI handling initial application processing as her "aha moment"—the first application where the productivity gain was undeniable and the quality threshold was adequate for the task.

The editorial standards and consistency work is more nuanced. Maintaining a coherent voice and quality standard across a distributed team of writers—each with individual styles, varying experience levels, and different relationships with AI tools—is a coordination challenge that editorial leadership has always faced. Lee's use of AI to develop consistent editorial judgment suggests something beyond simple automated editing: using AI to surface standards and apply them systematically in a way that makes institutional knowledge transferable.

The 24-hour dual model review publication is the sharpest demonstration of AI-accelerated editorial production. Publishing substantive analysis of two major AI model releases within a single day would have been impossible for a small team without AI assistance at multiple workflow stages—initial benchmarking, draft synthesis, editorial refinement, and final production. The pace of AI model releases has itself created demand for this kind of accelerated publishing capability.

The honest acknowledgment that copy editing automation remains problematic is a useful calibration. Final quality assurance—where subtle errors in grammar, style, and clarity must be caught before publication—still requires human judgment that AI doesn't reliably provide. This is the remaining friction point in an otherwise increasingly automated editorial pipeline.
