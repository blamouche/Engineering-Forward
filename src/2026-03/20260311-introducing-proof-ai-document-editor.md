# Introducing Proof
**Source**: https://every.to/on-every/introducing-proof
**Date**: 2026-03-11
**Author**: Dan Shipper
**Keywords**: Proof, document editor, AI agents, collaborative writing, provenance tracking, open source, Every.to, agent-native tools

## Elevator pitch
Proof is an agent-native document editor where AI and humans collaborate on writing with equal editing capabilities, provenance tracking showing who wrote what, and no login required—built on the premise that most writing in the next decade will be AI-generated.

## Takeaways
- Agent-native design: AI systems have the same editing capabilities as human collaborators—not a sidebar or suggestion panel, but direct co-authorship.
- Provenance tracking via colored margins: green for human contributions, purple for AI, providing visual attribution throughout the document.
- No login required; open-source—prioritizing accessibility and trust over gatekeeping.
- Problem addressed: AI-generated documents currently fragment into local markdown files, making collaboration and sharing difficult.
- Every.to uses Proof for planning documents, strategy memos, research briefs, and daily task lists with initial drafts starting in Proof.
- Framing: "Most of the writing that will happen in the next decade will be generated by AI agents"—Proof is infrastructure for that reality.

## Synthesis
Proof's design choices reveal a particular theory of how AI-human writing collaboration should work. Most existing tools treat AI as an assistant embedded in a human workflow: suggestion boxes, accept/reject interfaces, AI that generates text that humans then edit. Proof inverts this: the document is a shared space where AI and human are co-equal contributors, and the interface is organized around tracking and communicating that dual authorship.

The provenance tracking feature is the key functional differentiator. When readers see a document, they currently have no way to know what percentage of the text was human-written versus AI-generated, or which specific sections came from which source. Proof makes this visible by default. This addresses a real and growing social problem: the collapse of document authorship creates uncertainty about accountability, authenticity, and quality that readers increasingly can't resolve.

The "no login required, open source" positioning reflects lessons learned from productivity tool adoption. Friction at the entry point kills collaborative tools; requiring accounts before a document can be shared or reviewed eliminates casual use cases. Making the provenance tracking trustworthy requires open-source verification—proprietary provenance tracking could be gamed.

Dan Shipper's framing—that AI-generated writing is the default of the next decade, not an exception—is significant as a product design philosophy. Most AI writing tools are designed around the assumption that human authorship is primary and AI assists. Proof is designed around the opposite assumption: AI generates, humans direct, review, and refine. The tool that looks correct depends heavily on which assumption proves true.
