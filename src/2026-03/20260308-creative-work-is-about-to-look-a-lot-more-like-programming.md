# Creative Work Is About to Look a Lot More Like Programming
**Source**: https://every.to/thesis/creative-work-is-about-to-look-a-lot-more-like-programming
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Weber Wong
**Keywords**: creative workflows, visual programming, AI tools, Flora, design systems

## Elevator pitch
Weber Wong argues that the real AI disruption in creative work is shifting from one‑off artifacts to reusable workflows—turning creativity into visual programming.

## Takeaways
- Generative AI accelerates output but still traps creators in “artifact thinking.”
- The next leap is reusable, shareable workflows that encode creative process.
- Competitive advantage moves from model access to workflow orchestration.
- Visual programming—building systems on a canvas—scales creative intent.
- Design’s evolution (e.g., Figma components) is a precedent for reusable creative systems.

## Synthesis
Weber Wong’s essay reframes the AI‑creative revolution as a shift in workflow architecture rather than model capability. He argues that creative professionals are still stuck in a “one‑output‑at‑a‑time” mindset—even with generative tools. Whether you click through Photoshop menus or type prompts in Midjourney, the result is usually a single artifact that doesn’t carry process knowledge forward. The core inefficiency is not speed, it is reset: each new project starts from zero, and the method cannot be reliably transferred or reproduced.

Wong calls this “artifact thinking,” a mental model where the unit of work is the final output. In this framing, generative AI only increases the speed at which artifacts are produced, but it does not solve the deeper issue of scalability and collaboration. A prompt is still disposable; you cannot hand it to a colleague and expect consistent results, nor can you reliably build on yesterday’s work without recreating the same steps. The supposed liberation of AI masks the same underlying fragility.

The alternative is “workflow thinking.” Instead of producing outputs directly, creators build systems that generate outputs. This is the leap from “designing images” to “designing the process that designs images.” Wong’s platform, Flora, embodies this idea with an infinite canvas where text, image, and video models can be orchestrated into repeatable pipelines. In that model, the creator’s advantage is not a secret prompt or a single great output; it is a reusable method that can be shared, modified, and scaled.

He connects this shift to a familiar precedent in design tooling. Before Figma, UI designers made every screen as a separate artifact; changing a button color required editing every screen. Components and auto‑layout transformed that workflow into a system of reusable primitives, enabling consistency and scale. Wong argues that AI creative work is now at an analogous inflection point: the next wave will replace manual or prompt‑based repetition with reusable systems and visual programming.

The essay also reframes competitive advantage in a world of rapidly improving models. As model access becomes commoditized, the differentiator becomes orchestration: how well a creator can chain models, manage variations, and encode their taste into workflows. “Visual programming” becomes the foundation, not just a metaphor. It describes a future where creative teams build libraries of workflows the way engineers build software modules—composable, versioned, and shareable.

The implication is strategic: creative professionals should invest in tools and mental models that turn taste into systems. AI’s biggest impact is not in making images faster, but in letting creators build repeatable pipelines that amplify their judgment across projects, teams, and time. In Wong’s view, the creators who thrive will be those who treat creative work as programmable, not merely generative.
