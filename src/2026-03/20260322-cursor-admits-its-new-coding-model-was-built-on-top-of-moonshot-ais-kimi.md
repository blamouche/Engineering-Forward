# Cursor admits its new coding model was built on top of Moonshot AI’s Kimi
**Source**: https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/22/cursor-admits-its-new-coding-model-was-built-on-top-of-moonshot-ais-kimi/
**Date**: March 22, 2026
**Author**: Anthony Ha
**Keywords**: coding models, open source, model provenance, Cursor, Moonshot AI

## Elevator pitch
Cursor acknowledged that its Composer 2 coding model was built on top of Moonshot AI’s open‑source Kimi 2.5 base, highlighting how frontier‑level products can be layered on open models—and how transparency about model provenance is becoming a reputational and geopolitical issue.

## Takeaways
- Cursor launched Composer 2 as a “frontier‑level” coding model but users identified Kimi 2.5 as the base.
- Cursor confirmed the open‑source origin, claiming most compute and performance gains come from its own training.
- Moonshot AI and Fireworks AI framed the use as an authorized commercial partnership.
- The omission sparked criticism, showing that provenance disclosure is now expected.
- The episode reflects broader U.S.–China AI tensions around model origin and trust.

## Synthesis
Cursor, the well‑funded U.S. coding‑assistant startup, launched a new model called Composer 2 and positioned it as “frontier‑level” in performance. Soon after the announcement, users on X pointed out evidence that the model’s identifier referenced Kimi 2.5, an open‑source model released by China’s Moonshot AI. The implication was that Composer 2 was built on a Kimi base, a claim Cursor initially did not disclose. The situation escalated publicly as the community questioned the lack of attribution and transparency.

Cursor’s leadership moved quickly to acknowledge the origin. Lee Robinson, vice president of developer education, confirmed that Composer 2 “started from an open‑source base,” but argued the final model differs meaningfully from Kimi. He stated that only about a quarter of the compute used in training the final model came from the base model, and that Cursor’s additional pretraining and reinforcement learning delivered distinct benchmark performance. Moonshot AI’s Kimi account reinforced the legitimacy of the usage, saying it was part of an authorized commercial partnership through Fireworks AI, and expressed support for open‑model ecosystems that enable downstream innovation.

This episode is notable for a few reasons. First, it highlights how quickly the market has normalized the idea that competitive products can be built by extending open models rather than training from scratch. Cursor’s business scale and valuation underscore that even high‑profile, U.S.‑based companies are leveraging open bases for speed and cost efficiency. Second, it shows that provenance expectations are rising. The community pushback was not about the legality of usage but the lack of upfront disclosure. The company’s co‑founder ultimately admitted it was a miss to omit mention of the Kimi base and said future disclosures would be clearer.

The issue is also charged geopolitically. The AI “arms race” narrative—often framed as U.S. versus China—means model origins can carry reputational and regulatory weight. Using a Chinese base model might be interpreted differently by customers, investors, or policymakers, even if the resulting model is largely trained in the United States. That makes transparency not just an ethical concern but a strategic necessity. It can mitigate speculation, prevent reputational damage, and preempt potential scrutiny around licensing and compliance.

From a product and ecosystem perspective, the situation reflects a broader reality: the open‑model ecosystem enables rapid iteration and commercialization, but it also demands clearer supply‑chain disclosures. As models become foundational infrastructure, provenance is increasingly like component traceability in hardware or software dependencies. Cursor’s rapid admission and clarification from Moonshot AI show an emerging norm of public accountability. The likely outcome is that developers, researchers, and companies will face stronger expectations to document base models, training lineage, and licensing posture as part of standard release notes.

Overall, the Composer 2 incident is less about a single company than a signal of a maturing AI ecosystem. The competitive advantage increasingly comes from fine‑tuning, reinforcement learning, data curation, and product integration, but the base model’s identity still matters. Transparency on model lineage is becoming a core trust factor, especially when the base comes from a different geopolitical context.
