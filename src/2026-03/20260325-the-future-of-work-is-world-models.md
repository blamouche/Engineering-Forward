# The future of work is world models
**Source**: https://www.strangeloopcanon.com/p/the-future-of-work-is-world-models
**Date**: March 25, 2026
**Author**: Unknown
**Keywords**: world models, agents, enterprise, simulation, operations

## Elevator pitch
As agent counts outnumber humans, companies will need enterprise “world models” to simulate business decisions and manage by exception rather than manual oversight.

## Takeaways
- Future companies may run hundreds or thousands of agents alongside humans.
- Oversight shifts from direct control to anomaly detection and simulation.
- Enterprises need explicit operational models of their business state.
- World models can predict outcomes of interventions like pricing or staffing.
- Orchestration, observability, and RL environments are inputs to a broader model.

## Synthesis
This essay argues that the next phase of AI‑driven work will require “world models” for enterprises. As companies deploy large numbers of agents—handling customer service, pricing, procurement, and internal workflows—traditional management practices break down. The author suggests the future resembles a strategy game: instead of directly controlling every action, leaders will manage by exception, scanning for anomalies and simulating responses.

The key missing ingredient is an explicit operational model of the business. Today, such models exist implicitly in people’s heads and fragmented dashboards. But a world of thousands of agents making decisions requires a shared, machine‑readable environment that tracks state, understands rules, and can predict the consequences of interventions. The author draws an analogy to autonomous vehicles, where world models and simulation are essential for training and testing. Business operations, in this framing, are similarly complex and require a simulated environment to evaluate “what if” scenarios before actions are taken.

The essay illustrates the idea with a real‑estate example: multiple properties run on disconnected systems, with data scattered across CRMs, accounting tools, and work order systems. In this environment, an enterprise world model would unify the state and simulate outcomes—such as the impact of a price cut, a maintenance decision, or a staffing change—providing executives with predicted ROI and trade‑offs. The model would turn management into a cycle of monitoring deltas, testing counterfactuals, and choosing interventions based on simulated outcomes.

The author argues that many current AI tools are partial components of this vision: orchestration platforms coordinate agents, observability tools track actions, and RL environments attempt to generate structured training data. But none of these alone answer the central question: “If I do X, what happens to the business?” The world model is proposed as the unifying layer that connects these components into a predictive, decision‑oriented system.

The piece also notes that building such models cannot happen in the abstract. Enterprises are messy, with heterogeneous systems and local variations. Creating a usable world model requires integrating real operational data, defining action spaces, and collecting outcome signals at scale. This is resource‑intensive but becomes increasingly valuable as agent counts grow.

Overall, the essay frames enterprise world models as the operating system for AI‑run organizations. As agents proliferate, companies will need a live, predictive simulation of their business to manage complexity, evaluate interventions, and maintain control. Without such models, autonomy scales faster than oversight, risking brittle operations and poor decision quality.
