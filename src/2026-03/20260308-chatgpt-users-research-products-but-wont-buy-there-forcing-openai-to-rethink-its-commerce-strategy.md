# ChatGPT users research products but won't buy there, forcing OpenAI to rethink its commerce strategy
**Source**: https://the-decoder.com/chatgpt-users-research-products-but-wont-buy-there-forcing-openai-to-rethink-its-commerce-strategy/
**Date**: March 08, 2026
**Author**: Unknown
**Keywords**: OpenAI, commerce, checkout, revenue, partnerships

## Elevator pitch
OpenAI is backing away from direct in‑chat purchases because users research products in ChatGPT but rarely complete transactions there, pushing the company toward partner‑app checkouts and shrinking its commission upside.

## Takeaways
- OpenAI is ending direct checkout inside ChatGPT and routing purchases through partner apps instead.
- Merchant adoption of ChatGPT checkout was minimal, with only a handful of Shopify sellers participating.
- The direct‑checkout model required heavy manual onboarding and lacked tax‑collection infrastructure.
- OpenAI still faces major revenue pressure as it prepares for an IPO and burns cash rapidly.
- The shift increases dependence on partners like Instacart, Target, Expedia, and Booking.com.

## Synthesis
The article reports that OpenAI is scaling back its plan to process purchases directly inside ChatGPT. Instead, transactions will now be handled by third‑party apps integrated into the chatbot. The earlier vision—announced with partners such as Shopify, Etsy, and Stripe—was that ChatGPT could capture a commerce commission by letting users check out without leaving the conversation. In practice, usage and merchant participation were weak. A source cited in The Information says users do research and compare products in ChatGPT but rarely follow through with a purchase there. As a result, OpenAI is shifting to a partner‑app model where the app owns the checkout flow and revenue capture.

Merchant onboarding also appears to have been a bottleneck. The article notes that integrating direct checkout required OpenAI to onboard each retailer individually, a labor‑intensive process at odds with the scale needed for a broad retail marketplace. By February, OpenAI still lacked systems to collect and remit state sales taxes. These operational gaps made the direct‑checkout approach costly to scale, even before user demand issues were factored in.

Moving forward, purchases will go through apps from partners such as Instacart, Target, Expedia, and Booking.com. Instacart already allows users to link existing accounts and pay through them. OpenAI and Stripe say they will continue to build the Agentic Commerce Protocol to support app‑based transactions, indicating that commerce remains a strategic priority, but not necessarily via a centralized checkout controlled by OpenAI. The shift implies that OpenAI will capture less direct revenue per transaction, since it now relies on partners’ app economics rather than owning the checkout layer.

The article frames the change as another revenue headwind for OpenAI as it approaches a potential IPO. OpenAI’s burn rate is high and its subscription business is limited by the small share of users who pay. The piece suggests that ads are a possible fallback, but notes that advertising in chatbots has already proven unreliable for peers like Perplexity. The commerce retrenchment thus undermines one of the more plausible near‑term monetization paths.

A strategic wrinkle is Amazon’s large investment in OpenAI, which may influence the commerce roadmap. The article suggests Amazon’s interest could be aligned with steering ChatGPT traffic toward Amazon’s own retail platform. Until recently, Amazon had blocked AI apps from accessing its product data, but the investment may change incentives. If Amazon becomes a preferred route for checkout, it could further reduce OpenAI’s direct control over monetization.

Overall, the piece argues that OpenAI’s attempt to internalize commerce inside ChatGPT faced both user behavior hurdles and operational friction. Users appear to trust chatbots for discovery and research but still prefer to purchase within familiar retail ecosystems. For OpenAI, that means the strategic value of commerce will come more from traffic and integrations than from taking a direct cut of payments. The shift underscores a broader pattern: conversational AI can influence purchasing decisions, but translating that influence into direct revenue at scale remains challenging.
