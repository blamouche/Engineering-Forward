# How to Make Sense of AI
**Source**: https://commoncog.com/how-to-make-sense-of-ai/
**Date**: 2026-03-18
**Author**: Cedric Chin
**Keywords**: AI business strategy, uncertainty, adaptation, field reports, framework, hype navigation, Commoncog

## Elevator pitch
During AI's rapid change, ignore opinions and forecasts—consume only detailed field reports of actual AI use cases, then evaluate them against four questions about outcomes, actions, context-specific value, and causal relationships.

## Takeaways
- The core problem: during technological upheaval, fear-driven content proliferates to capture attention, creating distraction rather than clarity.
- The proposed method: ignore all opinions and forecasts; consume only detailed field reports of actual AI use cases.
- Four questions to evaluate each field report: What new outcomes are possible? What actions can we take? What's the relative value to our specific context? What causal relationships exist?
- Core insight: "When a new technology emerges, impacts are uneven and ultimately unpredictable"—context-specific observation beats broad analysis.
- Effective businesspeople don't predict futures—they adapt faster than competitors by observing present capabilities clearly.

## Synthesis
Chin's framework is specifically designed for the information environment during a rapidly evolving technology, where the volume of AI opinion and analysis far exceeds the volume of actionable evidence. The distinction between opinions/forecasts (which are abundant and often contradictory) and field reports (which provide specific evidence about specific applications) is the key filtering criterion.

The four evaluation questions are designed to extract actionable information from field reports rather than just filing them as interesting anecdotes. "What new outcomes are possible?" identifies genuine capability expansions. "What actions can we take?" translates observation into decision. "What's the relative value to our specific context?" filters for relevance rather than impressiveness. "What causal relationships exist?" builds the mental model of how and why AI works in a given application, enabling generalization to related contexts.

The "uneven and ultimately unpredictable" framing correctly identifies why broad AI analysis has limited decision-making value for most businesses. AI's impacts vary dramatically by industry, function, organization size, existing technology stack, and dozens of other factors. A field report from a healthcare company automating clinical documentation provides more actionable signal for similar healthcare companies than any general prediction about AI's economic impact.

The adaptation speed argument is worth examining critically. It's true that faster adaptation matters more than prediction accuracy when the future is uncertain. But adaptation speed depends on having accurate information about what's working now—which requires filtering out the noise that Chin's method addresses. The method is essentially "optimize your information diet for decision quality rather than engagement or novelty."
