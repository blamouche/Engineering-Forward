# Andrew Ross Sorkin on the Risk if AI Succeeds
**Source**: https://www.bigtechnology.com/p/andrew-ross-sorkin-on-the-risk-if
**Date**: 2026-03-13
**Author**: Alex Kantrowitz (interviewer), Andrew Ross Sorkin (guest)
**Keywords**: AI employment, mass unemployment, productivity paradox, private credit, AI risk, DealBook, wealth distribution

## Elevator pitch
Andrew Ross Sorkin argues the primary AI risk isn't market disruption but mass unemployment—and that the productivity gains AI promises create a paradox: whose buying power enables the growth those gains are supposed to generate?

## Takeaways
- The primary AI risk is not market disruption but mass unemployment if AI delivers on its productivity promises: "we are the cost" that productivity gains will eliminate.
- The productivity-growth paradox: technological efficiency doesn't automatically translate to wealth distribution—"It's not, are there more things to do—it's who's going to have the money to do those things?"
- Private credit opacity: interconnections between private equity and private credit create systemic risk through delayed valuations that mask leverage.
- Human value in content: audiences still prefer humans with direct access (journalists with locker room access, experienced analysts) over AI-generated content in certain domains.
- The risk isn't that AI fails—it's that AI succeeds and the economic gains concentrate rather than distribute.

## Synthesis
Sorkin's framing inverts the standard AI risk narrative. Most AI discourse treats failure to deliver as the primary risk (hype without substance, wasted investment). Sorkin's point is that successful delivery creates its own risk: if AI dramatically reduces the cost of knowledge work, the workers currently performing that work face displacement at a scale that could exceed the economy's capacity to reabsorb them quickly.

The paradox he identifies is structurally important. AI-driven productivity increases corporate efficiency by reducing labor costs. But labor costs are also workers' incomes—the purchasing power that funds consumption. If productivity gains concentrate in corporate profits rather than wages or consumer prices, the macroeconomic consequences aren't just about displaced workers; they're about reduced aggregate demand. A more productive economy where fewer people can afford what it produces is not straightforwardly better.

The private credit observation is less AI-specific but tells a similar story about structural risks that become visible when growth assumptions are challenged. Private credit markets have grown enormously by offering investors higher yields through opacity—delayed valuations mean interconnected leverage isn't marked to market until something forces it. AI disruption to certain professional services could trigger a revaluation cascade.

The human-preference observation about journalism is worth noting precisely because it's domain-specific. Sorkin's position in media gives him visibility into reader behavior around AI-generated versus human-written content. The finding that audiences still prefer humans with direct access suggests authentication and firsthand experience retain value that AI can't replicate by synthesis—a more nuanced claim than either "humans are irreplaceable" or "AI will replace all content workers."
