# The most important question nobody's asking about AI
**Source**: https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/dow-anthropic
**Date**: 2026-03-11
**Author**: Dwarkesh Patel
**Keywords**: Anthropic, Department of War, AI governance, mass surveillance, autonomous weapons, AI alignment, regulation, AI safety, government contracts

## Elevator pitch
The DOW's designation of Anthropic as a supply chain risk for refusing to remove safeguards against mass surveillance and autonomous weapons reveals the most important unresolved question in AI: to whom should AI systems be aligned?

## Takeaways
- The DOW designated Anthropic as a supply chain risk when the company refused to remove safeguards against mass surveillance and autonomous weapons use from its models.
- The government should have simply declined to use Anthropic's models rather than using designation as coercion—the difference matters for the precedent it sets.
- Structural vulnerability: as AI becomes more pervasive, tech companies will increasingly be unable to segregate AI for specific government uses, potentially facing forced choices between government contracts and their AI providers.
- Mass surveillance risk: AI gives "everybody more leverage on whatever assets and authority they currently have"—including authoritarian and abusive uses by powerful actors.
- Purpose-built AI regulation will be weaponized by power-seeking actors, making restrictive regulatory frameworks potentially more dangerous than beneficial.
- The central question: "to whom should AI systems be aligned?" has not been adequately addressed by society, policy, or the AI industry.

## Synthesis
The Anthropic-DOW conflict is unusual because it makes explicit a tension that usually remains implicit: governments are major customers for AI capabilities, but governments also sometimes want to use AI for purposes that AI safety researchers consider dangerous. Most AI policy discussions treat government as the regulator; this case shows government as a customer exerting leverage through procurement.

Patel's "should have just not used them" point is important for norm-setting. The designation mechanism transforms a purchasing decision into a coercive instrument: instead of "we won't buy from you," the message becomes "we'll threaten your entire business model unless you comply." Whether or not Anthropic's safeguards are the right safeguards, the precedent of using supply chain risk designations to coerce AI safety decisions is dangerous independent of the specific case.

The mass surveillance observation reflects something the AI capabilities discourse often underweights: most AI capabilities are dual-use, and the asymmetry matters. Defensive uses of AI surveillance capabilities (finding criminals, identifying threats) require democratic oversight, legal process, and proportionality constraints that offensive or authoritarian uses don't. Capabilities developed for one purpose are available for the other. "AI gives everybody more leverage" is neutral as a description but highly asymmetric in practice when the actors include both democratic institutions with accountability mechanisms and authoritarian ones without.

The "to whom should AI be aligned" question is perhaps the deepest unresolved issue in the field. Most alignment research focuses on the technical problem of making AI systems do what they're supposed to do. This assumes a clear principal—the user, the developer, society—whose preferences should be satisfied. But these principals conflict: users want convenience, developers want revenue and safety, governments want control, and society has diverse and competing values. The cases where these conflict, like Anthropic-DOW, are where the alignment question becomes concrete.
