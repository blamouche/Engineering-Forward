# Please Stop the AI Confidence Theater
**Source**: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/cp/204728693
**Date**: 2026-07-02
**Author**: Elena Verna (Lenny's Newsletter)
**Keywords**: AI hype, AI adoption, AI confidence theater, realistic AI use, AI anxiety, AI workflows, productivity theater

## Elevator pitch
The performative hype around AI on social media — endless "AI changed my life" posts — is creating anxiety and distorted expectations while most actual AI usage remains basic summarization and email drafting; the gap between theater and reality is harming innovation and adoption.

## Takeaways
- The cycle of AI doom headlines (killing education, eliminating jobs, destroying SaaS, replacing Google) creates an atmosphere of anxiety that outpaces actual capability changes.
- When challenged with "Cool. Show me." — asking people to demonstrate their life-changing AI workflows — most reveal basic tasks: summarizing Slack, answering emails, scheduled scans, research, and booking.
- The author works at an AI company and uses AI all day, yet still feels like everyone else has cracked the code — illustrating the gap between perception and reality.
- AI Confidence Theater harms innovation by robbing people of genuine "aha" moments with AI tools, replacing them with inflated expectations that lead to disappointment.
- The plea is to dial down performative AI hype so people can discover and adopt AI tools at a realistic pace without feeling they're falling behind.

## Synthesis
Elena Verna's essay is a pushback against the performative dimension of AI discourse — what she calls "AI Confidence Theater." The piece catalogues the relentless cycle of AI headlines that have dominated social media: AI killing education, eliminating writers and engineers, replacing Google, killing SaaS, and now mandating that everyone have an AI agent. Each wave creates anxiety and FOMO that far exceeds what the technology actually delivers in most people's daily workflows.

The most cutting observation is the "Cool. Show me." test. When the author asks people who claim AI changed their life to demonstrate, the workflows are invariably basic: summarizing Slack threads, drafting email responses, performing scheduled scans, doing research, booking things. Useful, yes — but not life-changing, and certainly not something whose removal would cause work to fall apart. This gap between the volume of AI hype (11/10) and the reality of AI usage (basic productivity tasks) is the central tension.

The harm argument is well-articulated. The theater isn't a victimless crime. It robs people of genuine discovery moments with AI by setting expectations so high that real, modest AI use feels like failure. It creates anxiety in professionals who feel they're falling behind. And it distorts innovation priorities — companies chase AI agent capabilities because of social pressure rather than actual need. The author, who works at an AI company and uses AI extensively, admits she still feels behind, which underscores how pervasive the theater has become.

The essay is ultimately a call for honesty. Stop performing AI confidence. Let people discover tools at their own pace. Acknowledge that most AI usage today is productivity augmentation, not transformation — and that's fine. The real adoption curve is quieter and more gradual than the social media hype cycle suggests.
