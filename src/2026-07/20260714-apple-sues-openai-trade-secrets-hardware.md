# Apple Sues OpenAI Over Trade Secrets and Hardware Ambitions
**Source**: https://www.bigtechnology.com/p/apples-lawsuit-against-openai-makes
**Date**: 2026-07-14
**Author**: Marty Swant and Alex Kantrowitz (Big Technology)
**Keywords**: Apple, OpenAI, lawsuit, trade secrets, hardware, AI competition, Tang Tan, Chang Liu

## Elevator pitch
Apple has filed a trade secrets lawsuit against OpenAI, alleging a months-long campaign by former Apple employees to accelerate OpenAI's hardware business — a rare legal move by Apple targeting a company that hasn't yet shipped a competing product.

## Takeaways
- Apple's lawsuit alleges theft of trade secrets and a deliberate campaign by OpenAI to accelerate its hardware business using Apple's proprietary information, naming former Apple employees Tang Tan (24-year Apple veteran, iPhone and Apple Watch design lead) and Chang Liu (who allegedly downloaded confidential files including manufacturing and testing details for Apple's circuit boards).
- This is unusual for Apple, which typically sues companies already shipping competing products (Qualcomm, Samsung) rather than pre-product rivals — suggesting the competitive threat from OpenAI's hardware ambitions is taken very seriously.
- The lawsuit reveals OpenAI's strategic direction into hardware, which puts it in direct competition with Apple's core business and validates concerns that AI companies are moving beyond software into devices.
- OpenAI responded that it has "no interest in other companies' trade secrets" and remains "focused on building innovative technology that empowers people everywhere."
- The case highlights the intensifying talent war in AI: OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, and Cursor are all expanding their European presence, and key employees moving between companies carry deep institutional knowledge that is difficult to protect.

## Synthesis
Apple's lawsuit against OpenAI, filed in July 2026, represents a significant escalation in the AI industry's competitive dynamics. Unlike Apple's typical legal targets — companies like Qualcomm and Samsung that are already shipping competing products — OpenAI is a pre-product rival in hardware, making this case qualitatively different from Apple's previous litigation.

The complaint centers on two former Apple employees: Tang Tan, who spent 24 years at Apple leading design on the iPhone and Apple Watch and is now OpenAI's chief hardware officer, and Chang Liu, a former Apple engineer who allegedly downloaded confidential manufacturing and testing details for Apple's circuit boards even after leaving the company. Apple's allegation that OpenAI engaged in "a months-long campaign to accelerate OpenAI's hardware business" using Apple's proprietary information paints a picture of systematic knowledge transfer rather than incidental overlap.

The underlying strategic significance is clear: OpenAI is building hardware, and Apple sees this as a direct competitive threat. When a company valued at $11 billion (Harvey's valuation as context for AI company valuations) starts hiring hardware executives from the world's most successful consumer electronics company, the competitive landscape shifts fundamentally. This isn't just about trade secrets — it's about whether AI companies can leapfrog from software into devices, and whether incumbents like Apple can prevent them from doing so by leveraging their accumulated expertise.

The case also illuminates the broader talent war in AI. OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, and Cursor are all expanding their European operations, and Sweden's Legora (Harvey's European rival) is pursuing aggressive M&A. The movement of key employees between these companies carries enormous institutional knowledge that is difficult to protect through NDAs alone.

From an engineering perspective, the case underscores a new reality: the most valuable "trade secrets" in AI may not be algorithms or model weights, but the deep manufacturing, supply chain, and design expertise that enables companies to ship physical products at scale. If OpenAI is indeed building consumer hardware, Apple's manufacturing know-how is among the most valuable knowledge any departing employee could carry.
