# Claude Science and Anthropic's Drug Discovery Strategy: Selling the Shovels
**Source**: https://every.to/context-window/a-tale-of-two-models
**Date**: 2026-07-05
**Author**: Every Staff / Ashwin Sharma
**Keywords**: Anthropic, Claude Science, drug discovery, Fable 5, Sonnet 5, AI models, biotech, dogfooding

## Elevator pitch
Anthropic launched Claude Science, a desktop research tool for scientists, and announced its own preclinical drug programs—not to become a pharma company, but to dogfood its AI tools and build the platform layer for drug development.

## Takeaways
- Claude Science lets scientists run analyses, visualize molecular and genomic data, and show the exact code behind every result—positioning Anthropic as a platform for scientific research
- Anthropic is running its own preclinical drug programs specifically to test and improve its AI tools, following the "dogfooding" strategy of deliberately using your own technology on real, difficult problems
- The strategy mirrors "selling the shovels" in the 1849 gold rush: rather than developing drugs end-to-end, Anthropic aims to fix the evaluation and verification bottlenecks in drug development and sell the resulting platform to pharma companies
- Fable 5 returned on July 1 and can spin up working apps from a single prompt (rebuilt Every's document editor Proof in about three hours), but Sonnet 5 landed as a "Goldilocks model pitched for everyone that impresses no one"—capable but without a clear use case advantage over GPT-5.5, Opus 4.8, or Fable 5
- AI still can't reliably make a PowerPoint deck; a 24-skill pipeline at $62 per deck was needed and still not recommended for most teams

## Synthesis
Anthropic's dual launch of Claude Science and its own drug programs reveals a strategy that's more sophisticated than it first appears. On the surface, a frontier AI lab running preclinical drug programs looks like a pivot into biotech. In practice, it's an extension of the dogfooding principle: by tackling one of the hardest verification problems in science—where biological systems move slowly and feedback takes months—Anthropic forces its AI to confront real-world complexity that synthetic benchmarks can't replicate.

The key insight is that drug development's biggest bottlenecks aren't in idea generation but in evaluation and verification. An AI can suggest a drug target or design a molecule in seconds, but confirming whether the suggestion is correct requires laboratory experiments and clinical studies. If Anthropic can solve this verification bottleneck—even partially—they build the workflow layer that ties data, models, experiments, and decision-making together. That platform, not any individual drug, is where the lasting value sits.

The parallel launches of Fable 5 and Sonnet 5 frame the competitive dynamics. Fable 5, back online after an outage, immediately demonstrated agentic capability by rebuilding a document editor in three hours. Sonnet 5, positioned as the "just right" model, found no clear niche—it was competent at everything but exceptional at nothing, with faster, cheaper, or smarter alternatives available for nearly every specific use case. This suggests the frontier model market is fragmenting: rather than one model to rule them all, the future belongs to specialized tools for specialized tasks.

The PowerPoint automation story is a useful counterpoint to the hype. Despite the impressive-sounding 24-skill pipeline, the result still wasn't reliable enough to recommend for most teams. The hardest part—figuring out what to say—remains beyond current AI capability. This underscores a theme across the week's coverage: AI is making extraordinary progress on well-defined tasks but still struggles with the ill-defined, context-heavy, judgment-driven work that occupies most knowledge workers' time. Anthropic's bet on drug discovery is, at its core, a bet that the platform for solving those hard verification problems will be more valuable than any single application built on top of it.
