# The United States' OpenAI Equity Stake, Meta The Neocloud, Karp's Attack
**Source**: https://www.bigtechnology.com/p/the-united-states-openai-equity-stake
**Date**: 2026-07-02
**Author**: Alex Kantrowitz
**Keywords**: OpenAI, equity stake, US government, Meta, neocloud, Palantir, AI industry, compute, regulation

## Elevator pitch
Three major AI industry shifts converge: OpenAI may give the US government a 5% equity stake, Meta is becoming a neocloud selling excess compute, and Palantir's CEO is attacking frontier labs for competing with their own customers.

## Takeaways
- OpenAI is considering giving a 5% equity stake to the US government to "clear political obstacles," with similar action expected from Anthropic, Google, Meta, and others—but this would create a fundamental conflict where the government is both a model approver and a vested investor
- Meta is developing plans for a cloud infrastructure business selling AI computing power and models, similar to SpaceX's approach—significant because it signals excess compute at Meta while Anthropic and OpenAI can't get enough
- Palantir CEO Alex Karp went after frontier AI labs in a CNBC interview, questioning data ownership, prompt security, and data caching—positioning Palantir as an alternative while some of the same criticisms could apply to Palantir itself
- The neocloud trend reveals a sorting of AI industry winners: those who sell compute plus a proprietary frontier model (plus sometimes an application) at a markup vs. those who only sell compute and licensed models
- OpenAI's Greg Brockman discussed the trajectory toward persistent agents that melt the interface away, and why compute will ultimately decide the AI race

## Synthesis
Alex Kantrowitz weaves together three seemingly separate stories that reveal a common thread: the AI industry is entering a phase of consolidation and power alignment that will reshape how compute, data, and regulation interact.

The most striking proposal is OpenAI's consideration of giving the US government a 5% equity stake. As Kantrowitz argues, this is likely an absurd idea that will never come to fruition. It would require a broad coalition of partners who have no incentive to join, and it creates an irreconcilable conflict: the government would simultaneously be responsible for regulating and approving AI models while holding a financial stake in specific labs' success. The US should share in AI companies' success through taxes, not equity. Yet the fact that this proposal is being floated at all signals how deeply intertwined AI policy and AI business have become.

Meta's pivot toward becoming a neocloud is more consequential than it initially appears. On the surface, it's a straightforward business decision: Meta has excess compute capacity and wants to monetize it. But the deeper implication is that the AI industry is sorting into clear winners and losers. The winners are companies that can sell compute plus a proprietary frontier model at a markup. Companies with only excess compute and no frontier model to offer are in a fundamentally weaker position. The fact that Anthropic and OpenAI can't get enough capacity while Meta has left over tells you everything about where the demand sits.

Karp's attack on frontier labs is the canary in the coal mine for a broader pattern. As AI labs consolidate power and begin competing with industries that rely on them—coding, design, finance, and beyond—the companies being disintermediated will push back hard. Karp's move is partly strategic positioning for Palantir, but it also surfaces a real tension: when your AI provider starts competing with you, trust erodes.

The podcast with Greg Brockman adds another dimension: OpenAI's trajectory toward persistent agents and the conviction that compute will remain the binding constraint. The fundamental question across all three stories is about power—who holds it, who distributes it, and whether the structures being built will concentrate or disperse it.
