# Drowning in Demos? Here's a Better Way to Prototype
**Source**: https://every.to/drowning-in-demos-better-way-to-prototype
**Date**: 2026-07-21
**Author**: Hilary Gridley (Every)
**Keywords**: AI prototyping, product management, decision-making, Whoop, beta testing, hack days, product strategy

## Elevator pitch
When AI makes building cheap, prototypes become pitches instead of decisions — the real discipline shifts from "can we build it?" to "is this even the right problem?" and only user data, not internal opinions, can answer that.

## Takeaways
- Cheap prototyping tools (Bolt, Replit, etc.) led to prototype bloat at Whoop — more prototypes than ever, but no better way to decide which ones were good
- The original purpose of prototyping was de-risking expensive builds; when building becomes cheap, that purpose evaporates and prototypes drift into becoming pitches for stakeholder excitement
- Going from 5 to 30 prototypes risks diluting focus across 6x as many ideas without making it any easier to choose — every prototype still demands someone's attention
- Whoop's solution: define outcomes before building (6-week cross-functional working group defined what AI should accomplish for users), give hack days lanes (engineers explore within defined pillars), and put rough versions in front of 12,000 beta members instead of stakeholders
- The product manager's job shifts from writing specs with answers to creating conditions where the team finds answers together — "lanes, not guardrails"

## Synthesis
Hilary Gridley, former head of core product at Whoop, describes a pattern now playing out across the industry: as AI tools make prototyping nearly free, teams drown in demos without getting better at deciding what to ship. The article is a product-management counterpart to the engineering-focused content that usually dominates AI discussions, and it articulates a structural problem most teams are experiencing but haven't named.

The core insight is that prototyping's original justification — confirming an expensive build was worth doing — disappears when building costs collapse. Without that forcing function, prototypes become pitches: demos designed to get colleagues excited rather than experiments designed to answer specific questions. The result is organizational noise, with engineers building cool things on hack days and PMs creating mockups to show possibilities, but no consistent way to evaluate which ideas deserve real investment.

Whoop's AI product team, led by Anjali Ahuja, found a three-part solution. First, they spent six weeks with a cross-functional group defining what AI should accomplish for members in terms of outcomes (capture more context, make data more actionable) rather than features (build a sleep coach, add a chatbot). Second, hack days got lanes — engineers could explore freely, but their prototypes had to test whether AI could move those specific outcomes. Third, instead of demoing to stakeholders, they put prototypes in front of 12,000 beta members who had opted in to test unfinished features, generating data instead of opinions.

The piece's most provocative claim is about the PM role itself. When the technology frontier moves weekly, PMs can no longer write requirements documents — they don't know what's possible. Instead, their job becomes creating the conditions for the team to discover answers: defining which problems matter, agreeing on what success looks like, and getting prototypes in front of users rather than internal stakeholders. Gridley calls this "lanes, not guardrails" — giving engineers creative freedom within defined problem boundaries rather than specifying exactly what to build. The teams that figure this out will ship fewer products but better ones; the rest will drown in demos.
