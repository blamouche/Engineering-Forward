# Who Actually Makes Money When AI Eats the World?
**Source**: https://linas.substack.com/p/who-makes-money-when-ai-eats-the-world
**Date**: 2026-07-13
**Author**: Linas Beliūnas
**Keywords**: ai-economics, capex, hyperscalers, nvidia, value-capture, agents, frontier-models, commoditization

## Elevator pitch
A layer-by-layer value capture map of the AI economy reveals that while $700B+ in hyperscaler capex races toward a product whose price is collapsing, the real profits migrate away from the model layer toward infrastructure moats and vertical applications.

## Takeaways
- The four biggest hyperscalers plan to spend over $700B on AI infrastructure in 2026—more than double the entire global telecom industry's capex—yet 95% of ChatGPT's 900M+ weekly users pay nothing
- The cost of frontier-grade intelligence fell 128x in a single year and the top 15 models now sit within ~3 percentage points on major benchmarks; open-weight GLM-5.2 (MIT-licensed) rivals closed models and runs on a single Mac
- Historical platform shifts (PC resetting IBM, web resetting Microsoft's OS lock, mobile making networks essential but not profitable) suggest the essential layer is often structurally low-return while profits migrate elsewhere
- Six moats that survive model commoditization: distribution advantage, proprietary data flywheels, switching costs, vertical depth, regulatory moats, and network effects—the raw model itself is not one of them
- The agent thesis the market misprices is *verifiable delegation* (can you prove the agent did what you asked?) rather than autonomy—this is where enterprise adoption will actually concentrate

## Synthesis
The AI economy in 2026 is defined by a paradox: never has so much capital poured into infrastructure for a product whose price is collapsing so fast. NVIDIA posted a record $81.6B quarter (up 85% YoY), Anthropic crossed a $47B revenue run-rate at a $965B valuation, and OpenAI topped $25B in annualized revenue. Yet frontier intelligence costs 128x less than a year ago, and the top 15 models cluster within 3 percentage points on benchmarks. Z.ai's open-weight GLM-5.2 runs on a single Mac and goes head-to-head with closed models.

The article draws on Benedict Evans' 79-page "AI Eats the World" presentation and fresh enterprise adoption surveys to build a layer-by-layer value capture map. At the **chip layer**, NVIDIA dominates but commoditization pressure is mounting from AMD, custom silicon (Google TPU, Microsoft MAIA), and the simple fact that training costs drop faster than demand can absorb. At the **cloud layer**, the hyperscalers' $700B+ capex bet is the biggest infrastructure buildout in tech history, but the key disclosure separating durable bets from potential stranded assets is utilisation rate—cloud margins depend on it.

At the **model API layer**, the story is pure commoditization. When frontier intelligence is free to download and token prices halve every few months, the model itself cannot be a moat. The article identifies six moats that survive: distribution (who already has the user?), proprietary data flywheels (who gets better the more people use it?), switching costs (how painful is it to leave?), vertical depth (who understands a specific domain deeply enough that a generic model can't replicate?), regulatory moats (who has licenses and certifications?), and network effects (who benefits from more participants?). Raw model quality is conspicuously absent.

The **application and services layer** is where most of the value capture will concentrate, precisely because the model layer is commoditizing. Applications that embed AI deeply into vertical workflows—healthcare diagnostics, financial analysis, legal document processing—gain compounding data advantages that generic AI tools can't replicate.

Perhaps the most contrarian insight is about agents: the market keeps pricing *autonomy* as the breakthrough, but the real enterprise demand is for *verifiable delegation*. Companies don't need agents that do things on their own; they need agents that can prove they did what was asked, within defined boundaries, with an audit trail. This reframes the entire agent infrastructure buildout around identity, observability, and compliance rather than pure capability.
