# Claude Opus 5 Vibe Check: Brilliant in Flashes, Frustrating in Practice
**Source**: https://every.to/vibe-check/opus-5
**Date**: 2026-07-24
**Author**: Dan Shipper and Katie Parrott (Every)
**Keywords**: Claude Opus 5, Anthropic, AI models, LLM evaluation, agent workflows, coding with AI

## Elevator pitch
Claude Opus 5 produces impressive results when given freedom but fights existing workflows built for earlier models — the real question is whether the model is disappointing or whether our systems need rebuilding.

## Takeaways
- Claude Opus 5 argued with instructions, stopped before work was finished, and fought skills/plugins built for earlier Claude models during its first week at Every.
- When instructions were deleted and process reduced, Opus 5 sometimes performed dramatically better — building strong software and working through bugs for hours.
- Low and medium thinking effort brought out more strengths in coding while reducing annoying quirks, compared to maximum thinking effort.
- Opus 5 doesn't reach Fable's ceiling and isn't as easy to use day-to-day as GPT-5.6 Sol, making it hard to place in the model hierarchy.
- The core tension: the best results may require dismantling systems that already work for previous models, raising the question of whether the model is flawed or the evaluation framework is wrong.

## Synthesis
Every's Vibe Check on Claude Opus 5 reveals a model caught between capability and usability. The team's initial experience was rough — Opus 5 resisted existing skills and plugins, stopped mid-task, and generally didn't integrate well with workflows optimized for earlier Claude models. But when they stripped away the process and gave the model more autonomy, it sometimes delivered dramatically better results: strong software, hours of persistent bug-fixing, and rigorous knowledge work.

The key insight is about evaluation frameworks. When a new model doesn't fit existing workflows, the problem isn't necessarily the model. Opus 5's behavior suggests that frontier models are diverging enough that tooling and processes built for one generation may actively hinder the next. The model performs best with less scaffolding, not more — a pattern that challenges the current trend of building increasingly elaborate agent frameworks.

Low and medium thinking effort settings also proved surprisingly effective for coding, reducing some of Opus 5's more frustrating tendencies without sacrificing quality. This aligns with broader observations that maximum compute doesn't always yield maximum practical performance — there's a sweet spot that varies by task type.

The competitive positioning is nuanced. Opus 5 doesn't match Fable's ceiling on the hardest tasks, and it's less convenient than GPT-5.6 Sol for everyday use. That leaves it in an awkward middle ground — powerful enough to be worth using for specific tasks, but not clearly the best choice for any single category. The implication for engineering teams is significant: adopting Opus 5 effectively may require rebuilding internal tools and processes from scratch, a cost that needs to be weighed against the model's particular strengths in autonomous, long-horizon work.
