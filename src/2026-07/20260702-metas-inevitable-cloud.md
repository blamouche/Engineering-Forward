# Meta's Inevitable Cloud
**Source**: https://spyglass.org/meta-cloud/
**Date**: 2026-07-02
**Author**: MG Siegler (Spyglass)
**Keywords**: Meta, cloud computing, AI infrastructure, neocloud, business diversification, enterprise sales, compute monetization, Wall Street

## Elevator pitch
Meta's two problems — over-reliance on advertising and enormous AI capex without direct monetization — have a single solution: a cloud business, and the company is finally moving toward launching one.

## Takeaways
- Meta's business is still almost entirely advertising-based despite years of diversification attempts (Workplace, smartglasses, the Metaverse rebrand), making it uniquely vulnerable among Big Tech peers who sell AI and cloud services directly.
- Wall Street has been punishing Meta's AI capex (estimated $140B/year) because unlike Amazon, Google, and Microsoft, Meta lacks an obvious direct path to monetize AI capabilities — ads are indirect.
- The Manus acquisition (later blocked by China) hinted at Meta's enterprise ambitions; the formation of "Meta Compute" and the Muse Spark API model sales are further steps toward a cloud offering.
- Google's cloud journey — taking years and requiring Thomas Kurian from Oracle to build enterprise sales muscles — is the precedent: a one-trick pony that successfully diversified, now approaching $100B/year in cloud revenue.
- Meta's stock shot up nearly 10% on the cloud news, suggesting investors see this as the missing piece that justifies the AI build-out spend.

## Synthesis
MG Siegler's analysis of Meta's inevitable move into cloud computing connects two problems that the company has been struggling with independently. The first is business diversification: despite changing the company name to Meta, building smartglasses, attempting enterprise software with Workplace, and betting on the Metaverse, the revenue mix remains overwhelmingly advertising-dependent. The second is AI capex justification: Meta is spending an estimated $140 billion annually on AI infrastructure, and Wall Street has been brutal in its assessment because — unlike Amazon, Google, and Microsoft — Meta has no direct way to sell AI capabilities. Ads are the monetization path, but it's indirect, and investors can't see the ROI clearly enough.

The solution to both problems is the same: Meta needs a cloud business. Siegler has been tracking this trajectory for months, noting signals along the way. The Manus acquisition (later reversed under pressure from China) was the first hint — Manus was already selling agentic workflows to enterprises, giving Meta a potential toehold in B2B sales. The formation of "Meta Compute" as a formal AI infrastructure initiative was the second signal. The rollout of Muse Spark models with API access was the third — you don't spend $140B a year for table stakes, and selling model access via API is a natural first step toward a broader cloud offering.

The Google precedent is the key historical analogy. Google was once dismissed as a one-trick pony — an ads business that couldn't diversify. It took years, multiple pivots, and bringing in Thomas Kurian from Oracle to build the enterprise sales muscle that Google Cloud needed. Now cloud is approaching $100B/year in revenue, nearly 20% of Google's overall revenue, and no one calls Google a one-trick pony anymore. Siegler's point is that Meta hasn't tried the one diversification strategy that demonstrably worked for Google.

The competitive landscape is challenging but not prohibitive. Meta wouldn't need to replicate the full AWS/Azure/GCP stack. A "neocloud" offering — selling AI compute and models via APIs, potentially including third-party models like DeepSeek — could be a meaningful start. Siegler suggests Meta could augment with other cloud services over time, potentially reaching double-digit revenue percentage from cloud. The comparison to xAI's Colossus data center and Elon Musk's compute strategy provides additional context: the AI infrastructure build-out is creating a new category of compute providers, and Meta has the scale to participate.

The market's reaction — a 10% stock jump — validates the strategic logic. Investors have been waiting for a credible monetization story to justify Meta's unprecedented capex. A cloud business provides that story: it's a direct revenue stream from AI infrastructure, it diversifies away from ads, and it puts Meta in the same competitive frame as its Big Tech peers. Whether Meta can execute — given its mixed track record on diversification — remains the open question, but the strategic inevitability is clear.
