# Tech Jobs Market in 2026, Part 3: Hiring Managers and Job Seekers
**Source**: https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/tech-jobs-market-in-2026-part-3-hiring
**Date**: 2026-07-07
**Author**: Gergely Orosz (The Pragmatic Engineer)
**Keywords**: tech hiring, job market, AI engineering, forward deployed engineers, hiring managers, fake candidates, referrals

## Elevator pitch
The 2026 tech job market is defined by a paradox: hiring managers can't find experienced talent while experienced engineers can't get interviews, with AI-driven resume spam, fake candidates, and a bifurcated market where AI-adjacent roles are red-hot and everything else is painfully slow.

## Takeaways
- **Catch-22**: Companies receive 800+ applications per role but can't find qualified candidates—AI-generated spam and fake applicants have made inbound hiring nearly useless for many companies
- **No trust**: AI-enhanced resumes make every candidate look impressive on paper, but interviews reveal fundamental gaps; cover letters are effectively dead since they're always AI-generated
- **AI engineering is on fire**: ML/AI/FDE roles see 2-3 recruiter messages daily, while non-AI engineers struggle to get callbacks—creating a "tale of two cities" market
- **Referrals are everything**: Both hiring managers and job seekers report that the only reliable path to interviews is through personal networks; cold applications are largely a black hole
- **Higher bar, lower comp**: Many companies have raised hiring bars while lowering compensation, except for AI engineering roles and AI-native businesses which still command premium offers

## Synthesis
Gergely Orosz's third installment in the Pragmatic Engineer's 2026 tech hiring series paints a picture of a market that can only be described as "weird." Based on interviews with 50+ hiring managers and job seekers, the data reveals a market in structural dysfunction rather than simple contraction.

The central paradox is striking: hiring managers report receiving hundreds of applications per role while simultaneously claiming they can't find qualified talent. The explanation lies in signal-to-noise ratio collapse. AI tools have made it trivial to generate polished, keyword-optimized applications at scale, with one hiring manager receiving 1,000 applications per day of which "maybe two are even relevant to the posting." Several companies have abandoned inbound hiring entirely.

The trust deficit extends beyond resumes. Multiple hiring managers reported candidates who appear genuine during screening but turn out to be entirely fake—sometimes different people showing up for interviews than submitted the application, sometimes candidates clearly reading AI-generated responses in real-time. This has created a vicious cycle: companies don't trust inbound applications, so they rely on networks; network hiring favors the already-connected, which disadvantages capable people without connections.

The market is sharply bifurcated. AI engineering, ML, and forward deployed engineering (FDE) roles see explosive demand—an Apple engineer reported getting significant pay bumps for AI infra roles. Meanwhile, generalist software engineers face what many describe as a "desert." The 10x engineer profile is now explicitly an AI-adjacent engineer with a strong network, not a generalist coding wizard.

Compensation tells the same story in reverse. Non-AI roles are seeing offers at multi-year lows, while AI engineering positions maintain or exceed 2024 levels. The EM and Staff+ market is particularly strange: these profiles are simultaneously the hardest to fill and the hardest to get hired for, suggesting a matching problem rather than a supply or demand problem alone.
