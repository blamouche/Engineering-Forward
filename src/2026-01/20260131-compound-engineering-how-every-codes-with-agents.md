## Elevator pitch

Every introduces "compound engineering," a four-step methodology where AI agents write 100% of the code while developers focus on planning, review, and building institutional knowledge that compounds over time.

## Takeaways

- Planning now consumes 80% of developer time, with agents executing implementation based on detailed research and specifications
- Single developers maintain production software serving thousands of daily users, achieving output previously requiring five developers
- The "compound" step captures lessons from bugs and design patterns into institutional knowledge that primes future agents
- Knowledge compounding makes subsequent features easier to build as the AI's understanding grows with each iteration
- Tools like Claude Code combined with model context protocols enable agents to test as they build through simulated user interactions

## Synthesis

Dan Shipper and Kieran Klaassen of Every present a comprehensive methodology for software development in an age where AI agents handle all code generation. Their framework, dubbed "compound engineering," represents a fundamental rethinking of how engineering teams should structure their workflows when typing code is no longer the bottleneck.

The methodology operates as a four-step loop. Planning comes first and now consumes the majority of developer time. Rather than jumping straight to implementation, agents conduct extensive research across existing codebases, commit histories, and internet resources to understand architectural patterns and best practices. This research phase produces detailed implementation plans that document objectives, proposed architecture, specific code approaches, and success criteria. The shift is significant: planning moves from a preliminary step to the core activity of engineering work.

The second phase is execution, where agents implement the plan step by step. The integration of model context protocols like Playwright and XcodeBuildMCP allows agents to test as they build, simulating real user interactions and iterating until functionality meets specifications. Human developers observe and course-correct rather than typing line by line.

Review combines automated and human evaluation. Linters, unit tests, and specialized code review agents examine security, performance, and complexity simultaneously. The parallelization of review perspectives that would have required multiple senior engineers now happens through agent orchestration.

The final step is what the authors call "the money step"—compounding. Every successful implementation, every bug fix, every architectural decision gets captured as institutional knowledge. Automated systems document these findings in formats that prime future agents. New team members inherit accumulated organizational knowledge immediately, without extended onboarding. The codebase itself becomes a repository of lessons learned.

The results at Every are striking. The company operates five production software products, each maintained by a single developer supporting thousands of daily users. They estimate individual developers now accomplish work equivalent to five developers from just a few years ago. This is not modest efficiency gain but a categorical shift in what a small engineering team can build and maintain.

The broader implications extend beyond productivity metrics. Traditional constraints begin to dissolve. Expensive replatforming projects become feasible when agents can rapidly understand and rewrite legacy systems. Engineering hiring can focus less on rote coding ability and more on architectural thinking and product judgment. The developers who thrive in this environment are orchestrators and strategists, not typists.

The methodology crystallizes practices that many teams have been discovering independently. By naming and systematizing the approach, Every provides a template for organizations navigating the transition to agent-assisted development. The emphasis on the compound step—on building institutional knowledge rather than just shipping features—distinguishes this framework from simpler visions of AI as a productivity tool.
