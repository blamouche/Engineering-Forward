# AI May Be Everywhere, But It's Nowhere in Recent Productivity Statistics

**Source**: https://www.theregister.com/2026/01/15/forrester_ai_jobs_impact/

**Date**: January 15, 2026

**Author**: O'Ryan Johnson

**Keywords**: AI, productivity paradox, Forrester Research, job displacement, automation, ROI, labor market

## Elevator pitch

Despite AI's widespread adoption, productivity statistics remain flat, echoing the PC revolution's failure to show measurable economic gains while Forrester estimates AI could still displace 6% of U.S. jobs by 2030.

## Takeaways

- Historical productivity growth has declined from 2.7% annually (1947-1973) to 1.5% (2007-2019), with AI yet to reverse this trend
- Forrester estimates AI could displace approximately 10.4 million U.S. jobs (6% of the workforce) by 2030
- 95% of generative AI projects lack tangible ROI according to MIT research, while McKinsey reports 80%+ of AI initiatives fail to deliver returns
- Many recent layoffs are "financial decisions masquerading as AI job loss"—companies freezing hiring pending AI implementation rather than actively replacing workers
- Jobs lost to automation historically don't return, unlike positions lost in traditional economic downturns

## Synthesis

Forrester Vice President J.P. Gownder presents a sobering analysis of AI's economic impact, invoking economist Robert Solow's famous productivity paradox: "You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics." This observation, originally made about the PC revolution, appears to be repeating itself with artificial intelligence. Despite the technology's ubiquitous presence in business operations, traditional productivity metrics stubbornly refuse to reflect meaningful improvement.

The numbers paint a concerning picture. U.S. productivity growth has been on a long-term decline, falling from 2.7% annually during the post-war boom (1947-1973) to just 1.5% in the pre-pandemic period (2007-2019). The current wave of AI adoption has yet to reverse this trajectory, raising questions about whether the technology's transformative potential is being overstated or simply taking longer to materialize than anticipated.

The disconnect between AI hype and measurable results is particularly stark in enterprise adoption. MIT research indicates that a stunning 95% of generative AI projects fail to deliver tangible return on investment, while McKinsey's findings are only marginally more optimistic, with over 80% of AI initiatives failing to generate returns. These statistics suggest that despite significant investment, most organizations are struggling to translate AI capabilities into business value.

Gownder draws an important distinction in the labor market narrative. While Forrester projects that AI could displace approximately 10.4 million U.S. jobs by 2030 (roughly 6% of the workforce), many current layoffs attributed to AI are actually "financial decisions masquerading as AI job loss." Companies are freezing hiring in anticipation of AI capabilities rather than actively replacing workers with functioning AI systems. This nuance matters because it reveals that the real AI workforce transformation may still be ahead of us.

The most concerning aspect of automation-driven job displacement, according to Gownder, is its permanence. Unlike traditional economic downturns where jobs eventually return, positions eliminated by automation tend to disappear for good. The Forrester analysis, based on examination of 800+ job categories and consultation with 200 organizations, suggests we should prepare for structural rather than cyclical employment changes as AI matures.
