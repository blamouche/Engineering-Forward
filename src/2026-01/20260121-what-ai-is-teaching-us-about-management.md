# What AI Is Teaching Us About Management

**Source**: https://every.to/also-true-for-humans/what-ai-is-teaching-us-about-management

**Date**: January 20, 2026

**Author**: Mike Taylor

**Keywords**: AI management, prompting, delegation, scientific management, New Taylorism, leadership skills

## Elevator pitch

Working with AI agents provides low-stakes practice in management fundamentals because the skills that make AI reliable—clear direction, explicit formats, examples, evaluation, and task decomposition—are identical to those that make human teams effective.

## Takeaways

- AI provides a low-stakes training ground for management skills since models don't hold grudges or get offended by unclear instructions
- Five prompting principles apply equally to human management: clear direction, specified format, quality examples, systematic evaluation, and sequential task breakdown
- Frederick Taylor's scientific management failed with humans who resented mechanical treatment but succeeds with AI that has no such objections
- As technical prompting aspects become automated, the skill will shift from computer science to business domains
- The convergence of AI engineering and management practices represents a new discipline that Taylor calls "New Taylorism"

## Synthesis

Mike Taylor proposes what he calls "New Taylorism"—a framework recognizing the convergence between AI engineering practices and effective management. His central insight is that the skills making AI agents reliable are identical to those making human teams effective. This observation reframes prompting not as a technical skill but as a management discipline in disguise.

Taylor argues that working with AI provides an ideal training ground for learning management fundamentals. Unlike human employees, AI models do not hold grudges or become offended by unclear instructions. This creates a low-stakes environment where professionals can practice delegation, learn to provide clear direction, and develop systematic evaluation approaches without the interpersonal consequences of making mistakes. The feedback loop is immediate and the cost of iteration is minimal.

The framework centers on five prompting principles that apply with equal force to managing humans. First, give clear direction with specific style references rather than vague guidance. Second, specify the desired format explicitly instead of leaving output structure ambiguous. Third, provide examples of quality work so the recipient understands the standard being asked for. Fourth, evaluate and measure results systematically rather than relying on subjective impressions. Fifth, divide complex goals into sequential steps that can be completed and verified independently.

Taylor draws an interesting historical connection to Frederick Winslow Taylor's scientific management movement from the 1880s. The original Taylorism attempted to optimize human work through systematic measurement and standardization. It ultimately failed because humans resented being treated as mechanical components in a system. Workers pushed back against the dehumanizing aspects of pure efficiency optimization. With AI, these objections disappear. Models have no feelings to hurt, no dignity to preserve, and no resentment to accumulate. The mechanical approach that failed with humans succeeds with AI precisely because AI lacks the human qualities that made scientific management unpalatable.

Looking forward, Taylor predicts that prompting will increasingly belong in business schools rather than computer science departments. As the technical aspects of working with AI become automated—models improving their own instructions, interfaces becoming more intuitive—what remains is fundamentally about task definition and quality evaluation. These are management skills, not programming skills. The professionals who excel at prompting in the future will be those who understand how to specify desired outcomes, evaluate results against standards, and decompose complex objectives into achievable steps.

The implications extend beyond individual skill development. Organizations that recognize this convergence can use AI projects as management training programs, developing leadership capabilities while simultaneously building AI solutions. The artificial separation between technical AI work and people management may dissolve as both domains prove to require the same underlying competencies.
