# The Future of 10x Engineering

**Source**: https://www.natemeyvis.com/the-future-of-10x-engineering/

**Date**: January 29, 2026

**Author**: Nate Meyvis

**Keywords**: generative AI, 10x engineers, productivity, software engineering, AI tools, engineering skills, career

## Elevator pitch

The emergence of generative AI will likely widen the productivity gap between elite engineers and median performers, while potentially eliminating lower-performing roles entirely.

## Takeaways

- The existence of 10x engineers is a real phenomenon, not a myth, with some engineers being substantially more productive than median performers
- AI tools will improve output for top engineers relative to average ones, but could eliminate lower-performing roles entirely
- At the 95th-99th percentile, AI might actually narrow productivity gaps by helping all excellent engineers avoid common pitfalls
- At the 99.9th percentile, gaps could widen as the best engineers leverage AI for architectural improvements and design quality
- Future top engineers may need different skills than current ones, with stamina and work ethic becoming increasingly valuable

## Synthesis

Nate Meyvis responds to Andrej Karpathy's observations about how artificial intelligence might transform productivity disparities in software engineering. The piece grapples with a question that has become increasingly urgent as AI coding assistants become more capable: will AI narrow or widen the gap between top-tier and median engineers?

Meyvis begins by reaffirming his belief that 10x engineers genuinely exist. This is not a universal position in the industry—skeptics have long argued that such productivity multipliers are myths or artifacts of poor measurement. But Meyvis finds the evidence compelling that some engineers consistently deliver output many times greater than their median peers.

The central thesis is that generative AI will likely amplify existing productivity differences rather than level the playing field. When AI tools help everyone code faster, those who were already exceptional gain proportionally more. The author suggests this could have stark consequences for the lower end of the distribution: roles that exist primarily to handle routine coding tasks may simply disappear.

An interesting nuance emerges when comparing different tiers of excellence. At the 95th to 99th percentile of engineering talent, AI tools might actually narrow gaps. These excellent-but-not-elite engineers often stumble on the same kinds of problems—bugs, architectural missteps, unfamiliar domains. AI assistants can catch these issues for everyone, reducing the variance among high performers.

But at the very top—the 99.9th percentile—Meyvis expects gaps to widen. The best engineers will use AI not just to write code faster, but to maintain cleaner architectures and prevent design mistakes. This capability receives less attention in discussions of AI coding tools, which tend to focus on rapid prototyping and feature generation. The architectural benefits may prove more consequential.

Perhaps most provocatively, Meyvis suggests that the skills defining top engineers will shift. Technical brilliance alone may matter less than the ability to sustain long periods of focused work. Stamina and work ethic could become differentiators as AI handles more of the cognitively demanding but routine aspects of programming.

The piece offers a measured view of AI's impact on engineering careers. Neither utopian nor dystopian, it suggests that AI will accelerate existing trends toward winner-take-all dynamics in technical talent markets. Engineers in the middle of the distribution face the most uncertainty about their future value.
