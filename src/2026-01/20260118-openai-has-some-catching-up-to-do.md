**Keywords**: OpenAI, Claude Code, Opus 4.5, Anthropic, Codex, developer tools, agent-native development, vibe coding, AI competition

## Elevator pitch

Claude Code and Anthropic's Opus 4.5 have displaced OpenAI's offerings as the daily driver for most startup founders and developers, forcing OpenAI to play catch-up in the agentic coding tool space.

## Takeaways

- At a recent founder dinner, nearly all programmer attendees cited Claude Code as their primary coding tool, with only one preferring OpenAI's Codex
- Just a year ago, the same group would have predominantly used GPT models, marking a dramatic shift in developer preferences
- Anthropic's "terminal-first" design bypassed traditional code editors, signaling commitment to an agent-native paradigm
- OpenAI's Codex CLI (April 2025) and Codex Web (May 2025) attempted to match Anthropic but faced limitations—CLI lacked access to the most powerful models, Web ran in a sandboxed virtual environment
- The shift represents a strategic win for Anthropic's vision of AI-first development workflows

## Synthesis

Dan Shipper's analysis presents a striking reversal in the competitive landscape of AI coding tools. OpenAI, long the dominant player in AI-assisted development through GPT models, now finds itself playing catch-up to Anthropic's Claude Code. The evidence is anecdotal but compelling: at a dinner with 20 startup founders, nearly all programmers in attendance named Claude Code as their primary development tool, with only a single holdout preferring OpenAI's Codex.

This shift becomes more striking when considering the timeline. Just one year prior, Shipper notes, the same group would have overwhelmingly relied on GPT models for coding assistance. The speed of this transition reflects both Anthropic's successful product execution and OpenAI's struggle to adapt its offerings to new developer workflows.

The strategic divergence between the two companies is instructive. Anthropic adopted a "terminal-first" design philosophy with Claude Code, deliberately bypassing traditional code editors to signal commitment to an agent-native development paradigm. This approach resonated with developers who were ready to fundamentally rethink their workflows rather than simply augment existing tools.

OpenAI's response came in stages: Codex CLI launched in April 2025, followed by Codex Web in May 2025. However, both products faced meaningful limitations. The CLI version lacked access to OpenAI's most capable models, while the Web version operated in a sandboxed virtual environment rather than directly on users' machines. These constraints may have seemed prudent from a safety perspective but limited the products' appeal to developers seeking the most powerful and flexible tools available.

Shipper's experience hitting usage limits on Codex while building a Markdown editor underscores a practical dimension of competition: even when capability is comparable, the user experience of quotas and restrictions can drive developers toward alternatives. The lesson for AI companies is clear—in a rapidly evolving market, being second with meaningful product constraints can mean ceding significant ground to competitors who move faster with fewer barriers.
