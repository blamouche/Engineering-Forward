# IA: le risque silencieux de la pensee standardisee

**Source**: https://www.journaldunet.com/intelligence-artificielle/1547465-ia-le-risque-silencieux-de-la-pensee-standardisee/

**Date**: January 26, 2026

**Author**: Julien Ricciarelli-Bonnal

**Keywords**: AI standardization, cognitive delegation, intellectual differentiation, homogenization, strategic thinking, competitive advantage

## Elevator pitch

Widespread AI adoption risks homogenizing professional thinking, as algorithms amplify existing ideas rather than generating novel ones, making human intellectual singularity more valuable than ever.

## Takeaways

- AI tools amplify existing ideas rather than generating novel ones, creating interchangeable outputs across industries and organizations
- Cognitive delegation to algorithms weakens independent reasoning capacity as users outsource initial thinking stages before conceptualizing problems
- Competitive advantage in saturated markets shifts from tool mastery to maintaining singular intellectual perspectives
- Strategic thinking, not creative resistance, counters the homogenization trend
- Human value lies in interpretation, context-sensitivity, and reasoned judgment that algorithms cannot replicate

## Synthesis

Julien Ricciarelli-Bonnal presents a measured critique of AI's impact on professional thought processes. The central thesis is encapsulated in a simple observation: "La machine ne pense pas mal. Elle pense comme tout le monde"—the machine does not think poorly, it thinks like everyone else. This framing shifts the AI discourse from questions of capability to questions of conformity.

The article identifies intellectual standardization as the primary concern. When organizations across industries rely on the same AI tools for strategic planning, marketing, and communication, their outputs converge. Corporate messaging becomes interchangeable. Marketing strategies follow identical templates. The algorithms, trained on vast corpora of existing content, excel at synthesizing and restructuring what already exists rather than generating genuinely novel approaches. Innovation becomes minoritized while standardized outputs become the default.

A second concern involves cognitive delegation. Users increasingly outsource the initial stages of thinking to algorithms—requesting structures before conceptualizing their own frameworks, seeking solutions before properly analyzing problems. This pattern, repeated across the workforce, gradually weakens independent reasoning capacity. The convenience of AI assistance creates dependency that atrophies the muscles of original thought.

The market implications follow logically. In environments where competitors use identical tools to produce similar outputs, differentiation cannot come from tool mastery. The competitive advantage shifts to professionals who maintain what the author calls "intellectual singularity"—perspectives, interpretations, and judgments that machines cannot manufacture. This positions human cognitive distinctiveness not as an anachronism but as a scarce resource.

The article avoids the trap of recommending AI rejection. Instead, it proposes strategic engagement: using these tools to accelerate work in understood domains while preserving space for independent thought in areas requiring genuine insight. The balance lies in recognizing what algorithms do well—synthesis, pattern matching, structure—while maintaining human ownership of interpretation, contextual judgment, and reasoned analysis.

The conclusion frames human thought as having never been more valuable precisely because AI commoditizes mediocrity. Those who rely entirely on algorithmic assistance produce work indistinguishable from everyone else. Those who maintain independent thinking capacity while leveraging AI appropriately create genuine differentiation. The article serves as a reminder that AI adoption strategy involves more than tool selection—it requires deliberate choices about which cognitive functions to delegate and which to preserve.
