# Why People Create AI Workslop—and How to Stop It

**Source**: https://hbr.org/2026/01/why-people-create-ai-workslop-and-how-to-stop-it

**Date**: January 16, 2026

**Author**: Kate Niederhoffer, Alexi Robichaux, Jeffrey T. Hancock

**Keywords**: AI workslop, generative AI, workplace productivity, management, organizational trust, AI adoption, employee burnout

## Elevator pitch

Research reveals that over half of employees admit sending low-quality AI-generated work to colleagues, a phenomenon called "workslop" that stems from unclear AI mandates and overwhelmed teams rather than individual laziness.

## Takeaways

- Workslop refers to low-effort AI-generated work that appears polished but shifts cognitive burden to recipients rather than solving problems
- 41% of surveyed employees received unclear AI implementation guidance while leadership faces pressure to demonstrate AI ROI quickly
- 53% of respondents admitted sending substandard AI work occasionally, with 10% reporting that over half their AI submissions were unhelpful
- Team trust reduced workslop incidents by 61%, while AI competence made employees 50% less likely to create workslop
- Organizational damage manifests through relationship deterioration, with engineers resigning and employees losing commitment to their organizations

## Synthesis

Harvard Business Review presents research on "workslop"—low-effort AI-generated work that appears professionally polished but wastes recipients' time by transferring cognitive burden rather than solving problems. The term captures a specific failure mode in AI-assisted workplaces where output quantity increases while value decreases.

The research surveyed 1,150 employees and found troubling patterns. Forty-one percent recalled receiving workslop that negatively affected their work, while 53% admitted to sending substandard AI work to colleagues. Ten percent reported that over half of their AI submissions were unhelpful. These numbers suggest workslop has become normalized rather than exceptional.

The authors locate the root cause in management failure rather than individual behavior. Leadership faces board pressure to demonstrate AI return on investment quickly. Boards view AI as a solution to productivity challenges, leading to vague directives like "use AI everywhere, every day." Employees receive unclear guidance while simultaneously experiencing budget cuts, role consolidation, and increased task loads without formal workflow redesign. The combination creates a pressure cooker where workslop becomes a survival strategy.

The organizational damage extends beyond wasted time. The authors emphasize relationship deterioration through specific examples: engineers resenting colleagues and resigning, researchers feeling violated when receiving AI-generated responses to substantive questions, employees losing hope for promotion and commitment to their organizations. These outcomes suggest workslop corrodes the social fabric that enables collaboration.

Two factors significantly reduced workslop incidence. Team trust lowered incidents by 61%, suggesting psychological safety allows employees to invest appropriate effort rather than satisficing with AI output. AI competence made employees 50% less likely to create workslop, indicating that skill with the tools correlates with quality judgment about when and how to use them.

The solutions framework operates at three levels. Culture requires rebuilding trust through feedback, dialogue, and collaborative practices. Practice demands clear AI usage norms with explicit human judgment review processes. Accountability involves introducing "AI collaboration architects" who bridge technology implementation and human relationships. The concluding insight frames the challenge not as a technology problem but a human one: "To make AI work at work, we need to get better at being human." Success requires enabling agency and trust rather than imposing mandates on depleted teams.
