# LLMs and Your Career

**Source**: https://notes.eatonphil.com/2026-01-19-llms-and-your-career.html

**Date**: January 19, 2026

**Author**: Phil Eaton

**Keywords**: LLMs, software career, developer skills, fundamentals, systems programming, professional development

## Elevator pitch

Phil Eaton argues that coding via LLM is not fundamentally different from using Rails or Stack Overflow—competitive developers have always combined pragmatism with curiosity about underlying systems, and that advantage persists.

## Takeaways

- Coding with LLMs is another form of adapting existing solutions rather than building from first principles, similar to past productivity tools
- Developers who treated all code as impenetrable black boxes were already at a disadvantage before LLMs arrived
- Software's trajectory continuously reduces hiring needs for standard problem-solving while complexity creates demand for fundamentally-skilled developers
- Companies operating at scale or building infrastructure will persistently value developers who understand core principles
- Interesting software development jobs will remain available to developers who maintain continuous learning focused on foundational concepts

## Synthesis

Phil Eaton presents a pragmatic framework for software developers concerned about AI's impact on their careers. His central argument is that coding via LLM is not fundamentally different from coding with Rails or perusing Stack Overflow. In all these cases, developers adapt existing solutions rather than building everything from first principles. The tools change, but the underlying dynamic remains constant.

This perspective reframes the anxiety around AI coding assistants. Eaton observes that competitive developers have always combined pragmatism—using whatever tools accelerate their work—with genuine curiosity about underlying systems. Those who treated all code as impenetrable black boxes, copying solutions without understanding them, were already at a disadvantage in the job market before LLMs arrived. AI assistants do not change this fundamental dynamic; they merely make the distinction between these two developer types more visible.

Eaton points to historical patterns in software development. The field has continuously reduced hiring needs for standard problem-solving tasks. Each wave of abstraction—high-level languages, frameworks, libraries, cloud services—has automated away work that previously required specialized knowledge. Yet complexity always emerges elsewhere, creating demand for developers who understand foundational concepts. The specific skills in demand shift, but the need for deep understanding persists.

The author identifies two categories of work that will continue to value fundamental expertise. Companies operating at significant scale face unique challenges that generic solutions cannot address. Their systems must handle edge cases, performance requirements, and reliability demands that push beyond what off-the-shelf approaches can manage. Similarly, those building infrastructure—databases, compilers, operating systems, networking stacks—work in domains where understanding core principles is not optional. These roles require developers who can reason about systems at a level that LLMs cannot replicate.

Eaton's career advice follows from this analysis. Rather than viewing LLMs as threatening, developers should deepen their knowledge in systems fundamentals. Seek roles where this expertise creates competitive advantage. The developers who thrive will be those who use AI tools to accelerate routine work while investing the time savings into understanding why systems behave as they do.

The conclusion is measured rather than dismissive of concerns. Interesting software development jobs going away remains unlikely, but only for developers who maintain continuous learning habits focused on foundational concepts. The passive consumption of AI-generated code without curiosity leads nowhere. Active engagement with the underlying principles—regardless of what tools generate the initial code—remains the path to career durability.
