# AI and My Crisis of Meaning
**Source**: https://brids.bearblog.dev/ai-and-my-crisis-of-meaning/
**Date**: 2026-02-24
**Author**: Unknown
**Keywords**: AI, software engineering, freelancing, productivity, career anxiety

## Elevator pitch
An independent developer describes how AI coding tools made custom software absurdly fast to deliver—and why that speed is triggering a personal “meaning” crisis about long-term value, income, and identity.

## Takeaways
- AI coding tools are compressing “weeks of work” into hours, especially for CRUD-style apps and internal tools.
- The short-term opportunity is an information asymmetry: builders who understand agents can arbitrage speed and ship faster than clients.
- That asymmetry feels temporary; the author fears clients will soon self-serve with agents.
- Psychological impact matters: productivity gains can translate into anxiety, not relief, when your value proposition is tied to the old effort model.
- There’s a subtle trust boundary: using AI in high-stakes compliance/audit situations can backfire if it erodes credibility.

## Synthesis
The post is a first-person reflection from a freelancer who left a large SaaS company partly out of fear that AI would hollow out the company’s value and, by extension, his own. Over the following year, he intentionally leaned into AI coding tools (Claude Code, Codex, Gemini CLI, etc.) while building real software for clients. The tools delivered: he shipped multiple projects and even navigated a highly compliant domain with a grueling audit.

But the author’s core point is emotional rather than technical: the more capable AI becomes, the more it destabilizes his sense of purpose. He observes a “November moment” inflection where demand for custom apps is surging because businesses realize they can replace sprawling stacks of generic SaaS with tailored solutions. In theory, this should be a boom time for a freelancer.

Instead, he feels he is living in a fleeting arbitrage window. The most striking anecdote is a 12-week project plan that effectively collapsed into a few hours of work because an agent “one-shotted” the bulk of the implementation. The remaining work is verification and small bug fixes—still valuable, but not aligned with the traditional narrative of craftsmanship and time.

The fear is not simply job loss; it’s that a decade of accumulated skill, which once reliably translated into meaningful work and income, can suddenly feel like it’s “slipping through the cracks.” The author ends with uncertainty: he wants to believe humans will adapt as they always have, but he doesn’t fully trust that story yet. The post reads as an early field report on how AI-driven compression of effort can create a crisis of meaning even for people who are “winning” in the short run.
