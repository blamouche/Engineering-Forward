# You Should Never Go Viral With Your AI App
**Source**: https://every.to/p/you-should-never-go-viral-with-your-ai-app
**Date**: February 27, 2026
**Author**: Victor Stepanov
**Keywords**: growth, product strategy, AI apps, virality, retention

## Elevator pitch
The article warns that AI apps often break when they go viral, arguing for deliberate, slower growth so teams can stabilize infrastructure, improve retention, and avoid reputational damage.

## Takeaways
- Viral spikes can overwhelm infrastructure and degrade user experience.
- Early demand can mask retention and product-market fit problems.
- Rapid growth attracts misuse and increases moderation burdens.
- Sustainable growth requires reliability, onboarding, and clear use cases.
- Teams should design for durability before pursuing distribution.

## Synthesis
The article makes a counterintuitive argument: for AI apps, virality is often a liability rather than a victory. A sudden surge of users can reveal fragility in infrastructure, overwhelm support, and expose unhandled edge cases. For AI products, where reliability and cost control are already challenging, a viral spike can quickly degrade the experience and damage trust.

One issue is that virality can mask the fundamentals. When a product is rapidly shared, the growth curve looks healthy even if users do not stick around. That creates the illusion of product-market fit, while retention and unit economics remain weak. The article argues that teams should focus on cohort quality and repeat usage before turning on growth loops.

Another challenge is risk and misuse. A wider audience increases the chance of adversarial behavior, policy violations, and content moderation failures. AI apps are especially exposed because model outputs can be unpredictable, and abuse can quickly become public. The article suggests that teams should harden their systems and safety policies before exposing the product to large, uncontrolled user inflows.

The recommended strategy is deliberate growth: keep distribution small enough that the team can learn, fix, and iterate. This allows infrastructure to mature, cost controls to be calibrated, and onboarding to be refined. When the product is ready, growth can be accelerated with more confidence. In short, the piece frames virality not as a goal but as a phase that must be earned through stability and retention.

The broader message is that AI products are still fragile compared to mature software. Winning in this space requires not just clever model usage but operational excellence. Teams that resist premature virality and instead build durable foundations are more likely to sustain growth when they eventually scale.
