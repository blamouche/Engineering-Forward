# Empiricists vs. Extrapolators

**Source**: https://www.secondbest.ca/p/empiricists-vs-extrapolators?utm_source=tldrnewsletter&hide_intro_popup=true

**Date**: Feb 18, 2026

**Author**: Samuel Hammond

**Keywords**: AI forecasts, scaling laws, epistemology, uncertainty, policy

## Elevator pitch

Samuel Hammond describes a divide in AI forecasting between empiricists who wait for evidence and extrapolators who project from scaling trends.

## Takeaways

- The “empiricist vs extrapolator” split shapes debates about AI timelines and impact.
- Empiricists emphasize uncertainty and complex-system unpredictability.
- Extrapolators argue that scaling laws make rapid progress forecastable.
- The key disagreement is about how much evidence is enough to act on.
- Institutions move slowly, so forecasting is necessary even when imperfect.

## Synthesis

The essay argues that disagreements about AI timelines are less about data and more about epistemology. Empiricists prioritize observed evidence and emphasize the limits of prediction in complex systems. Extrapolators lean on scaling laws and historical trends, arguing that once progress becomes visible, its trajectory is more predictable than skeptics admit. This divide explains why people looking at the same breakthroughs can reach very different conclusions about urgency.

Hammond suggests that extrapolators see AI progress as analogous to thermodynamics: once you identify stable invariants, you can project forward with some confidence. Empiricists, by contrast, see AI as more like weather forecasting—highly sensitive to initial conditions and therefore resistant to confident prediction. The essay doesn’t dismiss empiricism, but it argues that waiting for perfect evidence is costly because policy and institutional responses lag technology.

The author frames scaling laws as the core evidence base for extrapolators. These laws have held across multiple orders of magnitude, giving credence to the idea that larger models and more data reliably yield better performance. If that pattern continues, then current capabilities imply future milestones sooner than many institutions are prepared for. The essay uses this to justify proactive preparation even if exact timelines remain uncertain.

Ultimately, the piece calls for a pragmatic balance. Forecasting AI is inherently uncertain, but uncertainty doesn’t remove the need to act. The cost of underestimating progress could be severe, so extrapolators view early preparation as rational. Empiricists worry about overreaction or misplaced confidence. The essay suggests that this tension is unavoidable, and that productive debate should focus on how to design policies that are robust to both fast and slow progress scenarios.

The result is a reframing of AI discourse: instead of asking who is right, the question becomes how institutions can build flexible responses in the face of competing epistemologies. The author’s implicit recommendation is to treat scaling trends as serious signals while remaining open to surprises and discontinuities.
