# How will OpenAI compete?
**Source**: https://www.ben-evans.com/benedictevans/2026/2/19/how-will-openai-compete-nkg2x
**Date**: 2026-02-19
**Author**: Benedict Evans
**Keywords**: ai, strategy, business

## Elevator pitch
Benedict Evans argues that OpenAI’s lead is fragile because models are commoditizing, engagement is shallow, and the real leverage will come from new experiences that may be built elsewhere.

## Takeaways
- Frontier model performance is converging across several labs, with no durable lead.
- OpenAI’s user base is enormous but engagement remains shallow and fragile.
- Distribution advantages favor incumbents like Google and Meta over pure AI labs.
- OpenAI’s capex and platform ambitions may secure a seat at the table, not defensible leverage.
- The next wave of value likely comes from new experiences built on top of models, not from chatbots alone.

## Synthesis
Benedict Evans frames OpenAI’s competitive position around four strategic questions. First, the core product lacks a durable competitive advantage. OpenAI has a massive user base, but engagement is narrow and there are no network effects or clear product-market fit beyond the novelty of a chatbot. Second, the market is still evolving rapidly: incumbents and startups are racing to invent new products and business models, and the foundation models themselves are on a path toward commoditization. Third, OpenAI and peers must cross a “messy middle” without existing distribution businesses or steady cashflows, while competing in a capital-intensive industry that favors players with large balance sheets. Fourth, there is a product-strategy tension in AI labs: breakthroughs originate in research, and product teams are often forced to package whatever the lab invents rather than shaping a deliberate roadmap.

Evans argues that the model layer offers no clear, lasting edge. Benchmarks show that half a dozen organizations ship frontier models with broadly comparable capabilities. Leadership leapfrogs every few weeks. There is no known mechanism for one company to achieve a permanent, self-reinforcing lead, unlike Windows, iOS, or Google Search. Any potential network effects—such as continuous learning from proprietary data—are speculative. Proprietary data could matter, but the incumbent platforms already have large user data troves and most vertical data can be purchased or recreated. In this environment, the expectation is convergence, not durable differentiation.

OpenAI’s strongest advantage is its user base, which Evans estimates at hundreds of millions. Yet usage is “a mile wide but an inch deep.” Many users are weekly active at most, only a small fraction pay, and a large share of users send relatively few prompts. That implies that most people do not experience features like memory or nuanced differences between models. This lack of habit undermines stickiness and makes the user base vulnerable to distribution-driven competitors such as Google and Meta, whose chatbots look similar to the typical user and can be bundled into existing products. The analogy is Netscape: early leadership in a largely undifferentiated category can be overturned by incumbents with distribution.

Evans argues that the chatbot UI itself is hard to differentiate. Like browsers in the 1990s, a chat app is essentially an input box and an output box. Product innovation in browsers was incremental, and the critical value shifted to services built on top of the browser rather than in the browser itself. The same pattern could unfold in AI: if model capabilities improve across the board, the real value may come from novel experiences built on top of models, not from incremental UI tweaks in a chatbot. This raises the question of who invents those new experiences and why it would necessarily be OpenAI.

He critiques OpenAI’s recent “everything, all at once” approach—platforms, browser ideas, media, research, and massive capex announcements—as a mix of flooding the zone and attempting to copy the form of past platform successes. The company’s stated strategy resembles a full-stack platform flywheel, from chips and cloud infrastructure up through APIs and apps. Evans is skeptical that this analogy holds. Building infrastructure does not guarantee platform leverage, because customers and end users typically do not care which cloud or model powers a product. Infrastructure oligopolies can exist without creating the developer-user feedback loops that made Windows or iOS defensible.

On capex, he notes that the scale being discussed is unprecedented and may create a small oligopoly of AI infrastructure providers. Yet an oligopoly does not necessarily translate into value capture for OpenAI if downstream experiences and revenue accrue elsewhere. The thrust of his argument is that OpenAI is racing to secure a seat at a table that may be very expensive and only moderately profitable, while the most meaningful opportunities could come from new product categories and distribution advantages that lie outside a standalone AI lab. The outcome hinges less on model superiority and more on discovering durable product and ecosystem leverage.
