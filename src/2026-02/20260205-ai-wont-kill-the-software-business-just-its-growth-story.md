# AI Won’t Kill the Software Business, Just Its Growth Story

**Keywords**: software stocks, AI narrative, market sentiment, growth expectations

## Elevator pitch
WSJ argues that AI may not destroy software companies, but the market’s belief that it will can still compress valuations and punish “software growth” narratives—as seen in a multi-month selloff in software equities.

## Takeaways
- The piece distinguishes business fundamentals from market narratives: damage can come from belief-driven repricing even if software demand persists.
- It frames recent software-stock declines as partly attributable to AI-driven fears (and rotation away from prior growth expectations).
- The IGV Software Index is cited as being down materially from a late-September peak, with an intensified selloff on a recent Tuesday.
- The headline implication: “AI eats software” is a powerful story that can shift capital allocation regardless of near-term revenue realities.
- For operators, this suggests AI strategy is not only product/tech—it’s also investor messaging and expectation management.

## Synthesis
This WSJ piece is primarily about market psychology. Its central argument is that AI doesn’t need to literally “kill” the software business to cause real damage; the *belief* that it will can be enough to drive a repricing of software equities.

The article points to a sustained slide in software stocks over multiple months and highlights a sharper selloff on a particular day, using the IGV Software Index’s drawdown from its late-September peak as a concrete indicator. While the excerpt accessible without full access is short, the framing suggests an investor narrative shift: if AI is expected to commoditize software creation, reduce pricing power, or compress growth rates, then valuations that depended on high growth expectations can fall—even before any company’s revenue line materially changes.

In that sense, the “growth story” is the real casualty. Even if software remains essential and profitable, the market may treat the category as lower-multiple, more competitive, or less defensible under an AI-accelerated future.

For engineering and product leaders, the meta-lesson is that AI impact is not only technical (how you build) but also strategic (how you position differentiation and moats). For investors, it’s a reminder that narratives can lead fundamentals and that the consequences of narrative shifts—capital access, acquisition currency, hiring capacity—are very real for software companies.
