# Writing about Agentic Engineering Patterns
**Source**: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Feb/23/agentic-engineering-patterns/
**Date**: 2026-02-23
**Author**: Simon Willison
**Keywords**: agentic engineering, patterns, documentation, coding workflows, TDD

## Elevator pitch
Simon Willison announces a new “Agentic Engineering Patterns” series, outlining a long-term documentation effort to capture repeatable patterns for building software with AI agents.

## Takeaways
- The series aims to define and document repeatable agentic engineering patterns.
- Willison positions agentic engineering as distinct from casual “vibe coding.”
- Early chapters include “Writing code is cheap now” and “Red/green TDD.”
- The format is a guide-like compendium rather than short blog posts.
- He plans a steady cadence and emphasizes human-authored writing.

## Synthesis
In this announcement, Simon Willison introduces “Agentic Engineering Patterns,” a new series intended to capture the emerging best practices of building software with AI agents. He frames the project as a structured guide rather than a set of short blog posts, signaling that the material is meant to be durable and referential. The underlying motivation is to make sense of a rapidly evolving landscape where AI systems increasingly write, test, and refactor code, but the engineering discipline around those workflows is still immature.

Willison’s framing is deliberate: he distinguishes “agentic engineering” from the more casual “vibe coding” that has become popular in the AI tooling community. The difference is in rigor. Agentic engineering is presented as a systematic approach that emphasizes repeatable patterns, explicit workflows, and disciplined feedback loops. Vibe coding, by contrast, can produce quick wins but may lack the guardrails required for production software. By collecting patterns, he wants to give engineers a stable vocabulary and set of techniques that move agent use from novelty to professional practice.

The early chapters provide a glimpse of the direction. “Writing code is cheap now” highlights how AI has shifted the economics of software creation, pushing teams to focus on specification, testing, and evaluation rather than raw output. “Red/green TDD” explores how traditional testing loops intersect with AI agents: rapid iteration can be amplified, but only if the red/green discipline is preserved. These topics signal that the series will be grounded in established engineering methods, adapted to a world where code can be generated at low cost and high speed.

Willison also emphasizes the format of the project. Instead of transient posts, he is building a guide-like resource that can grow over time, with chapters that read more like documentation than commentary. That choice suggests a focus on clarity and reuse. Patterns are meant to be applied by others, so the writing needs to be precise and stable. He also notes a commitment to human-authored writing, using AI tools for editing and polishing rather than drafting, which aligns with his broader philosophy on transparency and responsible use of AI.

The announcement implicitly argues that the biggest challenge in the AI era is not just model capability, but the surrounding engineering practice. When AI can generate large amounts of code, the limiting factor becomes how well you can define tasks, verify results, and integrate outputs into existing systems. Patterns provide a way to encode those lessons. They make it easier for teams to adopt agentic workflows without reinventing the same decision-making and review structures each time.

Overall, the series is positioned as a field guide for a new era of software engineering. By collecting patterns, Willison aims to stabilize the conversation around AI agents and give engineers a concrete toolkit for building reliable systems. The announcement is less about a single technique and more about the ambition to codify a shared playbook, so that teams can move faster while maintaining quality and accountability.
