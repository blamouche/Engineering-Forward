# How Does AI Impact Skill Formation?
**Source**: https://www.seangoedecke.com/how-does-ai-impact-skill-formation/
**Date**: 2026-02-03
**Author**: Sean Goedecke
**Keywords**: AI, skill formation, learning, coding agents, GPT-4o, Anthropic Fellows, software engineering, productivity

## Elevator pitch
A critical analysis of the Anthropic Fellows paper on AI and skill formation finds that the "AI doesn't speed you up" conclusion relies on 50% of participants manually retyping AI code, while the real insight — that AI reduces per-task learning — is obvious but may be offset by higher task volume.

## Takeaways
- When excluding participants who manually retyped AI code (50% of the cohort), AI users were 25% faster at the task
- AI users who used AI for questions but wrote their own code performed nearly as well on knowledge quizzes as non-AI users
- Software engineers are paid to ship, not to learn — avoiding AI to learn more actually makes you worse at your job
- Moving faster with AI means more opportunities to learn through breadth, potentially offsetting lower per-task depth of learning
- The study used GPT-4o despite being funded by Anthropic and conducted in 2025, raising questions about whether better models would change results

## Synthesis
Sean Goedecke provides a nuanced critique of the Anthropic Fellows paper "How AI Impacts Skill Formation," which has been widely interpreted as evidence that AI makes developers slower and dumber. His analysis challenges both the headline conclusions and the broader narrative around AI-assisted coding.

The paper's structure mirrors the 2025 MIT "Your Brain on ChatGPT" study: participants performed a cognitive task (learning the Python Trio library), split between AI-required and AI-forbidden groups, then took a quiz on retention. The banner result was that AI users completed the task no faster but scored significantly worse on the quiz.

Goedecke identifies a critical methodological issue that undermines the speed conclusion. Half of the AI-using cohort spent most of their time literally retyping AI-generated code instead of copy-pasting or writing code with AI guidance. This puzzling behavior — which didn't help their quiz scores — skewed the timing data. When these retypers are excluded, AI users were 25% faster.

On the learning deficit, Goedecke acknowledges it's real but argues it's obvious and less alarming than presented. Users who employed AI for general questions while writing their own code scored only about one point lower on quizzes — a minimal tradeoff for 25% faster completion. The concerning pattern is when AI does all the code writing, but Goedecke makes two counterpoints.

First, software engineers are paid to ship, not to learn. If AI accelerates delivery, avoiding it makes you worse at your job regardless of learning benefits. He compares this to medicine, where doctors supplement on-the-job learning with dedicated professional development. Future software engineers may similarly need to carve out explicit time for codebase study, separate from production work.

Second, faster completion means more tasks completed, potentially generating more total learning despite a lower per-task rate. An engineer shipping 2x as many changes acquires greater breadth of knowledge across subsystems, even if depth per task suffers. The net effect on total learning remains an open question.

Goedecke also questions the choice of GPT-4o for the study, noting it was already six months old at the time and that Anthropic (who funded the study) has significantly better models. While he sympathizes with the practical challenge of studying fast-evolving AI tools, the model choice may have disadvantaged the AI-using cohort.

The piece concludes by identifying the key unanswered questions: whether lower per-task learning is offset by higher task volume, and whether explicit study time can substitute for learning-by-doing. These answers will likely define the software engineering profession for the next decade.
