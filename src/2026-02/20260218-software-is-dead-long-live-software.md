# Software Is Dead — Long Live Software

**Source**: https://insights.euclid.vc/p/software-is-dead-long-live-software?utm_source=tldrnewsletter&hide_intro_popup=true

**Date**: Feb 18, 2026

**Author**: Euclid Ventures

**Keywords**: SaaS, vertical AI, software markets, defensibility, enterprise software

## Elevator pitch

Euclid Ventures argues that AI is reshaping software economics, triggering short-term value destruction but ultimately expanding markets—especially for vertical SaaS with strong defensibility.

## Takeaways

- Public software valuations fell sharply amid fears of AI-driven commoditization.
- Vertical SaaS is positioned as more defensible than horizontal software.
- AI-native entrants can unbundle weak incumbents, but durable moats still matter.
- Defensibility hinges on workflow lock-in, data gravity, and trust.
- The long-term outcome is market expansion, not contraction, as productivity gains are reinvested.

## Synthesis

Euclid Ventures frames the recent software market selloff as the first phase of an AI-driven reset. The article cites sharp valuation drops and investor panic following a wave of AI product launches, but argues that this “SaaSmageddon” is a short-term destruction phase rather than a terminal decline. The core thesis is that AI will commoditize some software functions while expanding the total market by lowering costs and enabling new categories.

The piece draws a line between horizontal software and vertical SaaS. Horizontal tools—generic CRMs, document tools, and productivity layers—are more exposed to AI substitution. Vertical software, by contrast, embeds into workflows, data, and regulatory environments that are harder to displace. The authors argue that these structural advantages create resilience even as AI changes the surface layer of interfaces and interactions.

A key part of the analysis is defensibility. Euclid emphasizes that speed is not a durable moat; over time, incumbents win through data loops, brand trust, and deep workflow integration. For AI-native startups, the path to durability requires building a “System of Intelligence” that becomes the authoring layer for high-value workflows and captures proprietary usage data. That creates compounding switching costs that generic AI tools can’t easily overcome.

The article also challenges the idea that AI will shrink software markets. The authors contend that productivity gains, when reinvested, expand demand and the scope of what software can do. AI lowers marginal costs, which should unlock new applications and broader adoption, even if some legacy categories are disrupted. This is similar to past technology waves where short-term displacement led to long-term market growth.

Overall, the message is that software is not dying; it is being reorganized. Incumbents that adapt and AI-native challengers that build defensible workflow positions can both thrive. The near-term volatility reflects uncertainty about which businesses can withstand commoditization, but the long-term opportunity—especially in vertical AI—remains large and growing.
