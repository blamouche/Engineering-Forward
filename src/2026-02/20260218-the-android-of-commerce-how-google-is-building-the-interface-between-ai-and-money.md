## Elevator pitch
Linas Beliūnas argues that Google is building an “Android of commerce” by open‑sourcing protocols that let AI agents browse, buy, and pay, positioning itself as the default interface between AI and money.

## Takeaways
- Google shipped three protocols that together form a stack for agentic commerce.
- Agent Payments Protocol (2025), UCP (Jan 2026), and WebMCP (Feb 2026) create the stack.
- WebMCP replaces brittle UI scraping with structured, callable tools.
- The strategic play mirrors Android: open infrastructure to capture distribution.
- Control of the AI‑money interface is emerging as a key competitive battleground.

## Synthesis
This Fintech Pulse issue from Linas Beliūnas frames Google’s recent protocol releases as a strategic play to control the interface between AI agents and money. The headline argument is that Google is not just improving AI agent efficiency; it is building the “Android of commerce”—an open infrastructure stack that can become the default platform for how agents browse, buy, and pay.

Beliūnas highlights three protocols shipped in rapid succession: the Agent Payments Protocol (September 2025), the Universal Commerce Protocol (January 2026), and WebMCP (February 10, 2026). Together, these form a three‑layer stack that allows AI agents to interact with commerce systems in a standardized way. The stack covers browsing and tool access (WebMCP), product and transaction semantics (UCP), and payments (Agent Payments Protocol). The combination, he argues, is an operating system for agentic commerce.

WebMCP is presented as a particularly important piece. AI agents today often navigate websites like a tourist in a foreign city—taking screenshots, parsing HTML, and guessing where to click. WebMCP introduces a browser‑native API that lets websites expose structured, callable tools directly to agents. This reduces computational overhead and increases task accuracy. Beliūnas cites improvements on the order of 67% lower overhead and around 98% task accuracy, arguing that this efficiency gain matters at scale.

However, he insists the more important story is strategic, not technical. By making the infrastructure open and widely adoptable, Google can replicate its Android playbook: build the platform, encourage ecosystem adoption, and capture value through distribution and default placement. In this framing, the protocols are less about helping agents and more about ensuring Google sits in the critical pathway of AI‑driven transactions.

The essay suggests that control of the AI‑money interface is emerging as a central power battle in fintech and commerce. If agents become the primary way people shop and pay, the platform that defines their interaction standards gains immense leverage. Open protocols can create legitimacy and adoption, while still allowing the platform owner to influence the ecosystem and monetize through distribution or embedded services.

Beliūnas’ call to action is implicit: fintechs, banks, and payments companies need to pay attention. The protocol stack could become the default rails for agentic commerce, and late movers risk ceding control to Google. In that sense, the piece is both an analysis of Google’s strategy and a warning to incumbents that the next interface layer is being defined now.

Overall, the issue positions Google’s protocol releases as a deliberate, coordinated move to shape the future of commerce. The technical improvements are real, but the deeper implication is that platform control—who owns the interface between AI agents and money—may determine who captures the next wave of value.
