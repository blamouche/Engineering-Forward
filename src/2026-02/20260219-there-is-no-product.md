# There Is No Product
**Source**: https://sidu.in/essays/after-ai-there-is-no-product.html
**Date**: 2026-02-19
**Author**: Sidu Ponnappa
**Keywords**: ai, saas, economics

## Elevator pitch
Sidu Ponnappa argues that AI is turning large swaths of software from scarce assets into cheap, replicable inventory, breaking the economic logic of traditional SaaS.

## Takeaways
- The core shift is not SaaS delivery but software’s asset economics collapsing.
- When software becomes cheap to build, it behaves like inventory with no moat.
- AI moves the “line” of what is hard to build upward each year, shrinking defensible product surfaces.
- Custom, single-tenant builds can undercut SaaS total cost of ownership at scale.
- Enduring value moves to software above the line: complex, domain-heavy, or regulated systems.

## Synthesis
Sidu Ponnappa reframes the “SaaS is dead” debate as a misunderstanding of the real economic shift. SaaS is a delivery model—cloud-hosted software sold on subscription—and that model is not going away. What is changing, he argues, is the economics of software as an asset. Traditional software product companies are built on the assumption that software is expensive and risky to build, and therefore scarce and valuable. You invest upfront, create an asset that competitors and customers cannot easily replicate, and then amortize that investment across many customers over many years. That is the engine of SaaS: build something difficult, then collect rent.

AI disrupts this logic by making more software cheap and fast to produce. When a product can be built quickly by agents at low cost, it becomes replicable inventory rather than a scarce asset. Inventory has no moat; it depreciates and can be manufactured on demand. Ponnappa’s claim is that below a moving capability line, software is no longer an asset you can amortize, but an inventory you must liquidate. Pricing debates and seat counts are downstream symptoms; the core issue is whether the software is still difficult to build.

He uses the notion of a “line” to describe capability thresholds. In 2024, AI could autocomplete and generate boilerplate but could not replace full products. In 2025, it built entire components—integrations, authentication, admin dashboards—moving the line upward and weakening moats for products built on standard workflows. In 2026, AI can generate full CRUD applications and internal tools, pushing many common business systems below the line. He points to real market signals—like large valuation drops following AI-driven product announcements—as evidence that once-stable categories can be pushed below the line quickly.

Ponnappa contrasts asset economics with inventory economics using the example of enterprise SaaS. A centralized SaaS product must serve diverse customers with conflicting workflows, creating high architectural complexity. A custom build for a single customer avoids multi-tenant complexity and can be simpler to build and maintain. As AI reduces build costs, custom development becomes more attractive. The financial logic flips: SaaS bills scale with headcount, but a maintenance contract for a custom system can be a fixed cost. At a certain scale, total cost of ownership favors custom software, especially when AI compresses development timelines.

He grounds the argument with a personal example from Gojek, where a vendor quoted hundreds of thousands annually for an HR module that the team built in-house for less. That was before AI; today, he argues, the same project would take weeks and cost far less. AI agents also reduce knowledge fragility by documenting as they build, decreasing the need to retain large, specialized teams to maintain context. What once looked like a vendor moat was simply the assumption that building custom software was harder than buying it. AI dissolves that assumption.

The conclusion is not that all software is worthless, but that the line between asset and inventory is moving fast. Software above the line—complex, regulated, or deeply domain-specific systems—still carries asset economics. Below the line, product strategy based on amortization fails because customers can build or commission comparable systems quickly. The practical implication is that software companies must identify whether their products sit above or below the line and adjust strategy accordingly, while expecting the line to keep moving upward as models improve.
