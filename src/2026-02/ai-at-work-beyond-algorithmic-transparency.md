## Elevator pitch
Algorithmic transparency (model cards, audits, prompt logging) is necessary but insufficient: the real accountability gap is shifting to *who* delegates judgment to AI, *how* that delegation is socially incentivized or penalized, and *what* gets lost when human work becomes legible as tokens, clicks, and scores.

## Takeaways
- Transparency should be decomposed: (1) tool transparency, (2) usage transparency, (3) transparency of human contribution—today we mostly govern (1), partially track (2), and ignore (3).
- “Shadow AI” is rational behavior: workers often hide AI usage because disclosure carries competence/motivation penalties.
- As AI mediates more work, organizations become data-richer while accountability becomes fuzzier (“who actually exercised judgment?”).
- Human–AI–AI loops (AI generates → AI evaluates) can accelerate throughput but hollow out learning and oversight if humans become mere relay/edit layers.
- A workplace ethics of *protected judgment* is needed: selective transparency + spaces for uncaptured thinking to preserve dignity and real responsibility.

## Synthesis
The essay argues that “more transparency” is often presented as the fix for AI risks at work, but that framing is increasingly misaligned with how AI is actually deployed. Policies and corporate governance tend to focus on making algorithms legible—documentation, audits, evaluation reports, logging—assuming that if we can inspect systems, we can hold organizations accountable.

The author proposes that transparency actually spans three layers. First is *tool transparency*: understanding how an algorithm works, what data it uses, and what risks it introduces. Second is *usage transparency*: knowing who uses which AI systems, for which tasks, under what approvals and guardrails. Third—and largely missing—is transparency of *human contribution*: what portion of a deliverable reflects a person’s judgment versus what has been delegated to AI.

The piece’s central move is to show that the accountability gap is migrating away from model internals to human and organizational decisions that are rarely captured in logs: why a worker delegated a task, what alternatives were considered, and whether the person meaningfully evaluated the output or merely accepted the most plausible answer on screen. This is made worse by “shadow AI”: a large share of employees use AI outside official channels, often intentionally hiding it. The author highlights evidence that disclosure of AI assistance can trigger negative merit judgments—people who admit using AI are evaluated more harshly than those who do not—creating incentives to conceal usage and thereby undermining any governance program built on self-reporting.

A second theme is that AI doesn’t only make systems transparent; it can make *workers* transparent to systems. As knowledge work is increasingly mediated by tools that read and write text, human judgment is translated into measurable behavioral traces (tokens, clicks, edits, time-to-respond) that can be summarized, scored, and optimized. This can erode professional “style” and epistemic authority—being able to say “this is what I think, and why”—because the path from judgment to output becomes a collaborative, partially opaque dialogue with a model.

Finally, the essay warns about human–AI–AI loops, where AI-generated work is evaluated by another AI system (e.g., automated reviews and meta-evaluators). These loops can create the appearance of oversight while reducing genuine understanding, with humans acting as adapters between machine outputs rather than as accountable decision-makers.

The implied prescription is not anti-transparency but a rebalancing: governance should protect spaces for judgment that are not fully instrumented, clarify delegation conventions, and design workflows that sustain cognition rather than offload it entirely—an “ethics of protected judgment” as a complement to algorithmic transparency.
