# CoderPad State of Tech Hiring 2026
**Source**: https://coderpad.io/survey-reports/coderpad-state-of-tech-hiring-2026/
**Date**: 2026-02-11
**Author**: Russell Heimlich
**Keywords**: tech hiring, AI tools, developer skills, recruitment, technical assessment, workforce trends, 2026

## Elevator pitch
A survey of 650+ global participants finds that AI has shifted from optional productivity tool to essential infrastructure for developers, while reshaping the skills companies value most and the methods used to evaluate candidates.

## Takeaways
- 82% of developers find generative AI useful (up from 76% in 2025); 54% would face significant productivity drops without it
- Writing new code is declining as a critical skill; debugging, fine-tuning, and system design are growing in importance
- Industry remains divided on AI in assessments: 34% ban it entirely, 46% allow it with varying constraints
- 53% of hiring leaders anticipate budget increases (up from 37% in 2025), with quality of hire as the top priority for 60%
- AI hasn't eliminated developer demand—it has intensified competition for talent proficient in AI-augmented workflows

## Synthesis
The CoderPad State of Tech Hiring 2026 report, based on responses from 650+ global participants, offers a data-driven snapshot of how AI is reshaping both developer work and the hiring processes used to evaluate developers.

The headline finding is unambiguous: AI has crossed from novelty to infrastructure. Eighty-two percent of developers now find generative AI useful, up from 76% in 2025. More telling is the dependency metric—54% say they would experience significant productivity drops if their AI tools were removed tomorrow. This figure implies that current developer output estimates already incorporate AI amplification, meaning productivity benchmarks from even two years ago significantly understate expectations.

The skills evolution is particularly consequential for hiring strategy. Writing new code—historically the dominant signal in technical assessments—is declining in importance as AI handles increasing proportions of initial implementation. Rising in its place: debugging (identifying why AI-generated code fails), fine-tuning (adapting outputs to organizational constraints), and system design (making architectural decisions that AI cannot make). Companies that continue screening primarily for raw coding ability may be selecting for the capability AI is most directly replacing.

Assessment methodology remains in flux. The industry is almost evenly split: 34% ban AI tools in evaluations while 46% allow them in some form. Neither approach has yet established itself as definitively superior, and the 60% of recruiters who still prefer technical discussions and live coding formats reflects continued uncertainty about how to interpret AI-assisted performance signals.

The hiring market itself is stabilizing. Fifty-three percent of hiring leaders expect budget increases, up sharply from 37% in 2025. Developer job-switching intent sits at a five-year low of 39%, suggesting that retention has become easier even as competition for AI-proficient talent intensifies. AI/ML specialists and full-stack engineers remain the hardest roles to fill, reinforcing that the constraint is not developer supply in aggregate but the specific combination of technical depth and AI fluency that the current moment demands.
