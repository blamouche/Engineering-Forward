# Performance Calibrations at Tech Companies: Part 2
**Source**: https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/performance-calibrations-part-2
**Date**: 2022-12-13
**Author**: Gergely Orosz
**Keywords**: performance management, calibrations, cognitive biases, manager strategies, individual contributor, promotions, tech companies

## Elevator pitch
The second part of Orosz's calibration series dives into in-meeting dynamics, cognitive biases that distort outcomes, and concrete strategies for both managers and individual contributors navigating these high-stakes processes.

## Takeaways
- Calibration meetings span weeks and involve significant political maneuvering, coalition-building, and negotiation between managers
- Common cognitive biases include recency bias, halo/horn effects, and in-group favoritism—all of which inflate or deflate ratings unfairly
- Managers should prepare thoroughly before meetings and be deliberate about how much information they share with their reports
- Individual contributors can influence their own outcomes by ensuring their work is visible and well-documented before the cycle
- The process at large tech companies (based on Uber experience + dozens of manager/VP interviews) is far more human and political than its formal structure suggests

## Synthesis
Where Part 1 of Gergely Orosz's calibration series covered the structural mechanics, Part 2 examines what actually happens when managers sit in a room together. The gap between organizational chart and reality turns out to be substantial.

The in-meeting dynamics Orosz describes resemble negotiation more than evaluation. Managers arrive with pre-prepared cases for their direct reports, form coalitions with allies in the room, and use political capital to push back when initial ratings seem unfair. A manager who has built goodwill with their director has more leverage to advocate for a team member than one who is isolated. This dynamic rewards managers who invest in relationships across the organization—not merely in their direct reports' development.

Cognitive biases systematically distort outcomes in predictable ways. Recency bias causes evaluators to weight the last two months of a performance cycle far more heavily than the preceding ten, disadvantaging engineers who do their best work on long-horizon projects. Halo and horn effects cause a single strong or weak impression to color assessments of unrelated capabilities. In-group favoritism advantages employees whose communication style, background, or working patterns match those of the calibration participants. Orosz notes these biases don't disappear with awareness—they require active structural countermeasures like forcing evaluators to cite specific evidence before assigning ratings.

The practical implications differ significantly by role. Managers should enter calibrations with prepared written documentation for each report, anticipate likely challenges to their assessments, and determine in advance which battles are worth fighting. The question of how much to disclose to reports—whether to tell them their provisional rating, what was said about them, or how the committee's views evolved—requires careful judgment balancing transparency against the instability that incomplete information can create.

Individual contributors have more leverage than they typically realize. The most effective approach is ensuring their work is visible and documented before the calibration cycle closes, not during it. Managers who must reconstruct a year of contributions from memory in a meeting will inevitably undersell compared to colleagues whose records are complete. The meta-lesson: performance at large tech companies is not purely a technical or delivery problem—it is also a communication and documentation problem.
