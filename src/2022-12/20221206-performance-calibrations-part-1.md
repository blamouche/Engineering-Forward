# Performance Calibrations at Tech Companies: Part 1
**Source**: https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/performance-calibrations
**Date**: 2022-12-06
**Author**: Gergely Orosz
**Keywords**: performance management, calibrations, tech companies, compensation, promotions, stack ranking

## Elevator pitch
Gergely Orosz explains why structured performance calibration meetings exist in large tech companies, how they work mechanically, and why they're essential for fair compensation and talent retention despite their imperfections.

## Takeaways
- Without formal performance systems, compensation opacity and unfair reward distribution drive talented employees out while underperformers stay
- Performance calibrations help companies identify top performers, those needing improvement, and promotion-ready employees
- The process creates defensible, systematic compensation decisions across teams and functions
- Budget allocation, bucketing approaches, and stack ranking are the foundational mechanics covered in Part 1
- Part 2 covers in-meeting dynamics, cognitive biases, and strategies for both managers and individual contributors

## Synthesis
Performance calibration meetings are a fixture of large tech organizations that many engineers encounter but few fully understand. Gergely Orosz begins this two-part series by establishing why they exist in the first place through a thought experiment that makes the alternative vivid.

Imagine a company where managers independently decide compensation with no cross-team alignment. The immediate consequences are predictable: one employee discovers a peer doing equivalent work earns 20% more, another learns raises are determined by how much their manager advocates for them rather than what they accomplished, and a third watches an underperformer retain their position because their manager avoids difficult conversations. Talented people, who have the most options, leave. Underperformers, who have fewer options, stay. The organization gradually selects against the people it most needs.

Calibration meetings solve this through structured cross-functional alignment. Managers gather to assign performance ratings collectively, with those ratings then driving bonuses, salary increases, and promotion decisions. The process ensures that a "meets expectations" rating means the same thing in engineering as it does in product, and that the distribution of outcomes across teams reflects company priorities rather than individual manager advocacy skills.

The mechanics covered in Part 1 include budget allocation (how much money is available for compensation changes), bucketing approaches (whether the company uses discrete labels like "exceeds/meets/below" or continuous ratings), and stack ranking (ordering employees within peer groups). These structural choices shape every downstream outcome. A company using forced distribution—where a fixed percentage must fall into each bucket—produces very different dynamics than one using manager judgment with calibration as a check.

Orosz approaches the topic empirically, drawing on his own experience at Uber and interviews with dozens of managers and executives. The framing throughout is pragmatic: calibrations are imperfect instruments that introduce their own biases and political dynamics, but they are far less bad than the alternative of uncoordinated, opaque individual decisions. Part 2 promises to surface where the system breaks down in practice and how to navigate it from both sides of the table.
